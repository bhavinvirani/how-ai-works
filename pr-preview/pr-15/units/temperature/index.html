The dial that cannot make it cleverer

A colleague sends you a screenshot. They asked the assistant for the three biggest risks in a supplier contract, and it came back with a clean, sensible list. You paste the same question into the same assistant four minutes later. Same words. Same document.

You get a different list. Two of the three match. The third is something else entirely, and it is worse.

Nothing changed in between. Not the question, not the document, not the model. Nobody deployed anything.

The natural conclusion is that it thought about it again and came out somewhere else. It did not. Run that question a thousand times and the machine works out the same opinion a thousand times — it is the same arithmetic over the same numbers, and arithmetic does not have moods.

What differs is one step that happens after the opinion, and there is a single dial controlling it.

Something still has to choose

The unit before this one ended on a row rather than a word. At every step the model does not produce the next piece of text; it produces every piece that could come next, each with a share of the probability, all of them adding up to one.

A row of percentages is not text. Something downstream has to turn it into one piece, and there are only two honest ways to do that.

Always take the largest. Straightforward, and completely repeatable: the same question produces the same answer today, tomorrow and next year. It also has a specific failure that anyone who has tried it will recognise. Text written this way has a habit of getting stuck — repeating a clause, then repeating it again — because the piece that most often follows a phrase is sometimes the piece that starts the phrase over, and a machine that always takes the largest has no way to break out. The deeper problem is that such a model has exactly one answer to any question, for ever. Ask it for a subject line ten times and you get the same subject line ten times.

Draw one at random, in proportion to the shares. A piece holding 55% of the row gets picked 55% of the time, one holding 5% gets picked 5% of the time. Variety comes free, several attempts at the same question are genuinely different attempts — and every so often something from the bottom of the row gets through.

Neither is right for everything, which is why the choice was never made for you. It was left on a dial.

The dial is not where you think it is

Here is the part almost every explanation gets wrong, so it is worth being slow about.

What the model hands over is not percentages. It is a raw score for each piece — an unbounded number, higher meaning “more like what usually comes next here”. Those scores are turned into percentages by one standard step called softmax, which takes any row of numbers and returns a row of shares: all positive, the largest still the largest, adding to exactly one.

There is one number in the request that acts on all this, and it does exactly one thing. Every raw score is divided by it, before softmax runs.

That is the whole mechanism. The number is called the temperature, borrowed from physics, where a hotter substance jostles about more — an analogy worth exactly that much and no more.

Divide by something small and the scores are pulled further apart, so the leader runs away with the row. Divide by something large and they are squeezed closer together, so the row flattens out towards giving every piece an equal chance.

Now notice what dividing cannot do. Divide every number in a list by the same amount and the biggest is still the biggest, the smallest is still the smallest, nothing has been added and nothing has been removed. The order is identical at every setting of the dial. A piece the model rated third best is third best at 0.1 and third best at 2.

So “turn the temperature up to make it more creative” is describing something other than what it sounds like. Nothing in the machine has become more imaginative, more knowledgeable or better. The model’s opinion was finished before the dial was consulted. All that changes is how much of the row is left over for the pieces it did not rate first.

Put numbers on it. In the panel further down, the model scores mild at 3.1 and cold at 2.4 — a gap of 0.7 that it produced once and never revisits. At a setting of 1, mild is twice as likely as cold. At 2, 1.4 times as likely. At the very bottom of the dial, about eleven hundred times as likely. The same two scores in all three cases.

“mild” is holding 55% of the row, and “aubergine”, the piece the model rates last, 0.4%. Roughly where a general assistant is set by default. The top two or three carry the row, and the same question can honestly come back two different ways. One run so far. Run it again and see whether the same sentence comes back.

It opens midway, with the model continuing The weather today is. Five pieces: mild at 55%, cold at 27%, unsettled at 12%, glorious at 5%, and aubergine, printed as 0.

Take the slider to the bottom first. Four bars vanish and mild goes to 100%. Now run it four times. The same sentence four times — The weather today is mild and dry. Nothing has been switched off; the machine is still drawing at random, exactly as before. There is simply nothing left to draw.

This end of the dial stops at 0.1 rather than 0, because dividing by zero is not a thing a dial can do. Interfaces that offer you a 0 are not dividing by it either — they quietly switch to always taking the largest, which is what this end is doing anyway. Either way it is where anything that has to be reproducible gets run.

Leave it on four runs and slide back to the middle. Four different sentences. These four draws happen to walk straight down the row — the first takes mild, the second cold, the third unsettled, the fourth glorious — which is what a row of percentages looks like when it is genuinely being sampled. One opinion, four outcomes, and not a single number in that row changed between them.

Now push the dial up to 1.1 or beyond. The fourth run opens with aubergine. At 1.2 the model rated that piece at 0.8%, and 0.8% of the time is 0.8% of the time. Watch what it does next: it does not stop, apologise or start the sentence again. It writes aubergine coloured and…, because continuing is the only operation it has.

Two honest caveats about that. The four runs are four fixed rolls of the dice, so that this page can point at them and so that you see what the sentences above describe; four is not a survey, and at the top of the dial the model is giving that piece about three chances in a hundred. And the row prints whole percentages, so at the middle setting aubergine shows as 0 — it is 0.4%, which is not never. It is about once in every two hundred and seventy presses.

What the two ends are actually for

Turn it down for anything with one right answer and a fixed shape. Pulling fields out of an invoice, classifying a ticket, converting a date, writing code where one wrong token stops the file compiling. Two reasons, and the second one matters more than it looks. Where there is a single correct answer, the second-best piece is not a pleasant variation, it is simply wrong. And a system that returns the same output for the same input is a system you can test; one that does not is a system where every failed test might be bad luck.

Turn it up for anything you are going to sift through yourself. Names, subject lines, opening paragraphs, ten different angles on a brief. Here a dud costs you nothing but the second it takes to skip it, and the whole value is in the spread. If you want six genuinely different attempts, this dial is how you get them; asking the same question six times at the bottom of the range will hand you the same answer six times.

The dial has no idea which of these you are doing. It is set per request, and most chat interfaces set it once, on your behalf, for everything you will ever ask.

Turning it down does not make it true

At the bottom of the range the model always takes its top-scored piece. If the top-scored piece is wrong, you now get the same wrong answer every single time, delivered with total steadiness. Consistency is not accuracy. Nothing anywhere in this dial has access to whether the favourite is correct; it only knows which score was largest, and it was going to know that at every setting.

The other direction fails for the same reason. Turning the dial up cannot make the model reach a fact that was not in the row, because temperature never adds anything to the row. It only makes it likelier to pick something it already rated badly.

There is one genuinely useful thing to do with all this. Because the variation comes from the draw and not from the model reconsidering, running the same question three times tells you something real about the row underneath. Where the three answers agree, the row was sharply peaked and the model was confident. Where they wander off in different directions, the row was flat — no strong opinion, and what you are reading is a guess wearing the same fluent prose as everything else. It is not proof: three agreeing answers can be three samples of one confident mistake. But as a thirty-second check on whether you are looking at knowledge or at a guess, nothing else is that cheap.

The other control, which does something different

Alongside temperature, most APIs and playgrounds offer a second control, usually called top-p (or nucleus sampling) and sometimes top-k. It is easy to assume it is a variant of this one. It is not, and the difference is the distinction this whole unit turns on.

Top-p sorts the row, keeps adding pieces from the top until their shares reach some total — 90%, say — and throws everything below that away. Then it draws from what is left. That is not a change to the gaps. It is a change to the list.

Which makes the two complementary rather than redundant. A high temperature with a tight top-p gives you variety among the plausible options while making the piece rated at four in a thousand strictly unreachable rather than merely unlikely. If you want adventurous prose without the occasional aubergine, that combination is how it is done.

For developers
var reply = model.complete(prompt, Options.temperature(0.0));

Temperature belongs to the request, not to the model, so two callers of the same deployment can be running at different settings and neither can see the other’s. It is worth logging next to the output; a bug report that says “it gave a different answer” is unanswerable without it.

And zero is not the guarantee it looks like — this is the one place the page above simplifies. Hosted models batch requests together and floating-point addition is not associative, so the raw scores can come out fractionally different depending on who else happened to be in the batch, which is occasionally enough to swap the top two. If you need the exact bytes again, store them rather than planning to regenerate them.

Checkpoint

An extraction tool occasionally pulls the wrong field out of an invoice. Someone proposes setting temperature to 0 so that it stops making mistakes. What will that actually change, what will it definitely not change, and what would you have to look at to find out whether it helped?

Show answerHide answer

It changes the variability and nothing else. The same invoice will now always produce the same extraction, which removes one whole source of error — the runs where the tool drew the second-best field instead of the best one.

What it cannot change is which field the model scored highest, because that was decided before the dial was consulted and is the same at every setting. Every mistake where the wrong field was the favourite survives untouched, and now arrives identically every time, which makes it look considered rather than accidental.

So the only way to know is to compare against invoices whose correct answers you already have, before and after. If the errors were coming from the draw, they disappear. If they were coming from the row, the score does not move at all.

Worth keeping as a shape. Consistency and correctness are separate axes, and a change that improves one says nothing about the other — with the extra sting that making a system consistent can make its remaining errors harder to notice, because they stop looking like noise and start looking like a decision.

Where it fits
