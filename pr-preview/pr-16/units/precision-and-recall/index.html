Two ways to be wrong

A clinic screens a thousand people a month for a cancer that about ten of them have. Two detectors are sitting on your desk and you have to pick one. The first is right 99 times in a hundred. The second is right about 91. You have a spreadsheet and an afternoon, and the decision takes ten seconds.

You have just chosen the wrong one.

The 99 per cent detector has a single instruction inside it. It says “clear” to everybody who walks through the door. It never raises an alarm, it never frightens anybody, and it is wrong only about the ten people a month the whole programme exists to find. The 91 per cent detector finds nine of them.

Where the 99 per cent came from

Nothing about that score is a trick. Count the answers and the arithmetic holds: 990 of the thousand people really were healthy, the machine really did say so, and 990 out of 1,000 really is 99 per cent.

The trouble is the question the score answers. Accuracy — how often the machine was right, across everybody — gives every person in the room one vote. And 99 per cent of that room is healthy people, so 99 per cent of the score is a question nobody needed asking. The ten people the clinic is actually looking for contribute one per cent between them.

That is what makes rare answers so dangerous to measure. Whenever the thing you are hunting is unusual — fraud in card payments, a crack in a bridge, a tumour in a scan — not looking at all is a strategy with an excellent score.

Two ways to be wrong

So stop asking how often the machine was right, and start asking what it got wrong. There are only two ways, and they are not the same thing at all.

It can raise the alarm when there was nothing there. Somebody healthy is called back, spends a fortnight frightened, has another test, and turns out to be fine. Call that a false alarm.

Or it can stay quiet when there was something there. Somebody ill is sent home reassured, and nobody looks again until it is much later. Call that a miss.

Adding both into one accuracy number quietly claims they cost the same. They almost never do. For this screening programme a miss is a funeral and a false alarm is a bad fortnight, so you would happily take a great many false alarms to avoid one miss. Now run the identical argument for a junk mail filter and it flips: a junk message in your inbox is an irritation, and a lost invoice is a real problem. Same two mistakes, opposite order of importance, and no way to tell them apart from a single score.

The two questions worth asking

Once the mistakes are separated, two questions do nearly all the work, and neither of them needs any jargon.

When it raises the alarm, how often is something really there? If the answer is one time in twenty, people stop coming back for the follow-up, and the alarm has made itself worthless.

Of all the people who really were ill, how many did it find? If the answer is two out of ten, it does not matter how calm the clinic is.

Both have formal names, which turn up on every model report card you will ever read: the first is precision, the second is recall. Learn the names so you can read the card, and ask the plain questions when you are in the room.

Turn the dial yourself

Here is the part that surprises people. You rarely have to choose between those two detectors, because they are usually the same machine.

A detector like this does not answer yes or no. It scores each scan for how suspicious it looks, and somebody — separately, by hand — decides how high that score must reach before anyone is called back. That bar is a dial, and turning it is what turns one machine into the first detector or the second.

Accuracy: 99.0 per cent. It has found none of the 10 ill people. It never raised the alarm, so there was nothing to be right about. This is the detector that says "clear" to everybody who walks in. It scores better than most settings on this dial, and it has never helped anyone.

For comparison: saying "clear" to everybody scores 99.0 per cent on this month. The highest accuracy anywhere on this dial is 99.1 per cent, and that setting finds 2 of the 10. Every setting was tried.

Three things are worth noticing, and the second is the one that matters.

The setting that scores highest of all — 99.1 per cent, better than the machine that does nothing — finds two of the ten ill people. It is the top-scoring detector on the whole dial and nobody would let it near a clinic.

Push further and the accuracy falls while the machine becomes genuinely more useful. Nothing is faulty. Every extra person called back is a real false alarm, and accuracy counts each one against the machine exactly as heavily as it counts each illness found in its favour.

And at the setting that finds all ten, accuracy has dropped to 74 per cent. On a spreadsheet it is the worst detector on the page.

Training will build the useless machine for you

This is not only a reporting problem. Point a training run at examples where one answer is rare, tell it to make its wrongness score as small as possible, and “always say no” is a genuinely good answer to the question you asked. The search finds it, because it is downhill, and settles there. Nothing has malfunctioned. Teams work around it by scoring the rare answer more heavily, or by holding back most of the common examples — both of which are a person deciding, by hand, what a miss is worth.

Where the dial sits is not a technical decision

No formula can place it for you. Moving the dial trades misses for false alarms at a rate the machine works out exactly, but the exchange rate — how many frightening fortnights one prevented funeral is worth — is nowhere in the data. Somebody has to decide, and should be answerable for it.

Which gives you the question to carry into every conversation about how well an AI system performs. Not “how good is the score?” but “at what, what does it get wrong, and how rare was the thing it was hunting?” That one keeps working right through to the end of this guide.

For developers
double accuracy  = (caught + cleared) / (double) screened;
double precision = caught / (double) (caught + falseAlarms);
double recall    = caught / (double) (caught + missed);

The first line reaches the dashboard; the other two get asked about after the incident review. Note that the second returns a quiet NaN when the model has never flagged anything — the useless detector announcing itself, and the only warning you get.

Checkpoint

A fraud model is reported as 99.8% accurate on last year's payments, where roughly one payment in five hundred was fraudulent. What have you actually been told, and what would you ask next?

Almost nothing. Waving every payment through without looking scores 99.8 per cent on that data, so the number you were given is entirely consistent with a model that has never flagged anything in its life.

Two questions get you somewhere. Of the payments that really were fraudulent, how many did it catch? And when it does flag one, how often is it right? Then a third, which is not about the model at all: who decided where the bar sits, and what were they told a missed fraud costs against a blocked customer?
