The only question it ever gets asked

A sentence with the last word taken off it:

The kettle had boiled but nobody had made the —

You finished it before you decided to.

Now notice the part you did not notice. You did not only produce tea. Offer you alternatives and you would rank them without pausing. Coffee, easily. Bed — wrong here, but people do make beds, so not nothing. Effort, oddly, also fine. Although, never; not wrong so much as impossible.

You held a view on every one of those, and you held it before anybody asked for it.

That ranking is the machine this Part is about. Not the tea. The ranking.

One difference, and it is the whole difference. You stopped at four alternatives because four were offered. The machine does not stop. Every time it produces anything at all, it puts a number on every entry in its vocabulary — the hundred thousand or so chunks you watched text being cut into — including the overwhelming majority that are obvious nonsense. It has no way of skipping them, because it has no way of noticing that they are nonsense.

That is a large language model. The rest of this unit argues that the paragraph you have just read is the whole definition, and that there is nothing else in the box.

Three words, read backwards

The acronym turns up several times a day and is almost never taken apart. It is worth doing once, because the three words are not equally honest.

Model is the oldest of them and the only one that describes the mechanism. You met it in the first Part: a pile of adjustable numbers in a fixed arrangement, set by being shown examples and corrected, over and over. It is a model in the sense of the house-price machine you turned two dials on. It is not a model in the sense of a scale model of a building — nothing inside it is a small copy of the world.

Language describes the material, not a talent. Nobody installed grammar and nobody wrote down what a pronoun is. The machine was fed text, so text is what it became good at scoring, and everything it appears to know about the world arrived as a side effect of that one pursuit.

Large describes a count, and nobody has ever fixed the threshold at which it starts applying. What the count actually is comes two sections down, and it is worth reading slowly, because it is the only part of this unit that is difficult to believe.

One number for every entry, and then nothing

Here is the operation, stated once, with nothing held back.

Give the model some text. It produces one number for every entry in its vocabulary, saying how well that entry would follow.

That is the end of the sentence. There is no second half.

Three things about it are easy to misread, and all three matter later.

Entry, not word. The list is the chunk list from the last Part — some whole words, mostly fragments — so a long or unusual word is not one entry but the first piece of several. Nothing about the operation changes. The column is simply a column of chunks.

Scores, not shares. The numbers come out on no particular scale; a raw score can be any size, positive or negative, and on its own it says nothing. The row is then put through a step you have already met. Softmax turns any row of numbers into a row of shares — all positive, the largest still the largest, adding to exactly one.

Which makes this the same shape of arithmetic as attention, one level up. There, a single word had one unit of attention to spread across a sentence. Here, the whole machine has one unit of confidence to spread across its entire vocabulary. Raising tea lowers everything else, as a matter of arithmetic, exactly as before.

A table, not a choice. And then it stops. The model does not pick anything. It has produced a column of a hundred thousand numbers, and that column is what leaves it. Some very ordinary code outside the model reads the column and takes one entry — that is the next unit, not this one. Hold the seam between them, because everything famous about these machines happens on the scoring side of it, and everything that feels like a decision happens on the other.

Writing a limerick, translating a lease, finding the bug in a stack trace, summarising forty pages, declining to help with something: every one of those is that operation, run once, and then run again on the same text with its own output added to the end.

There is nowhere in the machine for translation to live. Not “it is hard to find” — there is no place of that kind. You have already been shown the complete inventory: a lookup table that turns chunks into numbers, then the same block stacked a fixed number of times, then the scoring. Nobody is holding anything back from you.

The size, written out

Now the other half, and the numbers are best read as digits rather than as words.

GPT-3 has 175,000,000,000 adjustable numbers. It was tuned on roughly 300,000,000,000 pieces of text. Producing one piece — one chunk, not one sentence — costs somewhere around two arithmetic steps per adjustable number, so about 350,000,000,000 multiplications and additions, every time, for every piece of every answer.

And that is the small end now. Recent models are tuned on more than ten trillion pieces. Reading at a steady two hundred and fifty words a minute, day and night, never sleeping and never stopping, a person would need something like eighty thousand years to get through that much text.

So both of these are true at once, and the drawing below is there to hold them together. The operation is one sentence long. The machine performing it is the largest artefact anyone has ever built.

Why the deflation is worth carrying

None of this is pedantry about a definition. It is the difference between expecting the right things from the machine and expecting the wrong ones, and almost every widely-held wrong expectation about these systems is the same mistake made three times over: assuming a part exists that does not.

People expect a part that checks. There is no step anywhere in that column where a candidate is held up against anything except how well it follows the text. “True” is not one of the inputs to the score.

People expect effort to track difficulty. It cannot. You met that claim on a small machine reading handwriting, and again on a stack of ninety-six blocks, and it survives being made this large without a scratch. The baffling question and the trivial one run the identical arithmetic and cost the identical money.

People expect it to know what happened last week. Its adjustable numbers stopped moving when training ended, and nothing in the operation reaches outside itself. Anything it says about last week either arrived in the text it was tuned on or was put in front of it by something else.

So when one of these machines does something that seems inexplicable, there is a question that explains most of it, and it is never “why did it think that”. It is: given everything written before this point, and everything this machine was tuned on, which entry scored highest?

Two of the three words have already slipped

Nobody defines “large”. A model with three billion adjustable numbers and one with five hundred billion both get called large language models, and the word tracks the mood of the field about as closely as it tracks the count.

“Language” is in worse shape. The same stack, essentially unchanged, now reads images and audio, and most of the products people call LLMs stopped being text-only some time ago. The name is a fossil from the years when it was accurate.

Model is the one word in the acronym still doing honest work.

The part nobody ordered

Deflation can be its own kind of dishonesty, so here is what the deflation does not explain.

The reasonable expectation, held by most of the people who worked on this, was that scoring the next piece extremely well would produce an extremely good autocomplete. Useful. Not interesting.

What happened instead, past a certain size, is that machines tuned on nothing but that began translating between language pairs nobody aimed them at, writing code that runs, working through arithmetic they had not been shown, and holding the thread of an argument across pages. Nothing in the training asked for any of it.

Why remains genuinely open. The best account available is a sketch rather than an explanation: an enormous quantity of human writing will not fit inside a fixed set of dials, so it cannot be stored, and the only way left to score the next piece well is to build machinery that generalises — machinery that represents how things relate, not merely which words tend to sit together. Something like that is probably happening. Which parts of it, and why the behaviour appears where it does, is an active argument and not a settled one.

Notice what that does and does not disturb. It does not add a second operation. The machine still produces one number per entry and nothing else. The surprise is not about what the machine does. It is about what doing that one thing well enough turned out to require.

For developers
// Ask for the scores, not just the text.
var res = client.chat(req.logprobs(true).topLogprobs(5));

for (var alt : res.logprobs().content().get(0).topLogprobs()) {
  System.out.printf("%-12s %.5f%n", alt.token(), Math.exp(alt.logprob()));
}

The Math.exp is not decoration. What comes back is the logarithm of each share, so every value is negative and the scale is nothing like the one you expect: -0.02 is a shade under 98%, and -9.1 is about one in ten thousand. Ordering survives untouched, which is exactly what makes this an expensive bug — sort by logprob() and you get the right ranking, then threshold on it as though it were a percentage and you have built a confidence check that fires on almost everything.

Checkpoint

A support assistant answers three sentences of company policy correctly and then, in exactly the same tone, invents a refund window that does not exist. The team lead proposes making the model check its answer before it commits to it. Using only what a large language model does, say what is wrong with that sentence — and where a check could genuinely go.

Show answerHide answer

There is no moment inside the machine when an answer exists to be checked. It produces one piece at a time, so at the instant it is scoring whatever follows returns are accepted within, there is no claim about refunds anywhere in the machine to hold up against anything — there is a column of chunks with numbers beside them, and that is the entire contents of the box. “Check” is not an operation the model has, and there is no step to insert it into.

The deeper problem is what the score is a score of. Every number in that column says how well a chunk would follow the text, given what this machine was tuned on. Whether the resulting sentence is true is not an input to that arithmetic and has nowhere to be recorded. Which is why the three correct sentences and the invented one came out of identical arithmetic at identical cost and arrived sounding identical — from inside, nothing distinguished them.

A check has to sit outside the model, on finished text. Look the policy up in the actual system of record and compare. Or hand the finished answer back to the same machine as fresh input, which now genuinely can be scored against, because this time the claim exists as text. That second one helps and is not a guarantee: you have asked a next-piece scorer to score pieces about its own output, and it is still not consulting anything true.

Where it fits

  • Every word is final the moment it is written — Scoring the vocabulary produces a table and then stops, so something outside the model has to take one entry, glue it on and run the whole machine again — which is where sentences actually come from.
  • Nothing in there ever checked — Nothing in a next-piece score has anywhere to record whether the piece is true, so a well-scored invention and a well-scored fact come out of identical arithmetic and arrive looking the same.
