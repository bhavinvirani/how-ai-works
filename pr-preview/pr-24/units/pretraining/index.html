It read everything once, and then stopped

The road atlas in the door pocket is a good one — every A-road, every village, the one-way system in towns you have never driven through. It was printed in 2019.

Last spring the council opened a bypass round the market town on your route. You have driven it perhaps thirty times. Page 74 still sends you straight through the high street, in the same clear type as everything else on the page, and everything else on the page is right.

The atlas is not broken and it is not lying. It is exactly as correct as it was the morning it came off the press, about a world that has since moved on. Nor will driving the bypass thirty more times put the bypass in it, because the atlas has no way of noticing anything. It was made in one enormous effort, once, and after that it was made of paper.

That is the shape of every large language model you have ever used, and this unit is about the process that gives it that shape.

The thumb trick, at industrial size

A long way back, this site asked you to put your thumb over the last word of an ordinary written sentence. Under the thumb was an exam question and its marked answer at the same time — nobody had to write the answer, because the answer was already there. Move the thumb along and you have another one. Every sentence anybody has ever typed is a stack of free questions.

That unit ended on a promise it was in no position to keep: this is the thing that took the ceiling off. Pretraining is that promise cashed. It is the same fill-in-the-blank game, run once, over as much text as can be gathered, on as much machinery as can be afforded, for as long as the money holds out.

The numbers are the part that does not survive being paraphrased, so here they are. One recent openly-published model was trained on fifteen trillion tokens — somewhere around eleven trillion words. A quick reader working eight hours a day gets through about a hundred and twenty thousand words. Eleven trillion is a quarter of a million years of that, with no weekends. It is also about two thousand times the whole of English Wikipedia.

The machinery is the other half of it. Tens of thousands of specialised processors, wired together so tightly that the wiring is its own research problem, running flat out for weeks or months. Nobody publishes the invoice, but the credible estimates for a single frontier run passed a hundred million dollars several years ago and have not come down since.

It happens once

This is the fact that reorganises everything else, so it is worth saying flatly.

Pretraining is not a routine. It is not a nightly job, not a subscription, and not something ticking over in the background while you use the thing. It is one run, with a start and a finish, and when it finishes it is finished. If it goes wrong halfway through, engineers rewind it to a saved point and start it again from there; nobody patches a model the way you patch software.

Everything after it is small change. The stages that turn what comes out of this run into something that will answer a question cost a low single-digit percentage of it, and often far less than that. That ratio is the entire economic shape of the industry: a handful of organisations can afford the expensive thing, and everybody else — thousands of companies, very nearly every AI product you have used — builds on top of what those runs produced.

Most of what was collected is thrown away

The usual sentence is that these models were trained on the internet. That is true in roughly the way a loaf is made of a wheat field.

Most of what a crawler drags back is not text anyone would want a machine learning from. Navigation menus. Cookie banners. Adverts. Near-empty pages. Spam. The same template repeated on ten thousand pages of the same site. And an enormous and growing volume of machine-written filler that exists only to be found by search engines. Feed that in and you get a model good at producing it.

So the pile is filtered, and the filtering is not a tidy-up — it is most of the work. In the pipelines whose details have been published, something like nine parts in ten of the text collected from the open web is discarded before training starts. Three kinds of cut do the bulk of it.

The junk goes first. Boilerplate is stripped out of pages, and whole categories are dropped: pages in languages nobody chose to include, pages that are almost entirely links, pages that trip the filters for spam or abuse.

Then the repeats. This one is less obvious and matters more. A passage that appears ten thousand times in the pile gets ten thousand nudges towards being reproduced exactly, and a model given that treatment learns to recite the passage rather than to learn the pattern inside it. Removing near-duplicates is not housekeeping; it is the difference between a model that understands a form of words and one that has memorised a particular set of them.

Then what is left is graded. A much smaller, cheaper model is often trained to score a page on whether it reads like something a person meant to write, and the low scores are dropped. This is the step that is most obviously a judgement, and nobody at any lab pretends otherwise.

The recipe is the secret

What survives is not fed to the model in the proportions in which it arrived, either. A pile that is mostly scraped web pages can be presented as though books, reference works and code were a far larger share of the world than they are, by the simple method of showing them to the model more than once.

The consequences of those choices are not subtle. Two models with the same architecture, the same size and the same training code come out noticeably different if their piles differ. Weighting code heavily makes a model better at things that are not code at all, which was one of the field’s genuine surprises. And a language that was thin in the pile produces a model that is thin in that language, permanently — which is the same shape of problem as the one that decided how text gets chopped into tokens, arriving one stage later and with larger stakes.

Which is why the recipe is the most closely guarded thing at any lab. Published descriptions of a new model will tell you its architecture in some detail, its size in fewer words, and what went into it in a sentence or in none at all. That is not an oversight. The mixture is understood to matter as much as anything else in the design, and it is the one part of a model that a competitor could copy in an afternoon.

The day it stops

When the run ends, what the model knows is finished.

The date on which the text stops is called the training cutoff, and it is the single most useful fact about a model that most people never look up. Nothing after it is in there. Not an election, not a product launch, not the death of somebody famous, not the version of the library you are working with today.

Here is the part that catches almost everyone. You can tell it. You can explain the bypass, patiently and clearly, and it will take the correction, use it well, reason with it competently, and thank you for it. And when the conversation ends, that is gone. Not stored badly — not stored. The next conversation begins from exactly the same unaltered model as the last one, because you were never handed a copy that could be written on.

Every conversation is a fresh, unmarked reprint of the 2019 atlas.

Two honest refinements, because the flat version above is slightly too clean.

The first is that the products which appear to remember you are keeping a file of notes on their own side and pasting the relevant parts into the top of each new conversation. That is genuinely useful and it is not the model changing. The model is being handed a briefing note, which it will read attentively and then forget again, exactly as before.

The second is that anything you put in front of it, it will use. A document you paste, a page a tool fetched, this morning’s figures typed in by hand — all of that lands in the conversation and gets treated as seriously as anything the model learned in training. This is how every current system answers a question about current events: not by knowing, but by being told, every single time.

It agreed with you, and it kept nothing

The trap is specific and it has caught serious teams. A model gets something wrong, someone corrects it, the model accepts the correction gracefully and gets it right for the rest of the session — and everybody concludes it has learned. It has not. Nothing that happens in a conversation writes to the model.

Two symptoms that this is what you are looking at. It is right on Tuesday and wrong on Wednesday from a fresh conversation. And it is right for the person who did the correcting and wrong for their colleague. If you need it to know something, the something has to be supplied every time — by you, by your system, or by a further training stage that costs real money.

The edge is fuzzy, and it cannot see it

A cutoff is a hard line in the data and a soft one in practice. The last stretch before it is thin, because the world had not finished writing about those months when the text was collected — so a model is reliably shakier about the half-year before its cutoff than about the five years before that.

Nor can a model look the date up. It knows roughly when its text stops the way you know roughly how old a photograph is, by what appears in it — which is why models state their own cutoff confidently and are sometimes wrong. Take the figure from the documentation, not from the model.

What is in the pile is being argued about

This stage is where nearly all of the genuine controversy around AI lives, and a unit that skipped it would be teaching the mechanism while hiding the argument. Four disputes, stated as disputes.

Copyright. A great deal of what was collected is under copyright. Authors, news organisations, publishers and image libraries have brought cases; some have settled, others are running, and courts in different countries have already come to different conclusions about whether training on copyrighted work is permitted. It is unresolved. Anyone who tells you it is obviously settled, in either direction, is telling you what they hope.

Consent. Most people who put something on the open web before about 2020 published it into a world where nothing like this existed. The mechanisms for asking not to be included arrived afterwards, and only work going forward. Whether “publicly visible” ever implied “available for this” is precisely the question being argued.

Who was in it. A pile of text is not a sample of humanity. It is a sample of what got written down, digitised, and left reachable — which leans hard towards particular languages, countries, decades and kinds of people. A model’s default manner of speaking is whatever was most common in the pile, and everybody else’s is treated as a departure from it.

What it cost to run. The energy and water used by the datacentres are disputed both in size and in how they should be weighed. Estimates vary by an order of magnitude depending on who is counting and what they count.

None of these is a footnote to the technical story. What went into the pile determines what comes out of it, and the pile is the part nobody will show you.

Where you actually meet this

You will almost certainly never run one of these. At this scale only a few dozen organisations in the world ever have. What you meet is the consequences, and four of them are worth carrying.

The cutoff date is the first thing to look up about a model, before the benchmark scores. Current facts have to be supplied rather than assumed, which is why so much engineering around these systems is really plumbing for handing the model text. A new model is a new model rather than an update, because knowledge cannot be topped up. And the same handful of pretrained models sits underneath a thousand products, because this is the one step almost nobody can afford to repeat.

It is worth being clear about what would break without this stage, too. Every later step — the ones that make a model helpful, safe, conversational, expert in your domain — adjusts the behaviour of something that already knows things. None of them can put knowledge in at anything like this scale, at any price a normal organisation could pay. The knowledge arrives here or it does not arrive.

For developers
List<Message> history = new ArrayList<>();     // yours, not the model's
history.add(Message.user("Our returns window is 60 days now."));
history.add(Message.user("What is our returns window?"));

String reply = model.complete(history);        // the whole list, every time

There is no model.learn(...) on that object and there is not going to be. Every request is stateless: the model is handed the entire conversation again from the beginning, reads it from cold, and keeps nothing. The list is a variable in your process.

The billing consequence follows immediately. Turn twenty resends turns one to nineteen, so the same question costs more late in a conversation than early in it.

Checkpoint

A company puts an assistant in front of its support team in March. In April the returns policy changes, and for a week the team corrects the assistant every time it quotes the old one — which it accepts, every time. In May it is still telling customers the old policy. What actually happened, and what would fix it?

Show answerHide answer

Nothing was learned, because nothing in a conversation can write to the model. Its knowledge was fixed when its pretraining run ended, and a week of corrections lived only inside the individual conversations where they were typed. Each of those ended, and the next one started from the same unaltered model.

The fix is to supply the policy rather than teach it: put the current text in front of the model on every request, through whatever mechanism the system has for handing it documents. A further training stage could shift its habits, but it costs real money and is the wrong tool anyway — a fact that changes belongs in text you can edit, not baked into weights you would have to pay to change again in June.

The general shape is worth keeping. Anything about the world that changes has to live somewhere you can update. The model is not that place, and it never was.
