The jobs nobody handed out

A copy-editor reads the same page four times.

Once for spelling. Once for the places where the tense slips. Once for names and dates that have to match something outside the page. Once for the sentences that say the same thing twice.

That is not slowness, and no amount of experience folds it into a single pass. Hold two of those questions at once and you find fewer of both. The eyes are the same eyes. What changes between passes is the question being held, and you only get to hold one.

Attention has that same limit, for a reason you can put your finger on.

One question at a time

In the last unit every word turned itself into three things — a question, a label and its contents — and the weights came out of comparing every question against every label. There is exactly one way of building that question and one way of building that label. One recipe, applied to every word in the sentence.

Change the recipe and every weight in the sentence changes with it. So a round of attention is not a reader you can ask things of. It is one question, put to every pair of words at once, and what comes back is one blend per word.

Ask it who did this and it cannot also ask what a pronoun stands for. The machinery that would have to ask the second question is already busy being the first.

That is what breaks if you stop here. A sentence is held together by several kinds of relationship at the same time, and a machine with one round of attention gets to follow exactly one of them.

Several at once, and then joined back up

Where the machine beats the copy-editor is that it does not have to take turns. Nothing in one round depends on the round beside it, so several can run in the same instant on the same sentence, each with its own recipe for questions and labels, each producing its own blend for every word.

One such round is a head. Running a row of them side by side and joining what they produce is multi-head attention, and it is what a transformer, the design nearly every large language model is built on, actually contains. The single round of the last unit does not appear on its own anywhere.

Joining is not choosing. The blends are laid end to end and mixed back down into one, so every word leaves the layer carrying all of the readings at once and nothing further along ever picks a favourite.

It is also cheaper than it sounds. The width of the layer is shared out among the heads rather than handed to each of them, so twelve heads in a layer 768 numbers wide get 64 numbers each. Twelve narrow readings for about the price of one wide one.

Which answers the obvious objection. Why not one enormous head instead? Because width was never the constraint. However many numbers a single head is given, it still has one recipe and still produces one set of weights per word. A second opinion needs a second recipe, and a second recipe is what a head is.

How many heads there are is not something the model works out either. It is typed into a configuration file before training starts, the same way the step size is.

Of the four heads, one has an opinion about “carried”. Head 2, the one asking “who did this?”, puts 40% of its attention on “student”. The other three have nothing to ask about this word, so each of them spreads 11% over every word in the sentence. Across the four readings, 31% of the attention sits somewhere different. There is a word in this sentence they disagree about more than this one.

These four heads were written by hand, and the questions under them are descriptions someone attached afterwards. A real model is given no questions at all — each head starts random and drifts into a job because being a copy of its neighbour makes the score no better.

Head 4 never lights up, whichever word you pick. It is asking about position, and nothing in attention knows where a word sits. That is the next unit.

It opens on carried. One row does something: the head asking who did this puts 40% of its attention on student. The other three lie flat at 11% on every word, which you have seen before and means the same thing here — a head with nothing to ask about this word spreads its attention evenly instead of picking.

Now move to she. A different row wakes up — the head asking what a word stands for, 43% on student — and the row that had the answer about carried falls silent. Same sentence, same instant, same machine.

Those two are not disagreeing about who is right. They are answering different questions, and only one of the questions was about she.

That word is also the answer to the hunt in the panel. Between the two furthest-apart rows, 37% of the attention sits somewhere different, and nothing else in the sentence comes near it — carried is second at 31%.

Then pick strong. Or because, or was. Nothing happens anywhere. Not one of the four has anything to ask about those words, so all four spread 11% evenly and hand back four identical readings, which between them are worth exactly one reading.

Five of these nine words are like that, and that is the honest size of the problem. Four heads is not many. A real layer runs twelve or sixteen, and a real model stacks that layer dozens deep.

One row never wakes at all, whichever word you choose. The fourth head is asking what came immediately before it, and nothing in attention knows where a word sits, which is the next unit’s business, not this one’s.

Nobody hands out the jobs

Here is the part that is easy to get wrong, and the reason those heads are numbered rather than named.

The four were written by hand, and the questions printed under them are labels attached afterwards so that you could read what was happening. The last unit was blunt about which parts of this machinery are the real thing and which are staged to be legible on nine words; this is the staged part.

A real head is handed no question. It starts random, like every other setting in the model, and for the whole of training the only pressure on it is the one pressure there is — make the wrongness score smaller.

Nothing in that pressure says “specialise”. What it says is narrower than that. A head duplicating the head beside it earns nothing, because the score is already getting that reading. A head noticing something no other head noticed makes the score better.

So they drift apart, and they do it because staying alike is worthless, not because the work was divided up.

So the labels arrive afterwards, and they arrive from people. Researchers have gone through trained models looking at what each head attends to, and a few are strikingly legible — one following a verb back to its subject, another tracking pronouns. Most are not. They are plainly doing something the model needs, since removing one makes it worse, and no description in English fits what it is.

There is no head labelled “grammar” inside a language model. There is a head that, when somebody looked, happened to be doing something a person chose to call grammar, in that model, at that layer, after that training run. Train the same design again from a different random start and the jobs land somewhere else.

For developers
n_embd = 768
n_head = 12          # so 64 numbers of width per head

# and this is the whole of "multi-head", twice
q = q.view(batch, tokens, n_head, n_embd // n_head).transpose(1, 2)
# ... attention runs here, with a head axis it never looks along ...
y = y.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(batch, tokens, n_embd)

The heads are not twelve objects. They are one array with an extra axis, so “in parallel” is not a scheduling claim. It is a single matrix multiply that happens to have a spare dimension. The last line is the joining: the axis is folded back in and the strips become one word again.

Worth noticing what is absent. n_head is a number somebody typed, and nowhere in the file, or anywhere else in the repository, is there a line saying what any particular head is for.

Checkpoint

A talk includes a diagram of a language model with twelve boxes in a row, labelled grammar, tone, pronouns, topic and so on. What is wrong with it, and what would an honest version look like?

Show answerHide answer

Nobody labelled those boxes. Every head starts random, and the only thing training rewards is a smaller wrongness score — heads end up different because a head that copies its neighbour adds nothing to that score, not because anyone divided the work between them. The words in that diagram are descriptions somebody wrote afterwards, by looking inside one trained model.

An honest version numbers the boxes and leaves most of them blank, with a note on one or two saying what a person found when they went and looked at this particular model, at this particular layer.

Worth keeping, because the mistake generalises past attention. A great deal of what looks like design inside a trained model is description after the fact. The parts are real. The names on them are ours.
