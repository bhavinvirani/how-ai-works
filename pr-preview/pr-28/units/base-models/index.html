Answering was never part of the deal

A screenshot lands in the group chat. Somebody has cropped a baking forum down to a single post, and the post is a question.

why does dough need to rest before you shape it?

Underneath it, the screenshot stops.

Nobody types the answer. What somebody types is and then four people argued about hydration for six pages, because that is what happens under a question on a forum, and everyone in the chat knows it.

You knew the answer too, near enough. Leave dough alone for half an hour and it stops fighting you.

You did not offer it, because nobody had asked you. What you had been handed was a page, and pages have shapes; asked what came next on one, you predicted a page.

That reflex, finish the document rather than answer the line, is the whole of what a language model can do when it comes out of the stage before this one.

Everything it will ever know, and one thing it cannot do

The previous unit followed a model through pretraining: months of machinery, a filtered mountain of text, and one game played from the first day to the last — here is some text with the next piece hidden, guess the piece.

What comes out of that has a name. A base model is a model that has finished pretraining and has had nothing done to it since. Grammar, dozens of languages, an enormous quantity of fact, the shape of an argument, the shape of a recipe, the shape of a legal disclaimer.

By cost, that is very nearly the whole machine. Everything done to it afterwards is a rounding error beside it.

Ask it a question and it will not answer you.

Not because it does not know. Because answering is not a thing it does.

What it does instead

Type that forum question into a base model, exactly as it stands. Here are four continuations, all of them the kind of thing real base models produce:

  • two more questions, this time about flour
  • a line reading Posted 3 years ago · 14 replies · Report
  • a chapter heading — Chapter 4: Shaping and proving
  • the same question again, in Spanish

Every one of those is correct. That is the part worth slowing down for. The machine is not confused, not broken and not too small. It is doing the single thing it has ever been scored on, and doing it well.

The mismatch is entirely on your side of the screen. You believe you asked something. Inside the machine nothing was asked, because there is nobody in there to ask. A run of characters arrived carrying the shape of a question, and in the pile it learned from, that shape is followed by all sorts of things.

The same model, one small change

Now stop asking. Start the sentence for it instead:

Dough is rested before shaping because

And it finishes: the gluten relaxes, so it stops springing back when you try to stretch it.

Correct, fluent, first attempt, no hesitation. Same model, same dials, nothing trained in between, no clever instruction and no example. The only thing that changed is that continuing the text and telling you the answer became the same act.

So the fact was in there the whole time, about a quarter of a second below the surface. It was never the knowledge that was missing.

That pulls apart two things which feel like one thing when the only machine you have ever used is a chat assistant. There is what a model knows. And there is whether anything in it registers that it has been addressed. Pretraining delivers the first in industrial quantity and the second not at all.

Nothing in the pile ever said “you”

It is tempting to file this under an oversight — a gap somebody will get round to patching. It is not. It falls out of the arrangement, and it could not have come out any other way.

Think about where answers actually sit in text. Under a question on a forum: more questions, a moderator note, a link, three replies that miss the point. Under a question in a textbook: four options labelled (a) to (d), with the answer forty pages away at the back. Under a question in a list of search results: a title, a date, an advertisement. Answers do follow questions, often, but they are one branch among many, and nothing in training ever marked that branch as the one that counts.

There is a sharper version of the same point. To the model, pretraining is one undivided document. No part of it is labelled as coming from anybody, and no part is labelled as being directed at the model. There is no second party anywhere in the arrangement, so there is nobody to be helpful towards. Being asked is a relationship, and the training had no room in it for one.

Helpfulness is a habit, and it is a thin one

Being an assistant, then, is not knowledge and it is not intelligence. It is a habit — a learned tendency about which shape of text follows which shape of text. Somebody has to put it there deliberately, afterwards, and putting it there is what the next two units are about.

Now hold that against the thing you actually use. The assistant you talk to is a base model with a habit painted over the top. The continuer underneath did not go anywhere and was not replaced. At every step, including the one it is taking right now, the machine is producing the most plausible next piece of text; the later stages changed which continuations it finds plausible, not what it is doing.

Two consequences fall out of that, and each gets a unit of its own further on. The first is that plausible and true are different properties, and only one of them was ever trained for. The second is that a habit is a strong tendency rather than a wall, which is most of what there is to say about why an assistant’s good behaviour can sometimes be talked out of it, and why nobody has managed to make that airtight.

Where this stops being trivia

Two places, and both of them have cost people a day.

The download page. Open models are usually published twice, under names that differ only by a suffix — one plain, one carrying instruct, chat or it. The plain one is the base model. The suffixed one is that same model with the habit trained in. If you want something that answers, you want the suffixed one, and the symptom of getting it wrong is exactly the screenshot at the top of this page: replies that read like a page torn out of the internet. The natural diagnosis is that the model is broken, or too small, or that the download went wrong. It is none of those.

The two endpoints. Hosted models are typically offered in two shapes: one that takes a lump of text and continues it, and one that takes a list of messages and replies to the last of them. It is easy to read those as two different machines. They are one machine and a wrapper. Your list of messages is flattened into a single document, with markers written in to say whose turn each part is, and that document is handed to the continuer, which is asked for the next bit.

Read that again, because it is the honest description of every conversation you have ever had with an assistant. The transcript is a document. The reply is a continuation of it. What the two stages after this one buy is a machine for which, once that document runs out at the marker where its own turn starts, the most plausible continuation is something useful.

Why anybody would want the base version

Base models are not rough drafts that got superseded. They are raw material, and people download them on purpose.

If you intend to shape a model’s behaviour yourself — for one profession, one house style, one language — then starting from a model already trained into somebody else’s idea of helpfulness means fighting that first. A habit is far easier to install than to remove.

They are also the only honest measure of what pretraining alone bought. Any claim about what scale by itself produces has to be a claim about base models, because every stage after pretraining is another variable in the experiment.

For developers
String document = chatTemplate.render(messages) + ASSISTANT_TURN_MARKER;
String reply = model.complete(document);

Two lines, and the second half of the first one is the part people forget exists. The moment you want a prompt shape your provider does not offer, you end up building that document yourself, and leaving off the marker that opens the assistant’s turn raises no error at all. The model simply continues the wrong turn and writes the user’s next message for them.

Checkpoint

A team wires an open model into their support tool. Every reply comes back as a handful of further questions, or as something that reads like the bottom of a web page. One person says the model is too small; another wants to spend a fortnight training it on the company's support history. Both are guesses. What is the one-minute check that settles it, and what would the result tell you?

Show answerHide answer

Stop asking it things. Hand it the opening of a sentence instead, A parcel marked as delivered that has not arrived is usually, and read what comes back.

If it finishes that fluently and sensibly, the model is fine and so is its knowledge. What is missing is the habit of treating a question aimed at it as something to answer, and no amount of extra size or extra data supplies that. They have downloaded the base version and they want the instruct one. The fix is a different file, not a fortnight.

Worth keeping as a shape. Knowing something and being willing to hand it over on request are separate properties, acquired at separate stages and for separate reasons, and a surprising number of confusing things a model does get easier to place the moment you stop treating them as one property.

  • Train the habit, hand over the fact — That is the stage where the missing habit gets installed, by showing the model tens of thousands of worked examples in which the text following a request happens to be a good response to it.
  • Nobody could write it, but anyone could choose — Getting a model to answer at all is one problem and getting it to answer well is a separate one, because nobody can write down what a good answer is. It has to be collected from people picking between real ones.
