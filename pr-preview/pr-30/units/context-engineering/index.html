The skill is what you leave out

Somebody is covering your desk for a fortnight, and the handover is one side of A4. Nobody imposed that. It is simply how much you have time to write on the Friday and how much they have time to read on the Monday.

You sit down to fill it and discover, within about a minute, that you are not writing. You are choosing.

The standing things have to go on: who to ring about what, the two promises nobody may make to a customer. Then the live cases. Then the awkward one that lands on Thursday, which needs half the page to itself if it needs anything at all. And the bottom third has to stay blank, because they will be writing on this sheet too.

Notice what the difficulty is not. It is not that you cannot write small enough. Hand yourself a second sheet and the same argument restarts one page later, because the number of things that could go on the page was never the limit. The job, the whole job, is deciding what does not go on it.

The sheet is shared, and most of it is not yours

A model’s context window is that sheet, and you have met the limit itself already: everything the model can see at the moment it answers has to fit inside a fixed number of tokens — the chunks text is cut into before a model sees any of it, a little under a word apiece — and its reply is written into the same space.

What that unit did not do is open the sheet up and look at who is on it.

The standing instructions. The application’s own briefing — who the model is here, what it must not say, how to lay an answer out. You did not write it and you cannot shorten it. In a real product it runs to hundreds or thousands of tokens, and it is sent again ahead of every single message.

The tool descriptions. If the assistant can search the web, send an email or query a database, each of those abilities reaches the model as a written description: a name, a sentence about when to use it, and a list of what it takes. One is nothing. Forty is an essay the model reads before it reads a word of yours, on every call, including the calls where none of them is relevant.

The conversation so far. Nothing is remembered between messages, so all of it is sent again every turn, and it only ever grows.

Whatever was fetched. In a system that answers from your own documents, a search runs before the model does, and its results are pasted in on your behalf. This is the biggest and the most variable of the five. Five passages at a thousand tokens each outweigh everything else on this list put together.

Room for the answer. Set aside before anything is let in, because the reply is written into the same window the question arrived in.

Five claimants, one pot, and exactly one of them is the sentence you typed.

Deciding how that pot gets divided, on each call, is a real job with a real name: context engineering. It is the work of assembling everything the model will see, inside a limit that does not move.

Prompting is one message. This is the whole page.

Prompting is about a single message — what to ask for, how to phrase it, which examples to put in. It quietly assumes that the message is the thing you control.

In any system worth the name, it is not. Most of what the model reads on a given call was assembled by software before your sentence arrived: a standing instruction somebody wrote in March, a history that has already been trimmed twice, five passages a search chose, a tool list that grew by three last week. Your sentence is a small part of what is on the page, and often not the part causing the trouble.

Which is why this has a separate name from prompting. Different unit of work. Prompting edits one message; context engineering decides what accompanies it.

The failure it exists to prevent is specific, and it looks nothing like a model problem. An assistant that answered beautifully in testing starts giving thinner, vaguer answers in production. Nobody changed the model. Nobody changed the prompt. What changed is that conversations got longer, three more tools were added, and somebody tuned the search to return eight passages instead of three. Everything got a little more crowded, so everything got a little worse, and there is no line anywhere in the code where that happened.

Three places the room actually goes

The moves that work are unglamorous, and all three are subtraction.

Summarise the old rather than carrying it

A conversation’s early turns are the cheapest thing to compress and the most expensive thing to keep, because you are paying full price for them on every remaining turn. Replacing the first twenty exchanges with a short standing summary — what was agreed, what the constraints are, what has been ruled out — buys back most of that room for a fraction of the length.

It is not free, and the unfree part is easy to miss. A summary keeps whatever the summariser thought was worth keeping, and nobody asked you which parts those were. What you are trading is detail you cannot name in advance for room you can measure exactly, which is an uncomfortable trade and still usually the right one.

Fetch fewer, and better

This is the one that surprises people, because the obvious move is the wrong one.

Retrieval is usually judged on whether the right passage came back at all, and that number goes up when you return more passages. So teams raise it, three becomes ten, and the measured retrieval quality improves while the answers get worse.

The reason is that the seven extra passages did not arrive as neutral filler. They arrived as near-misses: pages that matched the same words, on the same subject, saying something else. Now the model is holding one page that answers the question and seven that look like it, with nothing anywhere marking which is which, and attention spent on one is attention taken from another.

Fewer and better means putting a second, sharper step after the search, one that reorders the candidates and cuts the list, and it costs real work. It is still the highest-value thing most document systems are missing.

Keep the standing instructions short, because you pay for them every time

Everything else on the sheet is paid for once per occurrence. The standing instructions are paid for on every call, in every conversation, for as long as the product runs.

So a hundred tokens trimmed from a system prompt is a hundred tokens saved on the first message, and on the fortieth, and on the four million after that. There is nothing else in one of these systems with that shape of leverage, and the standing instruction is almost always written once, in a hurry, by whoever set the thing up, and never read again.

The same argument applies to tool descriptions, and harder, because they are longer. Most steps of most jobs could only ever need two or three of the available tools. Describing all forty of them anyway is a cost paid on every request, to no end.

The middle of the page

There is one more finding here, and it is the one people find hardest to believe, because it says that where you put something matters.

A model does not use a long window evenly. Material at the very beginning and at the very end is picked up most reliably. Material buried in the middle of a long context is the easiest thing to skate over — present, paid for, read, and still less likely to make it into the answer.

So a passage can be in the window and effectively unused, which breaks the comfortable assumption underneath most of this work: that getting the right thing in is the same as getting it used.

That makes reordering the cheapest improvement available anywhere in this subject. It costs nothing, drops nothing, and nothing new has to be built.

The recipe is short. Put the instruction that has to hold at the very top or the very bottom. Put the strongest passage nearest the question. Let the weakest ones take the middle, where they were going to be half-read anyway.

One window, five documents, and one page that answers

The panel below is the same board the window unit used. Not a similar one — the same instrument, the same arithmetic, with different things loaded into it.

That is not a shortcut. It is most of what this unit has to say. A window has no idea what kind of thing it is holding. An instruction somebody wrote in March, a sentence you typed, an answer it wrote itself, a page a search fetched two hundred milliseconds ago: all of it is tokens, all of it competes on identical terms, and nothing in there is marked important. Only the claimants changed.

This window holds two thousand tokens. Two hundred and twenty are gone on the standing instruction before anything happens, and five hundred are held back for the reply. That leaves one thousand two hundred and eighty, so more than a third of the window is spoken for before a single message or document arrives.

When it will not fit, throw out

What almost everything does when nobody has decided otherwise. It is cheap, it needs no judgement, and it always throws away the beginning — which is where people put the things that have to hold for the whole conversation.

  • it — the answer it gave then150 · in the window
  • you — the question being answered right now45 · in the window, and cannot be dropped
  • fetched — the returns policy, the page that answers it320 · in the window
  • fetched — the shipping policy, pulled in because it reads alike280 · in the window, and cannot be dropped

A thousand tokens is about a page and a half. Real windows are hundreds of times bigger — and they fill up, for exactly the reasons drawn here: one pasted document, one long transcript, one day of back-and-forth. A bigger window moves the moment. It does not remove it.

It opens at five pieces: two messages of earlier conversation, the question being answered now, and the first two pages the search returned. Everything fits, with four hundred and forty-five tokens to spare, and the returns policy, the page that actually answers the question, is in there.

Read the last line of the readout before you touch anything. You have typed eighty-five tokens. It has read two thousand nine hundred and fifty-five. Almost nothing the model is reading came from you, which is most of the reason this is somebody’s job rather than a phrasing tip.

Step to six. A third page arrives and it still fits, with ninety-five tokens spare. Step to seven and it does not.

Now try all three rules on that seventh piece, because this is the reassuring part. Dropping the oldest throws out the two earlier messages. Dropping the biggest throws out last year’s returns policy, withdrawn in March, which is exactly the page you would have wanted gone. Summarising the oldest squeezes those two earlier messages into a twenty-four-token stand-in. Three rules, three different casualties, and every one of them keeps the page that answers the question.

Then step to eight. One more fetched page, a support thread that happens to use all the same words, and every rule breaks.

The oldest first reaches past the history and takes the returns policy itself, because it arrived before the four pages that followed it.

The biggest first takes the withdrawn policy and then the current one, because the page that answers a question is usually a substantial page. The rule that saved you at seven pieces is the rule that costs you at eight.

Summarising the oldest crushes four pieces, seven hundred and ninety tokens of them, into ninety-nine, the returns policy among them. It survives at an eighth of its length, and whether the clause that mattered made it in is not something anybody checked.

Nothing was retrieved wrongly. The right page came back, and it came back first. It is the fifth one, a support thread that nobody needed and no rule knew to prefer, that pushed it out.

Worse than an empty window

A window with no returns policy in it is not neutral. It is four pages that use all the same words and answer something else.

Asked with nothing in front of it, a model has some chance of saying it does not know. Asked with four near-misses in front of it, it has a page about shipping, a page about warranties, a withdrawn policy from last year and a support thread — all plausible, all relevant-looking, none of them the answer. The most reasonable next sentence is an answer built out of those.

Retrieval that fetches the wrong thing is not a smaller version of retrieval that fetches nothing. It is a different and worse failure, and it is the one that crowding produces.

A bigger window moves the moment; it does not remove it

The instinct at this point is to buy more room, and more room is genuinely available — windows have grown by orders of magnitude in a few years, and they will grow again.

It does not fix the allocation problem. It postpones it, and it postpones it into worse conditions.

Doubling the window doubles what fits and roughly quadruples the work of attending to it, because every piece of the text is compared against every other piece. The bill and the wait go up faster than the capacity does.

It also makes the middle longer. The stretch of context that gets skated over grows with the window, so a system that fills the new space with marginal material has bought itself more places to bury the thing that mattered.

And the practical effect is the one nobody plans for. A tight window forces the trimming to happen. A generous one means nobody trims anything until the day it overflows — by which point the standing instruction has been added to eleven times, forty tools are described on every call, and the conversation nobody summarised is nine months old.

Empty is not wasted

A window is quoted as a number, and a number invites you to fill it. Every habit you have about capacity says that unused space is space you failed to use.

Not here. The empty part of a window is not a failure to fill it; it is the attention the model has left for the things you did put in. The measure of a well-assembled context is not how much of the window it occupies. It is how little.

For developers
int budget = MODEL_WINDOW
           - REPLY_RESERVE           // taken out first, always
           - count(systemPrompt)
           - count(toolSchemas);     // the line people forget

List<Passage> chosen = new ArrayList<>();
for (Passage p : reranked) {         // best first, not search order
    if (count(p) > budget) break;
    chosen.add(p);
    budget -= count(p);
}

Two lines are doing the arguing. count(toolSchemas) is the one that gets left out, because tool definitions are written as JSON and JSON is verbose: a dozen tools with described arguments is routinely longer than the system prompt sitting above them, and it is charged on every request whether or not the model uses one.

reranked is the other. Iterating the search results in the order the search returned them fills the budget with whatever scored well on a keyword match. Iterating a reordered list fills the same budget with the best candidates, at the same price. Same loop, same tokens, different answers.

Checkpoint

A team's document assistant works well. In one release they do three sensible things: they move to a model with a window four times larger, they raise the number of retrieved passages from three to ten, and they add six new tools. Nothing else changes — same documents, same instructions, same model family. Answers get noticeably worse and the bill roughly triples. Explain why the larger window did not save them, and say which change you would undo first.

Show answerHide answer

The window was never the constraint on quality. It was the constraint on capacity, and those are different failures: running out is loud, and crowding is silent. Making the window bigger removed the loud one and left more room for the quiet one.

The ten passages are the change that hurt the answers. Seven of them are near-misses — same subject, same words, different content — and nothing in the window marks which of the ten is the one that answers the question. Attention spread across ten is attention taken from the one. It is also now far more likely to be sitting somewhere in the middle, which is where material gets used least reliably.

The bill comes from both, and the tools are the half that hides. Seven extra passages are at least attached to a request somebody made. Six tool descriptions are sent on every single call, in every conversation, whether or not that request could ever have used one of them — a standing charge nobody asked for and nobody sees on any particular answer.

Undo the passages first. It is the change that touched the material answers are built from, reversing it is free, and the improvement shows up on the next request. Then cut the tool list down to what a given step could plausibly need. The larger window can stay. It is not doing harm, it is simply not doing the work they hoped it would.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. When a system gets worse after a release that added room, stop looking at the room and look at what filled it.
