Nearest is not the same as matching

There is a hardware shop on the high street with about forty thousand things in it and one man behind the counter who started there in 1988.

You go in and ask for the thing that stops a door slamming.

He is back inside a minute with a door closer, which is what it is called, and which you did not know when you walked in. Nothing on any drawer in that shop says stops a door slamming. Had the shop been a wall of labelled drawers and no man, you would have left with nothing.

Come back on Thursday and ask for a number 8 by 40 pozidriv countersunk screw in stainless.

Now he is the slow one. He knows roughly where the screws live and he will get there, but roughly is what he does, and roughly is not what you asked for. The drawer marked 8 × 40 PZ CSK A2 would have had you back out on the pavement in four seconds.

A shop with only the man is bad at the second question. A shop with only the drawers is bad at the first. Nobody calls having both a clever arrangement; it is just what a shop is.

Hold on to that, because a system searching your documents has exactly the same two halves, is bad at exactly the same two things, and ends up at exactly the same answer.

A question is a position too

Getting documents into one of these systems is a two-part job, and you have met both parts. The documents are cut into pieces small enough to be useful. Then every piece is run through the machinery that turns text into a position — a long list of numbers saying where something sits, in a space arranged so that things meaning similar things end up near each other.

That is the whole of the storing step. Cut it up, and put each piece somewhere.

Nothing so far has said how anything comes back out.

Here is the move, and it is one line long. Run the question through exactly the same machinery.

The question is text. The machinery turns text into positions. So the question gets a position too — the same kind of object, in the same space, measured against the same everything.

And now the question has stopped being a question. It is a spot. The passages are spots. Searching is: which spots are nearest to this one?

Sit with that for a moment, because the rest of the unit falls out of it. No word of the question is ever held up against any word of a passage. Nothing is looked up. There is a measurement, how far this position sits from that one, and a list sorted by the answer.

Which explains the thing that looks like a trick. A passage can come back first while sharing not one word with the question, because words were never what got compared. The question landed in the part of the space where being shut out of an account lives, and a passage about failed sign-in attempts had been sitting there for months, put there by the same machinery, in the same way, before anybody asked anything.

Semantic search is the usual name for this, semantic meaning to do with meaning. Vector search is the other name, vector being the technical word for the list of numbers that is a position. They are the same thing and neither name is more correct.

The number you will see quoted

The distance in these systems is almost never a ruler measurement. It is an angle, how nearly two positions point the same way, reported as cosine similarity, where 1 means the same direction and 0 means directions with nothing to do with each other. It is the scale the panel below reports on.

Angle rather than length, because the length of a position turns out to carry mostly how much text there was, and hardly anything about what the text was about. Measuring by length would make a one-line answer and a five-page document on the same subject look unrelated. Measuring by angle lets them sit on top of each other, which is what you wanted.

Where near is exactly the wrong idea

Now the second question in the shop.

Type E-4102 at a system like this and follow what has to happen. That string is text, so it gets a position, the way everything does. But a position is learned from company kept — from what turns up around a thing, across an enormous amount of writing. E-4102 has almost no company. It appears a handful of times in one manual, next to whatever happened to be next to it.

So it gets a position, and the position carries almost nothing.

Now the sharper half. Whatever position E-4102 ends up with, E-4120 ends up with one almost exactly like it. Two strings of the same shape, appearing in the same kind of sentence, in the same kind of document, doing the same job in every one. On a map built out of how things get used, they are the same kind of thing, so they go in the same place.

Nothing has gone wrong there. You asked for near and it gave you near. What you wanted was identical, and identical is not a very small distance, it is not a distance at all.

The same goes for a surname, a part number, a version string, an invoice reference, a street. Anything whose entire value is that it is exactly itself and not the thing next to it.

The half that does not know anything

The other half is older than everything else on this site and nobody has improved on it much.

Split every passage into words. Keep a list, for each word, of the passages containing it. Look a word up, get the passages back. That arrangement is called an inverted index, inverted because an ordinary index runs from document to words and this one runs the other way, and it is what sat under every search box you used before about 2020.

It knows nothing whatsoever about meaning, and that is precisely why it works here. E-4102 and E-4120 are two different strings, so they sit on two different lists, and no amount of looking alike changes that. There is nothing in there to fool.

One refinement matters enough to name. A word appearing on every passage tells you nothing; a word appearing on one tells you a great deal. So a match is weighted by how rare the word is across the shelf, which is why the is worth nothing and ashgrove decides the whole question on its own. Production systems use a formula called BM25, which is this idea plus two sensible corrections for long documents and repeated words. The panel below uses the plain version, and nothing in what follows depends on the difference.

Two questions, and meaning search has won both outright. This is roughly where most explanations of it stop.

E-4102. Somebody has pasted an error code with the word error in front of it.

Read the meaning column slowly, because the interesting part is not that it is wrong. Error E-4120 scores 0.63. Error E-4102, which is the page that answers the question, scores 0.62. One hundredth of a point.

That is not a near miss. A hundredth is the machine reporting that it cannot separate the two, and which of them surfaces on top is a coin landing. If the gap were wide you would be looking at a tuning problem. At a hundredth you are looking at the shape of the tool.

The word column has no such difficulty. It scores the right page 3.47 and the other 1.39, and the reason is printed underneath: the right page matched error and e-4102, the wrong one matched error and nothing else, because e-4120 is a different string and different strings live on different lists.

Ashgrove. A place name, on its own.

Look at the whole meaning column rather than the top of it. Every one of the eight scores between 0.15 and 0.26 — a band a tenth of a point wide, across an entire shelf. Nothing here is more relevant than anything else, and that is not the machine being cautious. The name has no company anywhere in what the model read, so it has no position worth having, and eight distances measured from a meaningless spot are eight indistinguishable numbers. The right page does come second, and there is no sense in which it earned that.

The word column returns one page, and it is the right one.

So the scoreboard reads two out of four, and two out of four, and it is the same two either way round. Whichever single method you build on, half your questions come back wrong, and they are precisely the questions the other method would have got right.

Running both is the ordinary answer

Turn on the switch and a third list appears. It gets all four.

It is not doing anything clever. The two columns sit on scales with nothing to do with each other — one is an angle between positions, the other a pile of rarity weights — so a score from one cannot simply be added to a score from the other. Each list is divided by its own best score first, which puts both on nought to one, and then the two are mixed:

combined=0.7×by meaning+0.3×by words\text{combined} = 0.7 \times \text{by meaning} + 0.3 \times \text{by words}combined=0.7×by meaning+0.3×by words

Read in words: seven parts how near it is, three parts how many of your words it has, once the two have been put on a comparable footing.

The 0.7 is not a discovery. Somebody picked it. Every product offering both halves exposes that number as a dial, and teams move it by running their own questions and looking at what comes back, which sounds unsatisfying and is simply where the subject is.

The arrangement is called hybrid search, and the name does it a disservice, because hybrid suggests a refinement somebody adds later once the basics are working. It is closer to the default. A team shipping meaning search on its own has not built the simple version. They have built the version that cannot find an order number.

What is real in that panel, and what is typed in

Worth being exact, because half of it is arithmetic and half of it is not.

Real: the whole word column. The pages are split into words, the shared ones are found, each is weighted by how few pages hold it, and the totals are added up. When the panel says a page shares not one word with the question, that is the same comparison reporting an empty result — checked by running it, not by somebody typing in a zero.

Typed in: the whole meaning column. A page in a browser cannot run an embedding model, so those distances were written by hand. They are chosen to behave the way measured systems behave — the ordinary questions answered well, the two error pages a hundredth apart, the bare name producing a flat column — but no model reported any of them, and they should be read as an argument rather than as a measurement.

What a vector database actually is

Comparing a question against eight positions is nothing. You can do it with a loop and a few lines of arithmetic. Comparing it against ten million, on every request, is a different proposition, and it is the entire reason a vector database exists.

What it is, is an index built for one question: given this position, which stored positions are nearest? It gets its speed by not checking most of them. The stored positions are grouped in advance, so a search visits a few promising groups and skips the rest, and comes back with almost always the right answers rather than certainly the right answers. That trade is called approximate nearest neighbour, and the approximate is not a flaw in it. The approximate is what you are buying.

Now the part the name encourages people to get backwards. There is no meaning stored in a vector database. Whatever meaning is involved was decided by the model that produced the positions, before any of them were stored, and the store holds numbers and answers questions about distance. It is a filing system, and it is excellent at being one.

Two things that catch people out

You may well not need one. Under a hundred thousand passages, an ordinary database with a similarity extension will answer this in a few milliseconds, and it is one fewer system to run, back up and pay for. The point at which a dedicated one starts earning its keep is a good deal further along than the marketing suggests.

The positions belong to the model that made them. Change the model that turns text into positions and every position you have stored becomes meaningless, not degraded, meaningless, because it is a location in a space that no longer exists. All of it has to be made again. Worth knowing before you have forty million of them and a re-run nobody has budgeted for.

The thing to stop believing

The instinct this unit is arguing with is that meaning search is the modern one and word matching is the old one, so a system built today should obviously use the modern one. Both halves of that are wrong, and the second half is wrong in a way that ships.

They are not a new and an old way of doing one job. They are two jobs. One answers what is this about, and it is the only thing that can find a page written in words the asker does not know. The other answers where does this exact string occur, and it is the only thing that can tell E-4102 from E-4120. Neither question is a worse version of the other, and no amount of progress on one will ever turn it into the other.

Which is why the man and the drawers are both still in the shop.

For developers
float[] position = embedder.embed(question);   // the same model that filled the store

List<Hit> byMeaning = store.nearest(position, 20);
List<Hit> byWord    = index.search(question, 20);

Two calls, two round trips, two sets of results, and nothing anywhere that makes them comparable. byMeaning carries angles; byWord carries scores from a different formula on a different scale. Merging them is your problem and there is no correct answer, which is why every library that does it ships a weight and a default.

The line to stare at is the first one. embedder has to be the same model, at the same version, that produced everything already in store. Swap it and nothing throws: you get positions in a different space, distances that are arithmetically perfect, and results that are quietly and plausibly wrong. It is one of the few mistakes in this area with no error message attached to it at all.

Checkpoint

A team ships an assistant over their support documentation using meaning search. It works well in the demo and well for most customers. Then complaints start arriving from the ones who quote an order reference, or a specific error code, or the surname of the engineer who visited — for those, the assistant answers confidently from the wrong page. Somebody proposes swapping in a better embedding model. Say why that will not fix it, and what will.

Show answerHide answer

Because there is nothing wrong with the embedding model. It is being asked for near and it is returning near, correctly. An order reference like GH-88412 has almost no company anywhere in the text any model was built from, so the position it gets carries almost no information, and the position GH-88421 gets is practically the same one. A better model puts both of those positions slightly more sensibly, and the two of them stay indistinguishable, because being indistinguishable is a fact about the strings rather than about the model.

Notice what the failure looks like from outside, too. It is not an empty result. Meaning search always returns something, because every stored passage has a distance, so the customer gets a confident answer assembled from the nearest wrong page. A method with no way of coming back empty-handed is the one that needs the other half most.

The fix is the dull one. Run word matching alongside it, over the same passages, and combine the two lists. That is not an upgrade to their system; it is the missing half of it, and it is what everybody shipping this in earnest already does.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. When somebody proposes making retrieval better, the question that settles it is what kind of question is failing. If people are asking in their own words, a better model may genuinely help. If the question contains a string that has to match exactly, no model will, and the thing you are missing is not smarter than what you have. It is older.
