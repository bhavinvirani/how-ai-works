When somebody already knows the answer

It is your first shift on the sorting line at a recycling plant. Cardboard to the left, plastics to the right, anything contaminated into the bin in front of you. Nobody hands you a manual.

Instead a woman called Dee stands at your shoulder. You reach for a pizza box and she says “grease, that one is contaminated”. You hesitate over a yoghurt pot and she says “fine, plastics”. Wrong, right, wrong, right, perhaps two hundred times before lunch.

By the afternoon she has stopped talking and you are keeping up with the belt. Ask you what the rule is and you cannot say it. Something about the sheen on the cardboard, the weight of it, the smell. You could not write it down for the next person any more than Dee could.

Notice what actually did the teaching, though. Not the belt, and not the practice. Every one of those two hundred attempts was worth something because somebody standing beside you already knew the right answer for that exact item, and said it out loud within a second of your getting it wrong.

The hole in the loop

The training loop has the same shape and the same requirement. Guess, compare, blame, nudge, but compare against what?

Every pass round that cycle quietly assumes that somewhere, sitting next to the example, is the answer the machine should have given. This unit is about the case where that assumption is simply true, because a person put the answer there first.

Ten thousand photographs, and beside each one a word somebody typed: cat, or dog. That word is a label — the correct answer for one example, recorded in advance by someone who already knew it. A pile of examples with their labels attached is an answer key, in exactly the sense of the answers at the back of a textbook.

Now the loop closes properly. The machine looks at photograph 4,109 and says cat. The answer key says dog. That gap is not a matter of opinion, and it is not a verdict that arrives next month: it is available immediately, for that exact photograph, and it points somewhere — less cat, more dog. Do that ten thousand times over, a few million times over, and the dials settle somewhere that also works on photographs nobody has ever labelled.

That is supervised learning, and for once the name is honest. It is as though a supervisor stands over the machine correcting every single attempt, the way Dee stood over you.

Two shapes of being wrong

The answer key can hold two kinds of answer, and which kind it holds changes what a mistake even looks like.

Sometimes the answer is a choice from a fixed set: junk or not junk, which of the ten digits, benign or malignant. That is classification, and being wrong means landing in the wrong box. There is no such thing as nearly right — you either picked the labelled box or you did not.

Sometimes the answer is a number on a sliding scale: the price a house sold for, tomorrow’s temperature, how long a delivery took. That is regression, and being wrong has a size. Guessing £240,000 when the house went for £310,000 is wrong by seventy thousand pounds, and wrong in a particular direction — too low. So each correction says more than a classification correction does: not only which way to move, but how far.

Same loop underneath, same arithmetic. The only thing that changed is what somebody wrote in the answer column.

Why almost everything you have met works this way

For most of this field’s history, supervised learning was not one kind of machine learning. It was the kind that worked. The fraud check on your card, the app that gathers up every photograph of your dog, the transcription in your voice notes, the model that flags a suspicious chest scan — every one of them was trained on examples somebody had answered first.

The reason it dominates is worth stating plainly. This is the only arrangement in which the machine is told, on every single example, exactly how wrong it was and in which direction. Nothing has to be guessed at, and nothing has to be waited for. A great deal of the rest of this site is, one way or another, about coping when that luxury is not on offer.

The bill arrives before the training does

Every one of those answers was produced by a person, one at a time, before any training began.

For cats and dogs that is cheap. Anyone can label a photograph, and plenty of people will do it for very little. For chest scans it is not cheap at all. Somebody has to be a qualified radiologist, they have to be paid, and there are only so many of them — the marking costs more than the computing does, and it takes far longer. For contracts marked up for legal risk, you are buying afternoons of a solicitor’s time.

So the ceiling on what you can build is not the size of your dataset. It is the size of your answer key. Seventy thousand scans sitting on a hospital server with three thousand of them marked is a three-thousand-scan project, and no amount of faster hardware moves that number by one.

It is also the precise reason the machines that write English could not have been built this way. Their training material runs to something like a trillion words, and no committee on earth could sit down and write the correct answer beside each one. Getting round that bottleneck, finding answer keys that nobody has to write, is most of why the last few years happened, and it is where this Part is going.

The answer key is somebody's opinion

The formal name for the answer key is the ground truth, which sounds grander than it is: it means whatever the people doing the marking decided was correct. Nothing checks them. When two radiologists disagree about a scan, one of those two judgements ends up in the key as fact, and the machine learns it exactly as confidently as it learns everything else. Where labelling is rushed, or sent to people who were never told what to do with the awkward cases, the damage does not arrive as visible errors. It arrives as a model that is quietly and consistently wrong in the same way its markers were.

For developers
// Two arrays, same length, same order.
double[][] measurements = loadFeatures();   // 10,000 photographs
String[]   answers      = loadLabels();     // 10,000 words, typed by hand

model.fit(measurements, answers);

Every supervised training call in every language has this shape: the things you can measure, and the answers, lined up row for row. The second array is the entire content of this unit, and it is the one that does not exist yet on the day somebody asks you to build the model.

Checkpoint

You have two million product photographs, and enough budget to have five thousand of them labelled by hand. Which of those two numbers limits what you can build, and why?

The five thousand. A photograph with no answer beside it gives the machine nothing to compare its guess against, so the other 1,995,000 are invisible to this method. They are not a smaller contribution, they are no contribution.

Worth holding onto, because it explains where the field went next. Faster hardware does not touch this limit, and neither does a cleverer model. The only ways out are to get answers more cheaply, or to find data that answers itself.
