Feeling your way downhill

The fog came down an hour ago. You are somewhere on a mountainside, you can see about a metre in every direction, and the car park is in the valley. It is getting dark.

You cannot see the valley. You cannot see the path, or the ridge, or the drop somewhere off to your left. What you can do is stand still and notice which way the ground tips under your boots, and then take one step that way.

Stop. Feel again. Step again.

It is slow and undignified and it works. You never need a map, and you never need to know how far away the valley is. You only need to tell downhill from uphill from exactly where you are standing.

Every model you have ever used was trained by doing precisely this, a few million times.

The mountain is made of settings

The mountain is not a real mountain, and this is the one part worth going slowly over.

The machine is a box of dials. Every possible position of every dial is a place you could stand: turn one dial a little and you shuffle sideways, turn a different one and you move in a different direction entirely.

Now give every one of those places a height. The height is the wrongness score — the single number saying how badly the machine’s guesses miss the examples it is being judged on. Terrible settings stand on high ground. Good settings sit down in the hollows.

That is the landscape. Training means getting to a low place in it, and nothing else is going on.

Why nobody just reads the map

The obvious move is to look at the whole landscape and walk to the bottom. Nobody can, and nobody ever will.

Finding the height of one spot means running the machine over the examples and scoring it, which costs real time, and the spots are not few. Allow each dial a thousand positions: one dial gives you a thousand places to check, two dials a million, three a billion. A model with a billion dials has more possible settings than there are atoms in anything you could point at.

So checking them all is not slow. It is impossible, and it stays impossible whatever anyone builds. The fog is permanent.

The one thing it can feel

There is exactly one useful thing the machine can get cheaply. For each dial, it can work out whether nudging that dial up would make the wrongness rise or fall, and how sharply. Do that for every dial at once and you have which way the ground tips underneath it, and how steeply.

The slope, in other words. Its proper name is the gradient — the slope of the wrongness landscape at the exact spot the machine occupies, and nowhere else. “Descent” just means going down.

Which gives the method its name, and the name is far more forbidding than the idea: gradient descent. Feel the slope. Step downhill. Repeat.

Every step is the same small sum, applied to every dial at once:

new setting = current setting − step size × slope

Read it out loud and it says: move each dial a little way in whichever direction makes the wrongness smaller, and move it further where the ground is steeper. The minus sign is doing the real work — the slope points uphill, so you go the other way.

Standing at a setting where the wrongness is 0.73. The ground tips down towards the left, so the next step goes left.

Two things are worth noticing, and the second one matters more.

The first: it stopped honestly. Every direction it could feel led upwards, so there was nothing sensible left to do. From inside the fog, that is indistinguishable from having arrived.

The second: it came to rest at a wrongness of about 0.59, and there is ground at 0.14 further along the same dial. It never went near it, and it never could have — the only route there climbs a ridge, and no step of this method goes uphill.

A hollow like that has a name: a local minimum — the lowest point in its own neighbourhood, and not the lowest point overall. Now set the walk to begin at 0.44 and let it run, then move that one notch to 0.46 and watch. Same landscape, same method, same number of steps, and this time it ends up in the deep valley. Nothing changed except where it started.

Why this matters less than it sounds

With one dial, getting stuck is easy. There are two directions and both have to lead up. With a billion dials, all billion have to lead up before the walk is trapped, which is a great many coincidences to ask for. Large models turn out to spend their time on long gentle slopes, and on ground that falls away in some directions while rising in others, rather than in traps. That was a genuine surprise, and it is part of why training them works at all.

What this means in practice

Training is a search, not a calculation. There is no formula anyone can evaluate to get the best settings. There is a long walk, and it ends when the ground goes flat or the money runs out, whichever comes first.

That has consequences you will keep meeting. Train the same model twice from two random starting points and you get two different sets of numbers with similar scores; neither is the answer. And nobody can show that a trained model is the best possible version of itself, because nobody can see the landscape it was found in either.

One question is left wide open, and it is the loudest one: how far should each step be? Too far and you leap clean across the valley and up the far side. Too small and you are still walking when the sun goes out. That is a number a human picks rather than something the machine learns, and it is next.

For developers
for (int step = 0; step < steps; step++) {
  double slope = slopeAt(setting);
  setting -= stepSize * slope;
}

Three lines, one of which is the loop. slopeAt is the only expensive call.

It is the part that touches the training data. In a real system setting is an array of billions of numbers rather than one double, and nothing else about the shape changes.

Checkpoint

The walk stops when the ground under it goes flat. Why is that not the same thing as having found the best settings?

Show answerHide answer

Flat means every direction it can feel from where it stands leads upwards. It says nothing about ground the machine never stood on, and it only ever measures the spot it currently occupies, so the rest of the landscape was never in evidence.

Worth holding onto, because it is the honest version of what “trained” means. A finished model is a place the walk stopped, not the best place there was.
