Nobody asks you to spell Smith

The man behind the desk has half your booking typed before you have finished saying good morning. Surname?

“Kowalczyk.”

He stops. You start again — K, O, W — and you are down to one letter a second while the couple behind you shift their bags from hand to hand.

Nobody in that queue has ever been asked to spell Smith.

Both names are built out of the same twenty-six letters, and he knows all twenty-six. Difficulty was never what separated them. One of those names was something he could take in whole; the other had to be assembled out of parts.

The promise from two topics ago

The unit on the map of meanings said word on nearly every line, and then owned up in a box at the side that word was standing in for something it would name later — something that would change what gets a position on the map.

This is that. A language model does not read words. It does not read letters either. It reads chunks from a list that was settled before the model was trained: some of them whole words, most of them fragments of longer ones, and a fair few that are a piece of punctuation and nothing else.

The chunks have a name, and you have been paying for them for years. They are called tokens, and the thing that does the cutting is a tokenizer.

Nothing in the previous unit breaks. Each token gets a position, distance between positions still means similarity, and everything a model does is still arithmetic on that map. The only correction is about what is standing at each place on it.

The two obvious answers, and what is wrong with each

The machine needs a finite list of things it can hold positions for. Only two lists suggest themselves, and both have been tried properly.

One entry per word. Sentences come out pleasantly short, and each entry carries a decent amount of meaning on its own. The trouble is that the list has to end somewhere and language does not. A typo, a surname, a new brand, a hashtag, a chemical, a word coined last Tuesday — every one of those is not merely awkward for a word-list, it is a hole. There is no entry, so there is no position, so there is nothing for the arithmetic to work on. The machine does not do badly with the word; it receives no word at all.

One entry per letter. Now nothing can ever be unknown, because everything anyone types is made of letters that are already on the list. But a sentence becomes four or five times longer as a sequence, and length is the expensive dimension — the machinery that reads a sequence pays for every extra step in it, sometimes steeply. There is a second cost too: a lone letter means almost nothing, so the machine has to rebuild every word from its letters every single time it meets one.

So: a list that ends, which cannot cope with the new, or a list that never ends, which cannot cope with the long.

The way out is to stop insisting that the pieces be either. Let the list hold chunks — whole words where a word is common enough to deserve one, fragments otherwise — and both problems shrink at once. Sequences stay short, because common text is mostly common chunks. And nothing is ever unknown, because anything unfamiliar simply comes apart into smaller pieces, and at the very bottom the pieces are single characters, which are all on the list.

The unfamiliar thing does not fail. It just costs more.

Nobody wrote the list

The obvious guess is that a team of linguists sat down and decided which chunks were worth having. Nothing of the sort happens. The list is built by counting, by a procedure short enough to describe in one paragraph.

Start with a list holding nothing but single characters. Read a mountain of text, and find which two neighbouring pieces turn up side by side more often than any other pair. Glue that pair together into one new piece, and add it to the list. Then do it again, with the new piece now available to be glued to something else. And again, and again, about a hundred thousand times.

Watch what that produces. t and h go together first, because English cannot get through a line without them. Some rounds later th and e are glued, and now the is a single piece. Endings like ing and tion earn their own entries, because they turn up on the end of thousands of different words that have nothing else in common. By the time the counting stops, a common word is a single piece and an uncommon one is still in fragments, and nobody decided which was which. Frequency decided, and frequency is a fact about the text that happened to be fed in.

Two consequences worth carrying.

The list is finished before training starts, and it never changes afterwards. A model released three years ago is still cutting up today’s words with a list built from text that predates them.

And the list is a claim about which language matters. Not an opinion anybody held — a by-product of what was in the pile.

The last preset puts English and a Hindi greeting side by side: hello is five characters and four pieces, and नमस्ते is six characters and eighteen. Not eighteen letters. Eighteen boxes with no letters in them at all, which is the last of the three things below and the one with consequences.

Three things this explains

Why the bill is in tokens

Every commercial model charges by the token, both for what you send and for what comes back, and every model has a ceiling on how many tokens it can hold in mind at once. Neither number is in words, and this is why: the token is the unit of work. The machine does one pass of its arithmetic per token, so the token is what actually costs something to produce.

It also means the same idea has different prices depending on how you phrase it. Ordinary English runs at about four characters to the token, which makes a hundred words somewhere near a hundred and thirty tokens. Code, long identifiers, tables of figures and unusual names all run denser than that, so a document full of them costs more than its word count suggests.

Why it cannot count the letters in a word

Ask a model how many r’s are in embarrassment and it will often get it wrong, which is startling in something that has just written you a competent paragraph about the word’s etymology.

It is not a reasoning failure. It is that the letters were never delivered. Cut that word up in the panel and thirteen letters arrive as eight pieces, and the two r’s end up in different ones — one of them fused to the a in front of it. The machine is not being asked to count letters it can see. It is being asked to count letters inside chunks it has only ever met whole.

Reading has the same blind spot in it. You are not currently seeing the individual letters of these words; you are seeing shapes you recognise, which is why proofreading your own writing is so hard and why a missing letter in a familiar word slides straight past. Ask someone to say how many s sounds are in a word they have just read fluently and they slow right down. Same problem, same cause.

This is also why a model can be brilliant at spelling in one breath and hopeless at it in the next. Spelling that it has read about, spelling written out in the text it learned from, it knows. Spelling it would have to work out by inspecting its own input, it cannot do, because the input has no letters in it.

Why some languages cost several times more

Here is the one that matters most, and it follows directly from where the list came from.

The pile of text used to build the list was mostly English, along with a handful of other languages that have a lot of material on the internet. So the glueing loop spent its hundred thousand rounds where the text was: on English fragments, English endings, English whole words. Languages that were thinly represented got few glues, or none.

A language with no glues does not get an error. It falls all the way to the bottom of the list — to the raw numbers a computer uses to store characters, two, three or four of them per character. That is what the boxes of E0 and A4 in the panel are. Not letters. Not even parts of letters. The plumbing underneath the letters.

The result is that the same sentence, saying the same thing, costs several times more in some languages than in English. Not because the sentence is longer or the language is more complicated, but because of what happened to be in a pile of text before anyone typed anything.

What that costs, and who pays it

Three consequences, and none of them is hypothetical.

You pay more. Where billing is per token, the same conversation in Hindi, Tamil, Burmese or Amharic can cost a multiple of what it costs in English — for identical content, from the same customer, on the same day.

You get less. Every model has a ceiling on how many tokens it can hold at once. A language that costs four tokens where English costs one has, in effect, a context window four times smaller: shorter documents, fewer turns of conversation, earlier truncation.

And it is likely to work less well. Every token is a step of arithmetic, so a language shattered into fragments gives the machine longer, thinner, more fragmentary sequences to learn from — on top of having had less text in the first place.

None of this was aimed at anyone. It is the arithmetic consequence of building a list by counting, using whatever text was to hand. It is also fixable: newer vocabularies are noticeably fairer than older ones, because people measured the gap first and then went and closed some of it. That is usually what it takes.

Where it sits in the machine

Tokenization is the first thing that happens to your text and the last thing that happens to the model’s, and both directions are invisible to you.

Your sentence is cut into tokens. Each token is looked up in the list to get its row number, and that row number fetches its position — the embedding from the previous unit. From there on, nothing in the machine is text; it is all arithmetic on positions. At the far end the model produces one token at a time, each one looked up and glued back into characters, which is why a response appears in little bursts rather than word by word.

That is the whole of the interface between language and machinery. Everything else in this Part happens on the far side of it.

For developers
int cost = tokenizer.encode(prompt).size();   // not prompt.length()

Whatever you were about to count instead is wrong. Characters are wrong, split(" ") is wrong, and a fixed divisor is wrong for anything that is not ordinary prose. There is also no general answer: the count depends on which vocabulary this model was built with, so the tokenizer you call has to be that model’s own.

Checkpoint

A support tool sends the same 200-word message to a model, once in English and once in Hindi. The Hindi version costs four times as much and gets cut off before the end. Nobody changed the message, the model or the code. Where did the difference come from, and why can the team not fix it by writing shorter Hindi?

Show answerHide answer

From the list, which was built long before either message existed. The glueing procedure spent its rounds wherever the text it was fed had the most material, and that text was mostly English. Hindi got few chunks or none, so its characters fall to the bottom of the list and arrive as several raw numbers each. Four times the tokens, for the same meaning.

Shortening the Hindi helps in exact proportion and does not close the gap, because the gap is not in the message. It is a property of the vocabulary, which was fixed before training and cannot be changed without training a new model. The team’s real options are to budget differently for that language, or to choose a model whose vocabulary was built with it in mind.

Worth holding on to as a shape. When a system treats two groups differently and nobody wrote a rule that does, the cause is usually something that was counted much earlier, by a procedure that was only ever asked what was most common.
