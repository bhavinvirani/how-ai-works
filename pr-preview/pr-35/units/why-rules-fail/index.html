Why rules run out

It is 1998 and you have been handed a job. Junk email is drowning your company, and you have been asked to stop it. You are a good programmer. A computer does exactly what you tell it, so you sit down to tell it what junk looks like.

Junk shouts in capitals. That is a rule. Junk is full of exclamation marks — another rule. Junk says “free”, junk says “urgent”, junk wants you to click something. Five rules, an afternoon’s work, and the flood stops.

For about a week.

Then the senders notice. The capitals become lowercase. “Free” becomes “no charge”. Meanwhile your colleague misses a real invoice, because it said URGENT in the subject line and your rule threw it away. So you add an exception. Then an exception to the exception. Six months later you are maintaining four hundred rules that argue with each other, and the junk is still arriving.

The thing you were never able to write down

Try it yourself. Below is a week of email and the five rules you started with. Find a set that keeps every real message and blocks every junk one. It is genuinely possible. Then switch to next week’s inbox, where nothing has changed except the wording, and watch your answer come apart.

The instrument is not being unfair to you. It searches all thirty-two possible combinations of those five rules and reports the best score any of them can reach. In the second inbox, the answer is that none of them work.

That is the first important idea on this site, so it is worth saying plainly: you were not failing at the task. The task was impossible as stated.

A job a child can do and nobody can explain

Junk email is the small version. Here is the large one.

You can recognise your mother’s face in about a tenth of a second. In a crowd, at an angle, in bad light, twenty years older than the last time you saw her. Now write down the rule you just used.

You cannot. Not because you are not clever enough, but because you never had access to it. Whatever your brain did, it did without telling you, and no amount of introspection produces the instructions. This is true of almost everything people are effortlessly good at: recognising a voice on the phone, catching a ball, hearing that a sentence sounds wrong, knowing a photo looks like a tumour.

So there are two piles of problems. One pile you can finish: sorting a list, calculating tax, checking a password length. You state the rule, the machine follows it, and it works forever.

The other pile has no rule to state. Not a rule that is hard to write — a rule that does not exist in any form anyone can get at. And unfortunately this second pile contains most of the things we would actually like computers to do.

The move that got us out

For decades, the second pile was simply where computing stopped. What changed was not a better rule. It was giving up on rules.

The idea is this. Instead of describing the job, collect thousands of examples of it being done correctly — thousands of messages already marked junk or not, thousands of photos already labelled. Then build a machine with a large number of adjustable settings, and adjust them, over and over, until the machine’s answers stop disagreeing with the examples.

Nobody ever writes the rule. The rule ends up buried in the settings, in a form no person could read. What you get is a machine that behaves as though it knows something you were never able to say.

That trade is the whole subject. Everything else — every layer, every model, every chatbot — is a consequence of taking it seriously.

For developers
boolean isJunk(Message m) {
  if (m.subject().matches(".*\\b[A-Z]{4,}\\b.*")) return true;
  if (m.subject().contains("free")) return true;
  if (m.subject().contains("urgent")) return true;
  // ...and 397 more, each added the week something got through
  return false;
}

You have written this method. Every new rule turns a previously green test red, and every fix for that test lets something else through. The file only grows. No refactor helps, because the problem is not that the code is badly organised. It is that the rule you are trying to express does not exist.

Where you will meet this again

Every time a model does something impressive and then something stupid, it is worth coming back here. These systems were never given rules. They were shown examples and tuned until they agreed with them, so they are excellent at things that look like their examples, and unreliable in exactly the places where the rule you assumed they had does not exist.

Checkpoint

Your five rules score perfectly on this week's mail. Why is that not evidence that the rules are correct?

Show answerHide answer

Because the score only tells you the rules fit the examples in front of you. It says nothing about mail that has not arrived yet, and the senders adjust. A rule that fits this week’s inbox perfectly can fail completely next week without a single character of it changing.

Keep hold of this. The gap between “scores well on what I have” and “works on what comes next” turns out to be the central difficulty in the whole field.
