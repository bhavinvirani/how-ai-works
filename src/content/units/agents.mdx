Two notes on the kitchen table for whoever is house-sitting.

The first one you wrote a month ago, and it is a list. _Water the plants on
Tuesday. Bins out on Thursday night. Lock the back door._ Nothing on it depends
on anything else, and you knew every step of it before you left.

The second is one line. _If anything goes wrong with the boiler, sort it out._

You could not have turned the second note into a list, and it is not because you
were in a hurry. The first thing to do is find out what has actually gone wrong,
and everything after that depends on what the first thing turns up. A dead pilot
light and a dripping valve have nothing in common except the ten seconds it
takes to tell them apart. There is no sequence to write down, because the
sequence does not exist until somebody is standing in front of the boiler.

So the second note does something the first one does not. It hands over the
decision.

Give a model a goal, give it some tools, and let it choose its own next move,
and what you have is an **agent**.

## The mechanism is smaller than the word

The word has picked up a great deal of weight. It gets used for things that
plan, things that remember, things advertised as some distance along the road to
a colleague. Underneath all of it is a loop with five steps, and you have already
met four of them.

1. The model is handed the goal, a description of every tool it may ask for, and
   everything that has happened so far.
2. It writes one thing: either the name of a tool with some arguments, or an
   answer.
3. If it named a tool, your code runs it — or refuses to.
4. Whatever came back is added to the end of everything that has happened so
   far.
5. Go back to step one.

The loop stops when step two produces an answer instead of a tool name, or when
you stop it.

That is the entire mechanism. There is no planner in there, no memory, no
separate part that decides. Nothing whatever was added to the model: it is the
same next-piece scorer from the units on large language models, doing the only
thing it has ever done.

## What actually changed

Two units ago you were left with a relay. The model writes the name of a
function, your code decides whether to run it, and the result comes back as
text. Every one of the five steps above is that relay.

Exactly one thing is different, and it is step two.

In ordinary software you write the order the steps run in. Programmers call it
the **control flow** — the path execution takes through your program. You wrote
it, it is sitting in the file, and you can read it before anything runs.

In an agent, the control flow does not exist until it runs. It is produced one
step at a time, by a machine that scores the next chunk of text.

That sentence is the unit. Everything below it is a consequence.

## Why anyone hands the decision over

It is worth being plain about what is bought here, because the answer is not
intelligence.

What you get is a system that can handle a job whose steps you could not have
written down. Not a harder job — a job of an unknown shape. The bill
investigation in the panel below is ordinary work that any competent person
could do, and it is still not something you can write as a sequence, because the
second step depends on what the first one found.

You could write it as a sequence. It would be a decision tree, you would have to
think of every branch in advance, and the day a meter turns out to be missing a
reading you would discover which branch you forgot. That is the trade: an agent
is what you reach for when enumerating the branches is harder than doing the job.

And it is a trade with a sting in it. A machine that can take a step you did not
think of can take a step you would not have allowed.

## One goal, three runs

The panel below holds one goal, one set of tools and one run. Step it through to
the end. Then change the single line that says what the meter came back with,
and read it again from the top.

One thing to be straight about before you do. The trace is written down rather
than produced by a model — a real run words things differently every time, and
its context would be tens of thousands of tokens rather than hundreds. What is
not simplified is the shape: one turn, one choice, one result, and the result
going into the pile that the next turn has to read. The arithmetic between those
numbers is the arithmetic of the real thing.

Press the plus until the run stops. On the first setting — the meter says usage
really did double — it takes five turns. It reads the bill, reads the meter,
checks the equipment log, checks the daily pattern, and answers.

The turn worth slowing down for is the fourth. Having found that a second
freezer was delivered on the 3rd, it does not stop there. It goes and checks
whether the extra use shows up on Sundays, when the shop is shut. Nobody wrote
that step, and nobody could have: it exists because a delivery date on its own is
not proof, and that is the kind of thing the writing it was scored on is full of.

Now set the meter to _usage unchanged_.

The first two turns are identical. Same reasoning, same tool, same call — because
at that point nothing had come back for a plan to depend on. From the third turn
the two runs have nothing in common at all. This one never opens the equipment
log, because there is nothing in the shop to look for. It goes to the tariff,
finds the fixed deal ended on 31 March, checks what else is on offer, checks
whether leaving the current rate costs anything, and asks to switch the account.
Six turns instead of five, and it ends by stopping instead of by answering.

It stops because `switch` is on a list of tools your code will not run without a
person saying yes. Read that carefully, because it is easy to give the model
credit for it. The model did not decide a tariff change was serious. It asked for
the switch in exactly the way it asked for everything else, and something outside
it declined.

### The run where nothing looks wrong

Set the meter to _no reading_.

What comes back is not an error. It is an ordinary, well-formed sentence: there
is no reading recorded for April, and the April bill is an estimate.

Watch what the third turn does with it. The reasoning is sound — with no April
figure it cannot compare the two months, so it will fetch the nearest actual
figure there is, which is March. Any of us would do that.

Read the fourth turn and it has quietly become something else. _March is what I
have, and nothing suggests April was any different._

Nothing suggests April was any different because nothing was ever looked at. An
absence has been read as evidence, in one clause, in the middle of a chain of
otherwise good reasoning. From that point the run is not investigating. It is
building a case.

So it sends an email, and the panel breaks the letter into its three separate
statements. _March usage was 1,940 kWh_ rests on something that came back.
_April's usage was no different from March's_ rests on nothing. _The April
estimate of £838 is far too high_ rests on nothing.

Two out of three, in a letter that has gone.

Notice what did not happen. Nothing paused. `email` is not on the held list;
`switch` is. A person was asked before a tariff change that could have been
changed straight back, and nobody was asked before an accusation went to the
supplier. That list is not a judgement about what is serious — it is a list, and
this one has the wrong thing on it.

Now hold the three runs beside each other. Same goal, same tools, same first two
turns to the word. Five turns, six turns, four turns. Answered, stopped, acted.
And one conclusion in three with nothing under it. Nothing chose between those
three futures except one line of text that came back on turn two.

## Every result is another thing to re-read

Look at the number printed against each turn: how much text the model has to
read on that pass.

It goes up every turn and never down, because nothing is ever taken out. The
model's own line goes in, the tool result goes in, and the next pass reads the
lot from the beginning — for the same reason a model re-reads everything it has
written before adding one more word.

The six-turn run starts at 320 tokens on the first turn and reaches 875 on the
sixth, with 1,005 sitting there when it stops. That is the context window from
the previous unit, being spent by something that decides for itself how many
times to spend it.

Add the turns up and the number that surprises people appears. Across that one
run the model read 3,580 tokens — about eleven times what the same goal would
have cost asked once and answered once. Six turns, eleven times the reading,
because every turn re-reads everything the turns before it produced.

That is the honest shape of what an agent costs. It is not that the answers are
longer. It is that the same growing pile gets read over and over, and how many
times is decided at run time, by the thing doing the reading.

<Aside title="It did not decide anything, in the sense you mean">
  It is very hard to read that trace without hearing somebody think. The lines
  are in the first person, they give reasons, they change course.

Every one of them is the next-piece loop. The model was handed a pile of text
ending in a tool result and produced the most plausible continuation, which —
because of how it was tuned — is a short line of reasoning followed by a tool
name. It is writing what a competent person's next line would look like.

That is not nothing. Writing the plausible next line is exactly how it gets the
Sunday check right. But the word _decide_ is doing work here that it does not do
anywhere else. Nothing weighed the options. Nothing considered whether sending
the email was wise. A continuation scored highest, and your code ran it.

</Aside>

## Small mistakes do not stay small

One more consequence, and it is the one that settles whether an agent is a good
idea for a particular job.

Suppose each turn goes right nine times in ten, which is generous for anything
with a model in it. Six turns of that, and the run as a whole is right about half
the time:

$$
0.9^{6} \approx 0.53
$$

Nine chances in ten, six times over, comes out barely better than a coin. Real
runs are longer than six turns, and it gets worse fast — twenty turns at the same
rate is about one run in eight.

That arithmetic is the optimistic version, and it is worth knowing why. It treats
the turns as independent, like six separate coins. They are not. Every result
goes into the pile the next turn reads, so a turn that goes wrong does not merely
fail — it changes what the next turn is choosing between. The third run in the
panel is precisely this. Turn three was a sensible move. Turn four is where it
becomes a mistake, and turn four is also the one that puts it in the post.

That is the difference between an agent going wrong and ordinary software going
wrong. Buggy software does the wrong thing, and then does the next thing from the
same place regardless. An agent that goes wrong builds on it.

## What makes one workable

None of this makes agents a bad idea. It makes them a thing with a known shape,
and every practice that works comes from the same place: the trouble is the
choice, so the answer is always to hand over less of it.

**Give it fewer tools, and narrower ones.** Every tool is one more thing that can
be chosen on every turn. A tool that can read one table is a tool that cannot
delete a different one. Most of what an agent needs is reading, and reading has
no incident attached to it.

**Cap the turns.** The loop has no natural end — step five goes back to step one,
and nothing inside it notices that it is going round. A run that hits the cap has
failed, which is fine and cheap. A run with no cap has a bill and a blast radius
that nobody can state in advance.

**Refuse to run anything you cannot undo.** Not flag it, not log it. Refuse it,
in your code, against a list you wrote, until a person says yes. And spend real
time on that list, because the panel shows you what a wrong one looks like. It is
not enough for the gate to exist. It has to have the right tools behind it, and
the model will not tell you which those are.

**Make a failed run loud.** An agent that goes wrong does not crash. It produces
a confident, well-written account of what it did, in which every intermediate
step reads exactly like the steps of a run that worked. If nothing in your system
compares what it claimed against what actually came back, nothing will notice.

And one question before any of that. Which parts of this job do you genuinely not
know the shape of in advance? Usually it is one or two, and the rest is a
sequence you could write out this afternoon. A program that runs a fixed sequence
and calls a model at the two points needing judgement is cheaper, easier to test
and far easier to reason about than a loop that decides everything. Hand the
decision over where you have to, and not an inch further.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    List<Message> context = List.of(goal, toolCatalogue);

    for (int turn = 0; turn < MAX_TURNS; turn++) {   // the cap is not optional
      Reply reply = model.next(context);             // one pass over ALL of it

      if (reply.isAnswer()) return reply.text();

      ToolCall call = reply.call();
      if (HELD.contains(call.name())) {              // your list, written first
        return Result.awaitingApproval(call);
      }

      context = append(context, reply, tools.run(call));
    }
    ```

    A dozen lines, and nothing is missing. Everything people attribute to "the
    agent" is either `model.next` — the same call you would make for a one-shot
    question — or the loop around it.

    Two of those lines are the whole of your control. `MAX_TURNS` is the only
    thing standing between you and an unbounded bill, and it belongs in the
    `for` rather than in a check somewhere inside. `HELD` is a list a person
    wrote before the run started: nothing in `reply` tells you how consequential
    a call is, and asking the model to rate its own call is asking the same
    next-piece scorer that produced it.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    context = [goal, tool_catalogue]

    for _ in range(MAX_TURNS):
        reply = model.next(context)

        if reply.answer is not None:
            return reply.answer
        if reply.call.name in HELD:
            return awaiting_approval(reply.call)

        context += [reply, tools.run(reply.call)]
    ```

    `context += ...` is where the cost lives. The list only ever grows, and
    `model.next` reads all of it every time round the loop, so the bill for a
    run goes up faster than the number of turns does. Providers cache the
    unchanged front of that pile and charge less for it, which helps a great
    deal and changes none of the shape.

    `tools.run` is the other line worth staring at. Every permission the agent
    has is a permission that function has — a database handle, an API key, an
    outbox. The model holds none of its own. Whatever you would not give a
    stranger with a plausible manner, do not put behind that call.

<Checkpoint question="A team ships an agent that handles billing questions. It has five read-only tools and one that issues a refund, and any refund over £50 is held for a person to approve. It tests beautifully. In its first live week it issues four hundred refunds of under £50 to customers who were owed nothing, each with a short and plausible explanation attached, and nothing anywhere errors. Explain how a system with a working approval gate produced that, and say what you would change.">
  The gate worked exactly as written, and it was never the protection anybody
  thought it was. It is a rule about an amount, not about whether a refund is
  deserved — and nothing in the loop ever asks whether a refund is deserved,
  because there is nothing in the loop capable of asking. Every refund the model
  reached for under the threshold was, as far as the system was concerned,
  authorised in advance.

The four hundred is the compounding. Somewhere early in each run a turn read
something ambiguous and produced a line concluding that the customer was owed
money. That line then went into the context, and the turn after it was not
judging the case afresh — it was continuing a chain in which the matter was
already settled. The refund is not the mistake. It is the fourth or fifth thing
built on the mistake.

"Nothing anywhere errors" is not reassurance, it is the symptom. A failed agent
run produces a confident account of itself, and four hundred plausible
explanations is precisely what four hundred failures look like from the outside.

What to change, in order of how much it buys. Take the refund out of the loop
entirely: let the model do the judgement and have ordinary software issue the
money, once, against a rule you wrote and a record you can check. Cap the turns.
Move the gate from the amount to the action, because the thing you cannot undo is
paying out, not paying out more than fifty pounds. And stop deciding it works by
reading traces — score it against a fixed set of past cases where you already
know who was owed what.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. An approval gate is a claim about tools, never
about intentions. If the outcome you are afraid of is reachable through a call
that is on nobody's list, the gate is decoration.

</Checkpoint>
