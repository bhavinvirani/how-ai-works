Three things happened to you last month, and all three were reported as
artificial intelligence.

Your card was declined at a petrol station, because something at your bank
decided that purchase did not look like you. Your phone gathered every photo of
your dog into an album, without being asked. And a chatbot wrote a birthday
message for your sister that mentioned a nephew you do not have.

One word, three machines. They were built in different decades, out of different
materials, and they go wrong in completely different ways. The shared name tells
you none of that — which is a shame, because the third one going wrong was
entirely predictable.

## Four circles, not four things

Draw a very large circle. Into it goes any machine doing a job that, if a person
did it, you would call thinking. That is a loose and generous idea, and a chess
program from 1985 belongs in it. Every move that program considered came from
strategy a human being had written down by hand, line by line. It beat strong
players. It never learned a thing in its life.

That outer circle is what **artificial intelligence** means, and what it has
always meant. Nothing in it promises learning.

Now draw a smaller circle inside. Only one kind of machine gets in: the ones
nobody could write the rule for, which were shown examples instead and had their
dials adjusted until they stopped disagreeing. The fraud check at your bank lives
here. Nobody described a stolen card; somebody showed a machine several million
transactions and let it settle. **Machine learning** is the name of this inner
circle. Every machine-learning system is artificial intelligence — and for most
of the history of the field, almost no artificial intelligence was machine
learning.

Smaller again. Some learning machines have two dials; the house-price machine you
turned had exactly two. Others have their dials arranged in layers, where what
comes out of one layer goes into the next, dozens of layers deep. That is
**deep learning**, and "deep" means nothing more mysterious than "a lot of
layers". This is the circle where photo recognition and speech transcription
abruptly started working, around 2012, after thirty years of not working.

One more, the smallest. Most learning machines choose: fraud or not fraud, dog or
cat, junk or real. A smaller group produces instead — a sentence, a picture, a
tune, a paragraph of code, none of which existed a second earlier.
**Generative AI** is that innermost circle, and it is where every chatbot and
every image generator sits.

Nothing lands in an inner circle without also being in every circle around it. A
chatbot is generative AI, and deep learning, and machine learning, and artificial
intelligence, all at once — the way a terrier is also a dog, a mammal and an
animal. There is nothing to choose between.

## The ring predicts the failure

This would all be pedantry if it did not tell you anything. What it tells you is
how the thing in front of you breaks.

A machine that follows written rules fails at the edge of what was written, and
nowhere else. Hand it the situation nobody anticipated and it does something
daft — but the daftness has an address. Some line, in some file, written by
somebody, can be found and changed. Brittle, and honest about being brittle.

A machine that learned from examples has no such address. It fails when the
situation stops resembling the examples it was shown — a kind of fraud that did
not exist last year, a shop it has never seen — and it fails _quietly_. It hands
back an answer in the same tone as always. There is no error message for "this is
unfamiliar to me", because nothing inside it is keeping track of what familiar
means.

Stack those dials into layers and the last of the explanation goes with them.
Nobody decided what the middle layers should pay attention to; they arrived at it
while being tuned, and it is not written in any language a person reads. So the
failures turn strange rather than merely wrong. A change to a photograph that you
would not notice can flip the answer completely, and there is nothing to open
afterwards that would tell you why.

The innermost ring adds a failure of its own, and it is the one that catches
people. A machine that chooses can only be wrong inside a fixed set of options —
ask a dog-or-cat sorter about a bicycle and it says dog, which is obvious
nonsense the moment you see it. A machine that produces has no fixed set of
options and no way to decline. Asked for something it does not have, it does not
stop, because producing is the only operation it owns. It makes something of the
right shape instead. That is where your nephew came from. Nothing malfunctioned.
The machine did the only thing it does.

And because the rings nest, the failures nest too. A chatbot inherits all four at
once: brittle at the edges of what it was tuned on, quietly wrong on anything
unfamiliar, unexplainable when it is wrong, and inventive when it should have
stopped.

## The two questions worth asking

The practical version is short. "AI-powered" is a claim about the outermost ring,
which is to say barely a claim at all — a spreadsheet with enough conditions in it
qualifies. Two questions get you somewhere.

Was the behaviour written down, or learned from examples? And does the system
choose between options, or produce new material?

The first answer tells you whether the failures have an address anyone can find.
The second tells you whether it can be wrong while sounding entirely certain.

<Aside title="The name keeps moving">
  As soon as something works reliably, people stop calling it artificial
  intelligence. Spell-check was AI. So was route-finding on a map, and the junk
  mail filter, and reading the handwriting on a cheque. None of them get the
  name now, because the name attaches to whatever is not quite working yet —
  which makes "is this AI" a question about fashion as much as about mechanism.
</Aside>

<Checkpoint question="A product page says it is 'powered by AI'. What have you actually been told — and what single question would tell you how it fails?">
  You have been told it sits in the outermost ring, which almost any software
  with a decision in it can claim. The question worth asking is whether the
  behaviour was written down or learned from examples.

If it was written down, the failures have a line number and somebody can change
that line. If it was learned, there is no line — the behaviour lives in the
numbers, the failures arrive as confident wrong answers on anything unlike the
examples, and what it was trained on becomes the only question carrying real
information.

</Checkpoint>
