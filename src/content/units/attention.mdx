Halfway down the second page of a tenancy agreement:

_The tenant may not sublet the property without written consent, and she must
give thirty days' notice before leaving._

**She.** You stopped for perhaps a quarter of a second, went back up the line,
found _the tenant_, and carried on reading.

Now notice what you did not do. You did not start the sentence again from the
top. You did not go rummaging in a summary of everything you had read so far,
hoping the answer was still somewhere in it. You asked one question — who is
_she_? One word answered it. Everything else in the sentence stayed
exactly where it was.

A machine that reads one word at a time cannot work like that. It carries a
single running summary and rewrites it at every step, so a word twenty places
back is not something it can go and look at. It is something it had to have
remembered, and by then the remembering has usually been written over.

The idea in this unit is almost rude in its simplicity. Stop making the words
take turns. Let every word ask its own question of every other word, all at the
same moment, and let each one rebuild its meaning out of whichever words
answered. That is **attention**, and nearly every language model in use today is
built out of it.

## Three things every word has to be able to make

Picture a large room at a conference. Two hundred people, and everyone is
wearing a badge with a few words on it about what they do.

You want to know something specific, so you say what you are after. You have no
idea who can help. What you actually do is read badges — not faces, not
histories, just the short line each person has chosen to advertise about
themselves — and you steer towards the ones that look like a match. Then you
listen to what those people tell you, which is a great deal more than what fitted
on a badge.

Three separate things are in play there, and keeping them apart is most of this
unit. What you asked for. What each person advertised. What each person actually
had to say.

Attention gives every word all three. A word arrives inside the machine as a list
of numbers standing for its meaning, and the model squeezes that same list three
different ways to produce:

- a **question** — what this word wants to know about the rest of the sentence
- a **label** — what this word is advertising about itself
- its **contents** — what it hands over to anything that picks it

All three are made from the same word, and none of them is the word. The label is
short and public, built to be matched against; the contents are the substantial
part, and nobody looks at them until the matching is over. That separation is not
decoration. It is what lets a word advertise one thing and deliver another,
exactly like a badge.

The formal names are **query**, **key** and **value**, and you will meet them
written as Q, K and V in every paper and every codebase. Question, label,
contents is what they mean.

## Every question, against every label

Now the arithmetic, which is smaller than you would expect.

Take one word, say _she_, and its question. Compare that question against the
label of every word in the sentence, its own included. Each comparison produces a
single number saying how well that question and that label go together.

Nine words, so nine numbers for _she_. Then the same for every other word: nine
questions, each meeting nine labels, eighty-one numbers in all. No word waits for
any other to finish, because no word needs anything another word has not already
got.

Those raw numbers then get divided by one fixed amount before anything else
happens — the square root of how many numbers each question is made of. Nothing
deep is going on there. A question built from sixty-four numbers produces larger
comparisons than one built from four, purely because more things are being added
up, and the division cancels that out so the next step behaves the same whatever
size the model is.

## A budget, not a set of dials

Scores on no particular scale are not usable, so they go through one more step.
**Softmax** takes any row of numbers and turns it into a row of shares: all
positive, the largest still the largest, and adding up to exactly one.

That last property is the most consequential fact in this unit, and it is easy to
read straight past.

Every word gets one unit of attention. Not one unit per relationship — one unit,
full stop, to be divided among the entire sentence. A word cannot decide that
three other words are all extremely relevant and lean hard on all three for
nothing. Leaning harder on one word means, as a matter of arithmetic, leaning
less on everything else.

Attention is a budget, not a set of independent dials. Hold on to that: a
surprising amount of what follows is about giving a model more budgets to spend
rather than a larger one.

After that, the last step is the easy one. The word's new meaning is the mix — a
large share of one word's contents, a smaller share of another's, a scrap of each
of the rest. The word has been rebuilt out of the sentence around it.

## Nine words, and a pronoun with a question

Below is one sentence, _The student carried the box because she was strong_,
and one round of attention over it. Choosing a word chooses the word doing the
asking; the number printed under each of the others is the share it spent there.

<AttentionMap client:visible />

It opens on _she_, asking who it stands for. Forty-three per cent of everything
_she_ has goes to _student_. Sixteen per cent goes to _box_, which is a
reasonable wrong answer rather than a random one — a box is at least the kind of
thing a pronoun could be standing for. The other seven words get about six per
cent each, which is this arithmetic's way of saying it found nothing there.

Look at where _student_ sits. Five words earlier, with four words in between,
and one of those in-between words, _because_, is pressed right up against _she_
and receives exactly the same six per cent as _The_ at the very start of the
sentence and _strong_ at the very end. Distance contributed nothing at all. It is
not that distance was overcome; there is nowhere in the arithmetic for distance
to enter.

Then read the strip underneath. _she_ arrived carrying one thing — it is a
pointing word, and that is the whole of it — and it leaves carrying 0.43 of a
person and 0.29 of a thing. The pronoun has been resolved, and nothing anywhere
in that process looked up a rule about pronouns.

Now choose **because**. Eleven per cent to every word in the sentence, itself
included, and the strip reports that it now means the plain average of the whole
sentence, which is a precise way of saying it learned nothing. That is not the
instrument giving up. _because_ carries none of the meaning these questions are
asking about, so its question matches every label equally badly, and a row of
equal scores comes out of softmax as a row of equal shares. Nobody assigned that
flat row. It was computed, by exactly the arithmetic that produced the
interesting one, and it is the best evidence on this page that none of these
answers were written down in advance.

Finally, change the question at the bottom and watch the same nine words
rearrange themselves. Ask who is doing this, and _carried_ leans on _student_.
Ask what a word is caught up in, and _box_ leans on _carried_. The sentence did
not change. The question did.

## What is real here, and what is stage scenery

One thing has to be said plainly, and this is the unit that owes it.

The arithmetic in that panel is the real thing. A score for every ordered pair of
words, divided by the square root of the width, pushed through softmax so each
row sums to one, then spent on the other words' contents. That is attention as
it runs inside a production model, not an impression of it. Every number on this
page came out of it.

What is staged is where the scores come from. In a real model nobody writes down
that a pronoun should go looking for a person. The three squeezes that turn a
word into a question, a label and its contents are made of numbers that are
**learned** — nudged a few million times towards whatever made the model's
answers less wrong, exactly like every other number in the machine.

So nobody decides what a round of attention ends up following, and nobody knows
in advance. Here those numbers are hand-written, so that nine words produce a
pattern legible enough to point at. That is the entire difference, and it matters
in one specific way: a real round of attention is usually not interpretable at
all. Researchers do occasionally find one doing something nameable, and those get
written up precisely because they are unusual. The honest picture is thousands of
them all doing something real, and almost none of them explicable.

Take the mechanism from that panel. Do not take the tidiness.

## Why this beat everything that came before

**Distance stopped mattering.** In a machine that reads one word at a time, a
word twenty places back has been through twenty rounds of rewriting before you
can ask about it, and what survives is a smear. Here _she_ reached _student_ in
one comparison, and it would have reached just as directly across five hundred
words. There is no fading, because there is nothing to fade through.

**And it all happens at once.** Reading in order is not only a design choice, it
is a speed limit: step two cannot begin until step one has finished, so a
thousand-word passage takes a thousand turns however much machinery you own.
Attention has no turns. Every question meets every label in the same instant,
which means the work splits cleanly across thousands of processors.

The second point is the less obvious one, and it is the one that changed the
industry. Attention did not win by understanding a single sentence better —
early on it often did not. It won because it could be trained on far more text in
the same week. Almost everything about the last few years follows from that.

<Aside title="What it costs" tone="caution">
  Every word compares itself against every word, so nine words means eighty-one
  comparisons and a thousand words means a million. Double the length of the
  text and you roughly quadruple the work, and the memory, which is usually what
  runs out first. That is why a model has a limit on how much it will read at
  once, why that limit is quoted in the specifications like a headline figure,
  and why a large slice of current research is people trying to get most of
  attention's behaviour for less than all of attention's bill.
</Aside>

## The line everybody quotes

$$
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

Read left to right, in the words this unit has been using. $Q$ is every word's
question, stacked into one grid; $K$ is every word's label; $V$ is every word's
contents. $QK^{\top}$ compares every question against every label in a single
multiplication — the whole table of eighty-one scores. $\sqrt{d_k}$ is that fixed
division, where $d_k$ is how many numbers a question is made of. $\text{softmax}$
turns each row of the table into shares adding to one. Multiplying by $V$ spends
those shares on the contents.

There is nothing in that line you have not already done by hand.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    double[][] scores  = matmul(queries, transpose(keys));
    scale(scores, 1.0 / Math.sqrt(headWidth));
    double[][] weights = softmaxRows(scores);   // every row now sums to 1
    double[][] output  = matmul(weights, values);
    ```

    The method worth naming carefully is `softmaxRows`, not `softmax`. Attention
    softmaxes each row on its own, one budget per word, and a helper that
    normalises the whole grid at once compiles, runs, and produces a model that
    never learns anything. The shapes do not protect you here; both versions are
    the same square of doubles going in and coming out.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    scores  = queries @ keys.transpose(-2, -1) / math.sqrt(head_width)
    weights = scores.softmax(dim=-1)   # every row now sums to 1
    output  = weights @ values
    ```

    Three lines, and the whole lesson is in `dim=-1`. Point the softmax at the
    wrong axis and every shape still lines up, the tensors are the same size, no
    exception is raised, and the loss simply refuses to come down. It is the
    classic first bug, and nothing except knowing what the row means will find
    it.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="A model reading a long report attaches the word she to the wrong person. A colleague suggests fixing it by making the model pay more attention to the right person. Setting aside whether that is possible, what does it cost, and where does the cost land?">
  It costs attention taken from every other word in that sentence. Each word has
  one unit to spend and no more, so raising the share on the right person
  necessarily lowers the share on everything else, including words the model was
  relying on. It is a trade, never an addition.

Worth keeping, because it generalises well beyond this unit. Any time somebody
says a model should focus more on something, the honest version of that sentence
has a second half, and less on something else. There is no setting anywhere in
the machine that adds attention.
