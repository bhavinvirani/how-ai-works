The machine has just said _dog_, and the photograph was of a cat.

Inside it are seventy billion adjustable numbers. Somewhere among them is the
reason it said that, and the repair is to move some of them, but which ones,
which way, and how far each? A wrong answer says none of that. It is one word.

There is an obvious way to find out, and it genuinely works. Take the first of
those seventy billion numbers. Nudge it up a hair, then run the entire machine
again on the same photograph. If the answer came out a little less wrong, that
number should go up; if it came out worse, down; and how far the answer moved
tells you how hard to push. Put it back. Start on the second number.

That is seventy billion complete runs of the machine to learn what to do about
one photograph. Then the next photograph. It is not a job you set going and
come back to after lunch. It is a job nobody alive would see finish.

## Everything downstream decides how much you matter

Three things stand between a song and the room, and every one of them has a
volume control on it. The phone, the app playing through it, and the speaker on
the shelf.

Turn the phone's volume up by two notches. How much louder does the room get?

There is no answer to that without knowing the other two. With the speaker's
dial near the top, two notches is a jolt that makes people look up. With the
speaker almost off, the same two notches on the same phone change nothing
anybody can hear, because whatever they add gets multiplied by something close
to zero on its way out.

So the effect of moving one control is not a fact about that control. It is
what the move does where it sits, multiplied by everything standing between it
and the sound.

A machine built out of layers has exactly that shape, with rather more controls
and rather more stages. Each dial does one small local thing, it multiplies
one number on its way into the next layer, and then every layer after it
multiplies the result again. A dial near the input can be the most important
number in the machine or completely beside the point, and which of those it is
depends on numbers it never touches.

That is why you cannot look at a dial and know what to do with it. Nobody can,
and neither can the machine.

## The one number that starts it off

Go to the end of the machine instead. It is the only place where anything is
known for certain: the answer came out, the right answer is sitting next to it,
so you know exactly how wrong the machine was and in which direction. One
number, and it took no work at all to get.

Put that together with the volume controls and the whole method falls out:

**a dial's share of the blame = how much a nudge to it moves the answer × how
wrong the answer was**

Read out loud: a dial that swings the answer a long way is a large part of why
the answer is wrong, and the wronger the answer, the more everyone has to move.
The second half of that multiplication is a single number the entire machine
shares. Only the first half differs from dial to dial, and the first half is
precisely the thing that looked unaffordable.

Here is the move that makes it cheap, and it is worth reading twice.

The sweep never works out a dial's effect from scratch. It works out one number
per _neuron_: for each neuron in a layer, how much the final answer would change
if that neuron's output changed by one. Once a neuron has that number, every
dial arriving at it gets its own answer immediately — whatever that dial does
locally, times the neuron's number. And every neuron in the layer below is
handed its own number the same way, by the neurons it feeds.

One number per neuron, one layer at a time, from the end towards the front. By
the time it arrives at the input, every dial in the machine has been told which
way to move and how far, and the machine was never run again.

That is **backpropagation** — propagating, meaning spreading, the error
backwards through the layers. What it produces is the slope of the wrongness
landscape you walked down in gradient descent: backpropagation is where that
slope actually comes from, and the step afterwards is somebody else's job.

## Hand out the blame yourself

That picture is honest and far too big to check by hand. So here is the same
shape shrunk until every number fits on one screen.

Six dials, guessing how long a bike ride takes from two facts about it: how far
it is, and how hilly. Two numbers go in on the left. Two neurons sit in the
middle, middle A and middle B, each adding up its own weighted version of
those two numbers. What the pair of them produce is combined into a guess in
minutes.

Take the eight-kilometre flat ride first. Middle A works out 18, middle B works
out 10, and the guess arrives at 38 minutes. The ride actually took 26. Twelve
minutes too long.

Before you press the button, pick a dial. Which one is about to be told to move
furthest?

<BlameFlow client:visible />

It is the dial between middle A and the guess. Turning that dial up by one adds
18 minutes to the answer, because middle A is sitting at 18 and that dial is
what middle A gets multiplied by on the way out. Eighteen minutes of movement,
on an answer that was twelve minutes out: 18 × 12 = 216, and no other product
in the machine comes to more. That single dial is handed 31% of everything the
sweep asks for.

At the bottom of the list is the dial carrying hilliness into middle A. One
notch on it moves the answer by two minutes, because the route was barely hilly
and there is almost nothing there for it to act on. 2 × 12 = 24, which is 3% of
the total and one ninth of what the top dial was told to do. Both are asked to
move. One is asked to move nine times as far.

Nothing chose that ranking. It is the product of two numbers that were lying
around anyway.

Now switch to the three-kilometre climb. Same machine, same six dials, nothing
retuned in between. The guess comes out at 39 minutes against an actual 45,
six minutes too short this time, and the largest share has moved to a
different dial altogether: the one carrying hilliness into middle B, at 29%.
That dial sits in the first layer, and it has just overtaken both of the dials
on the output.

It won because this route has a hilliness of 9 for it to act on, and because
whatever passes through middle B is doubled on its way to the guess. Plenty to
work with, multiplied by a strong onward link. On the flat ride the same dial
was near the bottom.

The direction flipped too. The guess was too short rather than too long, so
every dial is now told to go up instead of down — one sign, on one shared
number, turning the whole machine round at once.

Then there is the third ride, which is the control. The machine is nearly right
on it, three minutes out, and every share in the list shrinks to match. When
the answer is nearly right, this method says so by asking almost nobody to
move.

<Aside title="Two ways that small machine is neater than the real thing">
  Its two middle neurons pass their results straight on. A real one squashes the
  result first — clipping negatives to zero, say — which is what stops a stack
  of layers collapsing into one. That squash adds one more multiplication to
  every chain: how steep the squash was at the value it was handed. One extra
  term in the same product, worked out in the same sweep, on the way past.

And every dial here is a positive number, so all six push the guess upwards
and a guess that is too long sends all six down together. Put a minus sign on
one and that dial goes the other way while nothing else changes. Real
machines are full of negative dials; the tidiness is this example's, not the
method's.

</Aside>

## Why this was the piece that was missing

Layered machines are older than most people assume. The idea was in print in
the 1950s and worked over through the 1960s, and it went almost nowhere. What
nobody had was a way to train one, and the obstacle was exactly the question
this unit opened with: past the first layer, there is no local way to tell what
a dial did to an answer produced three layers later.

So count the two routes to the same six numbers. Running the bike machine
forwards costs six multiplications, one per link. Sweeping the blame back out
of it costs eight — one per link for that link's own share, plus a couple more
to hand the numbers back a layer. Getting those same six numbers by nudging
each dial and re-running takes forty-two.

At six dials that is an irritation. It is the shape that matters.

Add a dial and the sweep gets one multiplication longer. Add a dial to the
nudging method and it gains a whole extra run of a machine that is itself now
bigger, so its total grows with the machine multiplied by itself, while the
sweep only grows in step with the machine. At seventy billion dials the sweep is
still two passes over the machine. The other route is seventy billion of them.

Deep learning was never really the discovery that stacking layers helps. That
was suspected from the beginning. It was the discovery that a stack could be
trained for a price somebody was prepared to pay.

## What it costs you in practice

In a modern framework the whole backward sweep is one line. You work out how
wrong the answer was, call something like `backward()` on that number, and
every dial in the model quietly acquires an instruction. Applying those
instructions is a separate call, and it is the subtraction from gradient
descent, unchanged.

Two consequences follow, and both of them show up on invoices.

The first is speed. One training step costs roughly three times what answering
a single question costs — the forward run, then rather more than that again on
the way back. What matters is that it is a small, stable multiple rather than
something that depends on the model or the example. It is why a training run
can be costed in advance at all, by measuring one answer and multiplying.

The second is memory, and it catches people out. Look again at what the sweep
needed: to work out the top dial's share it multiplied by 18, which is the
value middle A produced on the way forward. That number exists only because the
forward run made it, and the sweep cannot start until it has it. So every
intermediate result in the machine has to be kept from the moment it is
computed until the sweep comes back for it. Training a model needs several
times the memory that running the same model does, and that is where it goes.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    try (GradientCollector collector = engine.newGradientCollector()) {
      NDArray loss = lossFn.evaluate(labels, model.forward(batch));
      collector.backward(loss);   // one sweep; every parameter now has a slope
    }

    optimiser.step();             // and only here does anything actually move
    ```

    The collector is the part worth noticing. It is what keeps every
    intermediate value from the forward run alive so the sweep has something to
    multiply by, and closing it is what releases them. Hold one open across
    work that does not need it and you are paying training-sized memory for
    inference-sized work.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    optimizer.zero_grad()   # throw away the last batch's numbers
    loss = criterion(model(batch), labels)
    loss.backward()         # one sweep; every parameter now has a .grad
    optimizer.step()        # and only here does anything actually move
    ```

    That first line is the classic bug. Slopes accumulate rather than replace,
    so forgetting it adds this batch's blame on top of the previous batch's and
    trains the model on a total nobody meant. Nothing errors. The run just
    quietly gets worse.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="Two dials sit in the same layer of the same machine, and after one wrong answer one of them is told to move fifty times as far as the other. Neither dial was changed, and both were judged on the same answer. Where did that difference come from?">
  From how much each dial moves the answer. A dial's share is that number
  multiplied by how wrong the answer was, and the second half is one number
  the whole machine shares, so it cannot be the source of any difference
  between two dials. The entire gap is in the first half: what each dial had to
  act on, and how strongly the thing it feeds carries through to the end.

Worth keeping, because it is what stops "which part of the model was
responsible" being a matter of opinion. Nobody forms a view about a dial. It is
a product of two numbers, one of which the forward run had already worked out
and left lying there.

</Checkpoint>
