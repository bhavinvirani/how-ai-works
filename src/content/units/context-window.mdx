You write to a company about a parcel that has not arrived. Someone replies. You
write back, they reply again, and by the fourth exchange the message on your
screen is four lines of answer sitting on top of a wall of quoted text —
everything either of you has said, oldest at the bottom, each layer indented one
step deeper than the last.

You have never read that wall. Nobody reads that wall.

The person answering you does. They have to. Replies come from whoever is on the
desk that morning, and the one who picked up your fourth message has never
spoken to you, was not there for the first three, and will be doing something
else on Thursday. Their answer makes sense because the whole thread came
attached to it, not because anybody at that company remembers you.

## The same arrangement, and it is the whole arrangement

A chat model is that desk.

When you send your fifth message, the software wrapped around the model sends
messages one to four along with it, yours and its own, as one block of text,
from the top. The model reads all of it as though for the first time, because it
is the first time. It writes a reply. Then it keeps nothing: not your name, not
the correction you made in message two, not the sentence it has just finished
writing.

Nothing carries over. Between one message and the next there is no notebook, no
file, no trace, nowhere for a trace to be kept. What looks like remembering is
re-reading.

That block of text has a name. Everything the model can see at the moment it
answers — the standing instructions the application sends ahead of you, the
whole conversation so far, anything else that has been pasted in on your behalf
— is its **context window**. Its size was decided when the model was built, and
it does not grow.

## What is in there that you did not put there

Something is usually in the window before your first word. Applications send a
standing instruction every time: who the model is meant to be here, what it may
and may not say, how to lay an answer out. You never see it, and you cannot make
it shorter. In a serious product it runs to hundreds or thousands of tokens, and
you are paying for it on every single turn.

More things arrive later without your involvement — the results of a search the
assistant ran, a page it fetched, a file you attached four messages ago and have
long since stopped thinking about. All of it lands in the same window and
competes for the same room as your sentences. To a window, a document somebody
pasted and a question you typed are the same kind of object, and neither one is
given priority over the other.

## Counted in pieces, not words

The size of a window is quoted in **tokens** — the chunks text is cut into
before a model sees any of it, about four characters of ordinary English apiece,
so a little under a word. Not words, and not characters.

That distinction does more work than it looks like it should. Whether something
fits is settled by how the cutting happens to fall, not by how long the text
looks on a page: code, a table of figures, or a run of unusual names all cost
several times what a page of prose costs. So does a language the vocabulary was
not built around. The window is the same size for everyone. How much of your
writing it holds is not.

## The answer has to fit too

Here is the part that catches almost everyone out. The window is not a budget
for what you may send. It is all the room there is, and the model's reply is
written into that same room.

Which puts the two in competition. A long conversation leaves less space for a
long answer, and asking for a long answer at the end of a long conversation asks
the window for something it does not have. This is why every serious system sets
a slice of the window aside for the reply before it lets any of your text in at
all.

## A thousand tokens, and a conversation that will not stop

The panel below is one window and one conversation, both drawn small enough to
watch. The window holds a thousand tokens, which is roughly a page and a half.
Ninety of them are gone before you type anything, on the standing instructions.
Three hundred more are held back for the reply. That leaves six hundred and ten
for the conversation itself.

<ContextBudget client:visible />

Step it forward and for a while nothing much happens. At turn five, four hundred
and ninety tokens of conversation are sitting inside six hundred and ten,
everything either side has said is still in there, and there is room to spare.

Read the last line of the readout anyway. You have typed a hundred and
twenty-five tokens. It has read one thousand nine hundred and forty-five.

That is the resend, priced. Nothing was remembered, so nothing was read once:
your first message has now been read five times over, and in a conversation that
runs to fifty turns it would be read fifty.

Turn six is where it stops fitting. The conversation wants seven hundred and
forty tokens and there are six hundred and ten, so something has to go, and the
rule the panel opens on is the one nearly everything uses when nobody has
decided otherwise. Drop the oldest. Turns one and two leave the window.

Turn one is where you said the trip had to come in under nine hundred pounds.

Nothing announces that. The answer to turn six will be perfectly fluent. Carry
on to turn nine, ask whether the boat trip is still affordable, and it will
answer — confidently, out of nothing, with no sign anywhere in that window that
a number is missing. It has not forgotten and it is not lying. It was never
shown.

Turn seven is worth watching on its own. It is a confirmation email pasted in
whole: four hundred and eighty tokens in one message, into six hundred and ten
tokens of room. Dropping the oldest, it becomes the only turn left standing. One
paste, and the conversation around it is gone.

## Then choose what goes instead

Now change the rule at the bottom of the panel, which is what it is really for.

**The biggest first** frees far more room per message thrown away, so six of the
nine turns survive to the end — turn one and its nine hundred pounds among them.
What goes instead is the pasted email and two of the model's own answers: the
substantial things, which are usually the things everything since has been
about.

**Summarise the oldest** replaces seven turns, one thousand two hundred and
twenty tokens of them, with a single stand-in costing a hundred and fifty-three.
A trace of the beginning survives, at an eighth of the price. But a summary
keeps whatever the summariser thought was worth keeping, and nobody asked you
which parts those were.

Three rules, three different holes. That is the honest shape of the problem: the
choice was never whether to lose something. It is only ever what.

## Why not simply make the window enormous

Two reasons, and neither is going anywhere.

The first is the bill. Attention compares every piece of the text against every
other piece, so the work does not double when the text doubles — it roughly
quadruples, and so does the memory, which is usually what runs out first. A
window ten times bigger is not ten times dearer to run. It is nearer a hundred.

The second is quieter and worse. Even inside a window that fits, a model does
not use all of it equally well. Material at the very start and at the very end
is picked up most reliably; material buried in the middle of a long context is
the easiest thing to skate over. Putting something in the window is not the same
as having it read.

<Aside title="More context is not better context" tone="caution">
  The instinct, once you know about the window, is to fill it: paste in the
  whole handbook, attach every file, leave the conversation running for weeks.
  This reliably makes answers worse.

The sentence that mattered is still in there. It is in there with forty pages of
near-misses, and nothing in the machine marks which is which, so the model
spends its attention across all of it, and attention spent on one thing is
attention taken from another. On top of that the answer costs more and takes
longer to arrive.

Deciding what to leave out turns out to be a real skill with a real name, and it
is a topic of its own later on. The one-line version: a window is a stage, not a
warehouse.

</Aside>

## Four things this explains

**A long conversation gets slower and dearer with every turn.** You are not
sending a message, you are sending the transcript. Turn forty costs what forty
turns of text cost to read, however short your fortieth message happened to be.

**Things fall out, and nothing tells you.** No warning appears, no gap is
visible, and the model does not report that it once knew something it now does
not. The constraint you set in your first sentence stops being enforced, and the
first sign is an answer that quietly ignores it.

**A new conversation really is a blank slate.** Starting again is not a polite
fiction or a soft reset. Nothing carries over, because there was never anything
to carry, which is also why "start a fresh chat" fixes so many problems that
look like the model being stubborn.

**"Memory" is ordinary software.** When an assistant greets you by name a week
later, no part of the model remembered you. Something wrote a note in a database
and pasted it into the window ahead of your first message. That is a genuinely
useful feature, and it is worth being precise about what it is: a filing cabinet
standing next to the machine, not a memory inside it. It occupies window room
like everything else, which is why switching memory on can make every message
more expensive without a single one of your messages changing.

<Aside title="Cheaper than resending sounds, and still resending" tone="note">
  Providers noticed that the front of a conversation is identical from one turn
  to the next, and most will now cache that unchanged prefix: send it again and
  the part that has not moved is billed at a fraction of the usual rate. It is a
  real saving and worth turning on.

It changes nothing else on this page. A cache is a receipt, not a memory. Those
tokens are still read, they still occupy the window, and they still fall out of
it when the conversation outgrows the space. A cached token is one you paid less
for, not one you no longer have to fit.

</Aside>

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    int reserve = 1024;                                    // room the reply needs
    int budget  = MODEL_WINDOW - reserve - count(instructions);

    while (count(conversation) > budget) {
        conversation.remove(0);                            // you, choosing what it forgets
    }
    ```

    Four lines, and they are the whole of what people call memory management on
    these systems. Note where `reserve` is subtracted: before anything else,
    because the reply is written into the same window the prompt arrived in.
    Leave it out and the failure is not a truncated answer, it is an error
    thrown the first time a real customer's conversation gets long, which is
    never in testing. `MODEL_WINDOW` belongs to the model rather than to you, so
    it changes the day somebody swaps the model out.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    kept = trim(messages, budget)          # whatever rule you chose
    if dropped := len(messages) - len(kept):
        log.info("dropped %d messages to fit", dropped)
    ```

    The logging line is the point. Every one of these systems drops messages
    eventually, almost none of them say so, and the resulting bug reports say
    "it forgot" or "it started ignoring my instructions", which sends whoever
    picks them up looking at the model, where there is nothing to find. One
    counter turns a mystery into a number on a dashboard.

  Neither has anything to do with the model remembering, because it still
  remembers nothing. The memory feature is ordinary storage: something writes
  notes to a database and pastes them into the window ahead of the first
  message. Those notes are tokens like any others, so every message in every
  conversation now carries them and a brand-new chat starts part-full. That is
  the bill.

The contradiction is the same fact from the other side. Forty messages in, the
conversation no longer fitted, and whatever was said at the start was dropped to
make room. The assistant did not overrule the customer. It never saw them say
it. And because nothing in a window indicates that anything is missing, it had
no reason to sound anything other than certain.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. Whenever a system appears to remember, ask
where the text is being kept and who is pasting it back in. The answer is always
ordinary software, and ordinary software has a size limit that somebody chose.
