Two requests to the same model, on the same afternoon, at the same published
prices.

The first hands over a thirty-page contract and asks whether clause 12
contradicts clause 4. The answer is two sentences long and it is on the screen
in under three seconds.

The second is one line, _write the opening chapter of a novel about a
lighthouse keeper_, and the reply runs to three and a half thousand words. It
arrives a few words at a time and takes a minute and a half.

Thirty pages in, three seconds. One line in, ninety.

Nothing was throttled and nothing went wrong. The machine did not find the
contract easy and the novel hard; it has no way to find anything hard, which is
a claim you have met more than once. What differs is which half of the job each
request was mostly made of, and the two halves are built so unalike that it is
slightly odd they appear on the same invoice.

## The half that happens all at once

You already know what happens when one of these machines reads, though it was
taught for a different reason.

Attention compares every word against every other word in the same instant.
There are no turns to take. The machine does not work along a sentence, it lands
on the whole of it together, which is exactly why the work could be spread
across thousands of processors and why it beat everything that came before.

Run that forward to a thirty-page contract. Thirty pages is not thirty readings.
It is one reading of something thirty pages long: one sweep, all of it at the
same moment, out the other side.

That sweep is not free, and it is worth being exact about what grows and what
does not. More text means more arithmetic inside the sweep — twice the pages is
at least twice the multiplying, and since every piece is compared against every
other piece, the comparing grows faster still. What does not grow is the number
of steps you have to sit through. It is one sweep whatever its size. The work
piles up sideways, into machinery that is running anyway, rather than lengthways
into time.

So a long question does cost more than a short question. It costs it in the one
shape a computer is extremely good at absorbing.

## The half that has to queue

Now the other side, which you also already have.

Writing is one piece at a time. Every piece is a complete pass through the whole
machine, and what that pass reads is everything written so far — including the
piece the pass before it produced.

Read that last clause again, because the entire cost structure of this industry
is inside it. Pass 401 cannot begin until pass 400 has finished, because the
thing pass 401 has to read does not exist until pass 400 makes it. Not "should
not begin". Cannot.

Which means no quantity of machinery shortens it. You met this argument the
other way round when a network that read one word at a time took eight rounds
over eight words and a thousand processors did not help. Attention took the
queue out of reading. It could not take the queue out of writing, and nothing
since has, because that queue is not an engineering compromise anybody agreed
to. It is what "one piece at a time" means.

Five hundred pieces is five hundred passes, in order, each waiting on the last.

## Where the ten seconds go

Notice that nothing in this unit is new machinery. Both halves were built in
earlier Parts, for reasons that had nothing to do with money, and neither one
was designed with a price in mind. Every figure below is what happens when you
set the two of them side by side on a clock.

So put them there, and the shape of every bill and every wait falls out of the
picture.

The rates behind that drawing — ten thousand pieces a second going in, fifty
coming out — are illustrative round numbers, and the exact figures move with the
model and the hardware. The gap between them does not. Reading is hundreds of
times quicker per piece than writing, everywhere, and for one reason: a sweep
swallows the whole prompt, and a pass yields exactly one piece.

The lower half of the drawing is the part worth carrying around. Make the
question ten times longer — three pages becomes thirty, which is the contract
from the top of this unit — and you wait under two seconds more. Make the answer
ten times longer and you wait ninety seconds more.

Which is the reverse of almost everyone's instinct. Asked to make one of these
systems faster, the first move people reach for is to send less. That is the
wrong half.

## "Slow" is two numbers

Users say _slow_ and mean one of two things, and the two have no fix in common.

**The wait before the first word** is the sweep plus a single pass. It has
almost nothing to do with how long the answer turns out to be — at the instant
the first piece appears, nothing about the length has been settled, because
there is nothing that settles it.

**The wait until the last word** is that, plus one more pass for every remaining
piece. It is almost entirely a fact about the answer's length.

They are quoted separately, usually as time to first token and total time —
_token_ being the trade's word for the pieces this unit has been counting. And
they go wrong for different reasons. A first word that arrives late means the
reading half is labouring: an enormous prompt, or a queue of other people's
requests in front of yours. A last word that arrives late behind a first word
that was punctual means only that the answer is long. From the user's chair
those two produce exactly the same complaint.

This is also the whole explanation of something you have watched a thousand
times without asking about. Answers appear a few words at a time because a few
words at a time is genuinely all there is. Piece one exists a fifth of a second
in; piece five hundred does not exist for another ten seconds. A system that
shows you nothing until the end is not working harder. It is holding finished
text back.

So sending each piece as it is made is the cheapest improvement in this unit. It
does not change the total by a millisecond. It changes when the waiting stops
from the reader's side, and a page that starts moving in a fifth of a second is
a different product from one that sits blank for ten.

## Now the bill, which has two rates

Every price list for these models quotes two figures rather than one: so much
per million pieces sent, and so much per million pieces produced. The second is
the larger, usually by somewhere between three and five times.

That ratio surprises people who expect it to be enormous, and it surprises
people who expect it to be one. Both halves do roughly the same arithmetic per
piece — a piece of prompt and a piece of answer each cost a trip through the
same set of dials. The difference is not how much work. It is how well the work
packs.

Reading two thousand pieces at once keeps every part of the machine busy for the
whole sweep. Producing one piece means hauling that entire pile of dials into
position to manufacture a single chunk, and then doing it again for the next
one. Same arithmetic, and the machine spends most of the writing half waiting on
itself. What the higher rate is really charging you for is how idle the hardware
is while your answer is being made.

### Which half your bill is actually made of

Here the obvious reading of that price list is usually wrong.

"Output costs five times as much" invites the conclusion that output is where
the money goes. It is a rate, not a total, and in most real systems the totals
lean hard the other way. A question that arrives with standing instructions,
forty tool descriptions, the conversation so far and five fetched pages in front
of it, which is what a request in a working product looks like, might run to
ten thousand pieces and be answered in three hundred.

Do the multiplication. Ten thousand at one, three hundred at five: ten thousand
against fifteen hundred. Roughly seven pounds in every eight of that bill is the
_cheap_ half, charged at the low rate, on a number nobody typed.

### The same paragraph, sent for the ten-thousandth time

The biggest single line in that ten thousand is the part that never changes. The
standing instructions and the tool descriptions are identical on every call, and
nothing in the machine remembers them between calls, so they are sent again and
read again, from the top, for as long as the product runs.

Providers sell the obvious fix. Mark the fixed opening of a prompt as cacheable
and the work of sweeping over it is kept for a while; a later call that begins
with exactly the same text skips it. You pay a fraction of the usual rate for
the part that was reused, often around a tenth, and the first word arrives
sooner as well, because reading is the half you just skipped.

Two conditions do all the work, and both are easy to break by accident.

**It has to be a prefix.** What is reused is the sweep over the opening of the
prompt, so everything shared must sit at the front, ahead of anything that
varies from call to call.

**It has to be identical, character for character.** One timestamp, one
customer's name, one tool list that gets rebuilt in a different order, and the
match ends at that character. A cache-hit figure stuck at zero is nearly always
this and nothing more exotic.

And it is worth naming what this is not. It is not memory. Nothing about the
conversation is retained, nothing is being recalled, and the answer is identical
either way. It is a re-read being skipped on text that is being sent again
anyway — the same trick you met inside a single answer, where the machinery
keeps its working from one pass to the next, stretched to work across separate
calls.

<Aside title="The discount for not being in a hurry">
  Providers run many people's requests through the same machinery at the same
  time, because packing them together is the only thing that makes the writing
  half use the hardware decently at all.

Which means your insistence on an answer _now_ is part of what you are paying
for. Most providers will sell you the other option: hand over a pile of work
with no deadline, accept the results within hours rather than seconds, and pay
about half. Nothing about the model changes.

It is the right lane for anything with nobody sitting in front of it —
overnight classification, backfills, an evaluation run over a few thousand cases
— and the wrong one for anything a person is waiting on.

</Aside>

<Aside title="Cheap is not the same as harmless" tone="caution">
  This unit says a long question is the cheap half. The unit on assembling a
  context says a crowded window makes answers worse. Both are true, and they are
  counting in different currencies.

Sending a whole document rather than a summary of it costs very little time and
a low rate per piece. What it can cost is accuracy: near-miss passages compete
with the one that answers the question, and the middle of a long context is
where material gets skated over.

So "reading is the cheap half" is a fact about the clock and the bill, and it is
never on its own a reason to send more. The reason to trim a question is
quality. The reason to trim an answer is speed.

</Aside>

## Four things that actually move these numbers

**Ask for less.** A limit on the length of the answer is a limit on the only
half that matters for speed and the dearer half of the bill, and it costs one
sentence of prompt. _Two sentences, no preamble_ is not a stylistic preference
here; it is the cheapest optimisation available anywhere in this subject.

The same arithmetic explains why a model that works through its reasoning before
answering costs what it does. That working is written one piece at a time like
everything else, at the output rate and at output speed, whether or not anybody
shows it to you.

**Do not be frightened of a long question.** Sending the actual document rather
than a summary of it is usually right, and the reason is on the drawing above.
The caution above still stands, though: cheap is a fact about the bill, and it
is not permission to send everything you have.

**Keep the fixed part fixed, and keep it at the front.** Standing instructions
and tool descriptions are the one thing you pay for on every single call
forever. Getting them cached is the difference between paying for them once an
hour and paying for them a million times.

**Stream anything a person is waiting on.** Free, in the strict sense that the
total time does not change at all, and it converts a ten-second blank page into
a ten-second read.

This Part has been about making the same job cost less: a smaller machine, one
taught by a larger one, one squeezed to fit on your own hardware. This unit is
where the reason for all of it sits. And it is also why the first thing to try
is not technical at all — the biggest single lever on the list above is a
sentence you type.

    ```java
    var res = client.send(request);
    var use = res.usage();

    log.info("in={} cached={} out={} cost={}",
             use.inputTokens(),
             use.cachedInputTokens(),
             use.outputTokens(),
             use.inputTokens()  * IN_RATE
           + use.outputTokens() * OUT_RATE);
    ```

    Three counters and one multiplication, logged per call, is most of cost
    observability. The first surprise is usually `inputTokens`, because it is a
    number nobody typed — the gap between it and the length of the user's
    message is everything your own code put in front of the model.

    `cachedInputTokens` is the one worth alerting on. On a system with a fixed
    system prompt it should be close to `inputTokens` minus the conversation,
    and if it sits at zero then nothing is being reused. The cause is almost
    always something near the top of the prompt that varies per request: a
    timestamp, a session id, a tool list assembled from a `HashSet` and
    therefore serialised in a different order each time.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    t0, first = time.perf_counter(), None

    with client.stream(**request) as stream:
        for piece in stream.text_stream:
            if first is None:
                first = time.perf_counter() - t0
            render(piece)

    total = time.perf_counter() - t0
    ```

    Two clocks, and most services record only the second one. They move for
    different reasons: `first` tracks how much you sent and whether the front of
    it was cached, and `total - first` tracks how much came back. A p95 on
    `total` alone cannot tell you which half regressed, and the two have no fix
    in common.

    `(total - first)` divided by the number of output pieces is the only figure
    here that is a property of the model and the hardware rather than of your
    own prompt, which makes it the one worth comparing across providers.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="A support assistant sends the same thirty-page policy pack with every question and writes back a full page of prose each time. Users say it is slow. The team's plan is to cut the pack down to five pages so there is less for the model to read. Say what that change would do, what it would not do, and what you would do instead.">
  It would help the bill and barely touch the complaint. Thirty pages is around
  twenty thousand pieces, and the machine reads all of them in one sweep — about
  two seconds. Cutting to five pages saves most of that: call it a second and a
  half.

Now look at what the users are waiting for. A full page of prose is roughly seven
hundred pieces, and every one of those is a separate pass that cannot start until
the pass before it has finished. That is around fourteen seconds, and cutting the
policy pack by five-sixths leaves every bit of it in place. They would take a
sixteen-second wait down to fourteen and a half, and nobody in front of the
screen would notice.

The change that moves the number they are complaining about is on the other side:
cap the answer. Two or three sentences, with the detail available on request.
That is the only half where length and time are the same thing. Then send the
pieces as they are produced, so the first sentence is on the screen almost
immediately, which does not make anything faster and does make the waiting stop
sooner.

And they may not need to trim the pack at all. It is identical on every single
call, which is the exact case prompt caching exists for: put it at the front,
keep it byte-for-byte the same, and stop paying to re-read it. If they want to
cut it down anyway, the honest reason is that a crowded window produces worse
answers — a quality argument, not a speed one, and worth making on its own terms.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. When someone says a system is slow, ask which
word they are waiting for. Trimming what you send moves the first one; trimming
what comes back moves the last one, and the last one is nearly all of it.

</Checkpoint>
