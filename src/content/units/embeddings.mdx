The ironmonger's on the corner has been there since before the war, and nothing
in it is where you would put it. Bin 3,180 is brass hinges. Bin 3,181 is fuse
wire. Bin 3,182 is bath plugs.

There is a system. Everything went into the next empty bin on the day it
arrived, and the numbers have been climbing ever since.

Ask the owner for bin 3,180 and it is in your hand in four seconds. Ask him for
"something like this, only longer" and the numbers are no help to him at all. He
walks off into the back of the shop and finds it from memory, because nothing
about 3,181 tells you what is in it. All it tells you is what turned up next.

A machine handed a pile of words is standing in that shop.

## A number can be a name without being a meaning

Everything inside a machine is arithmetic. Dials get multiplied, sums get
compared, one number gets nudged a little closer to another. The word _happy_
does not take part in any of that. It is not a number, and there is no obvious
number it ought to be.

So the first move is the shop's move. Take every word anybody uses, sort them
into one long list, and give each word its row number. This is not a bad idea
and real systems really do it — the number is a **name**, a way of saying which
word you mean in a form a computer can hold on to.

What the number is not is a meaning. Sorted alphabetically, row 1,742 is _cat_
and row 1,743 is _catapult_, which are neighbours by an accident of spelling.
_Kitten_ is three thousand rows away.

That is not merely untidy. It means a machine that has been shown a million
sentences about cats has learned nothing at all about kittens, because as far as
its arithmetic is concerned those are two unrelated names. Every word has to be
learned from scratch, on its own, from however few examples it happened to get.
Rare words never stand a chance.

## A position instead

Change one thing. Instead of a single number that names the word, give each word
a **position** — a couple of numbers saying where it sits, the way a grid
reference says where a village is.

The numbers have not become more meaningful on their own. What has appeared is
the space between them. Two words now have a distance, and once there is a
distance, an arrangement can be judged: put words that mean similar things close
together and words with nothing in common far apart, and _near_ starts carrying
information that a row number never could.

A machine that learns something about one word now automatically knows a little
about everything sitting nearby, because everything it does with a position it
does with the positions around it too.

That position is called the word's **embedding**, and the whole arrangement is
an embedding space. It is a graceless name for one of the prettiest ideas in the
subject.

## Nobody chose the positions

Here is the part that makes it work, and it is the fill-in-the-blank game
wearing a different hat.

Nobody sits down and arranges the words. The positions are learned — cover a
word, make the machine guess it, score how far off the guess was, nudge. The
positions are among the dials being nudged.

Think about what winning that game requires. If the machine is guessing what
goes in _the ——— pulled into the yard_, then whatever it does with _lorry_ it
had better also do with _truck_, because either one could be the answer and it
has no way of knowing which. The cheapest arrangement that achieves this is to
put those two words in the same place.

That is worth saying precisely, because it is easy to get backwards. Words do
not end up near each other because they appear together. _Lorry_ and _truck_
almost never appear in the same sentence. They end up near each other because
they appear in the same company — surrounded by yards and drivers and
motorways, in gaps where each of them would have done.

Run that over billions of sentences and the map builds itself. Nobody defined
similar. It fell out of the fact that similar words get used in similar company,
which was sitting in the text all along waiting for someone to notice.

That correction changes what gets a position. It changes nothing else here.
Whatever the pieces turn out to be, each one gets a position, distance between
them still means similarity, and everything below still holds. Read "word" as
"whatever unit the machine reads" and you will not have to take any of this

## Walk around a small one

Below is a map with twenty-five words on it. The marker starts on _king_.

Leave the arrows alone at first and just move. The two sliders slide the marker
across the map and up it, and the sentence underneath names the words nearest to
wherever you have put it. Drop it on _kitten_ and the nearest three are puppy,
cat and dog. Drop it on _doctor_ and they are teacher, nurse and engineer.
Nobody wrote either of those lists.

Notice how much of the map is empty. Most positions are not near anything at
all, which will matter in a minute.

## The arrows are the surprising bit

Now the second thing, which is stranger than the first.

Set the arrow to _man → woman_. That is not a setting; it is a measurement. It
is the step you would take to walk from the dot marked _man_ to the dot marked
_woman_, and the map shows it faintly, where it was measured.

The marker is still on _king_. Take that same step, that exact length in that
exact direction, from there instead. It ends on _queen_.

Do it again from _uncle_ and it ends on _aunt_. From _boy_ it ends on _girl_.
One arrow, measured once, between one pair of words — and it keeps landing on
the counterpart of whatever you start it from.

Then try the other arrow, _puppy → dog_, which if it is about anything is about
growing up. Lay it down at _kitten_ and it ends on _cat_. Lay it down at _boy_,
which is nowhere near the animals, and it ends on _man_.

You will meet this written as arithmetic, and it is worth being able to read:

$$
\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen}
$$

In words: start from where _king_ sits, take away _man_, add _woman_, and you
end up beside _queen_. Subtracting one word and adding another is only another
way of writing "take the step that leads from man to woman", and every word in
that line is a position, so the plus and minus are the ordinary ones.

Nobody built those directions. Nobody checked that the man-to-woman step would
work starting from _uncle_. It works because of how the words were used by the
people who wrote the text, and the arrangement had no cheaper way to keep all of
that straight.

## What the demonstration is not telling you

Three honest corrections, because this idea is usually oversold.

**The map above is drawn by hand.** Twenty-five words, placed deliberately, so
that there would be something small enough to look at. The shape of it is
faithful — that is what real maps do — but no machine learned this one.

**Two numbers is a flattening.** A real word map gives each word a few hundred
numbers, sometimes a few thousand. Not two axes but hundreds of them at once,
which nobody can picture and which does not squash down onto a page without
losing most of what is in it. Every flat picture of an embedding space you have
ever seen, including this one, is a shadow.

**The arithmetic is patchier than the demonstration suggests.** Put the marker
on _kitten_ and follow the man → woman arrow. It lands in open ground, nearest
to the word it set off from, because this map holds no feminine of a kitten and
was never going to invent one. Most starting points do that, and real maps
behave the same way — which is why the usual way of running the famous
demonstration quietly forbids the answer from being one of the three words you
started with. Without that rule the answer that comes back is very often _king_
itself. The trick is real. It is not the clean machine it looks like on a slide.

## The idea is not really about words

The move generalises, and this is where most of its working life is spent.
Anything you can teach a machine to place can go on a map like this: photographs,
songs, products, faces, molecules, whole documents. Similar things land near
each other for the same reason and by the same route.

Once things are positions, "find me more like this one" stops being a question
about meaning and becomes a question about distance, which a computer can answer
for a billion items without understanding any of them. That is what is behind
the search box that copes with you not using the site's words, and the row of
recommendations under whatever you just watched.

It is also where every language model starts. Before the machinery in the rest
of this site gets to do anything, the incoming text is turned into positions.
Everything after that is arithmetic on the map.

<Aside title="The map learns the prejudice too" tone="caution">
  Put the marker on _doctor_ in the instrument above and follow the man → woman
  arrow. It lands on _nurse_.

That is not a joke somebody dropped into the demonstration. Word maps built from
real text put those two words in exactly that relationship, and the early ones
were shown doing it — along with putting traditionally male occupations nearer
to male words and traditionally female ones nearer to female words. Nobody
intended it and nobody wrote it down. The maps were built out of how people
actually write, and that is how people actually write.

It matters because these maps are load-bearing. They sit underneath systems that
sort job applications and rank search results, where "near" quietly becomes
"relevant", and a machine reproducing the average of what it read is doing
exactly what it was built to do.

</Aside>

<Checkpoint question="The words 'lorry' and 'truck' almost never turn up in the same sentence as each other. A map built by reading text puts them practically on top of one another anyway. How did that happen?">
  Because position comes from the company a word keeps, not from the words it
  appears beside. Both of them show up surrounded by yards, drivers, loads and
  motorways, in gaps where either would have been a good guess — so anything the
  machine does with one of them it needs to do with the other, and the cheapest
  way to arrange that is to put them in the same place.

Worth holding on to, because it also tells you what the map is not. It is not a
record of what words mean. It is a record of how they were used by the people
who wrote the text it read, which is why it can be right about lorries and
trucks and wrong about doctors and nurses in the same breath, without anything
having gone wrong.
