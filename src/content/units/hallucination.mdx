A form comes back across the desk with one box still empty, and the person
holding it asks whether you happen to know the reference number.

You have never seen this document. But you have seen a hundred like it, so if
you had to put something in that box you would put `HB-4417/22` — two letters, a
dash, four digits, a slash, a year — you would do it without pausing, and it
would look exactly right.

Producing the _shape_ of an answer and producing the answer are two different
jobs, and the first one is enormously easier. You have just done it, at speed,
on a document you have never read.

The machine only ever does the first job. Not usually, and not when it is
struggling. Always — including every single time it happens to be right.

## What those numbers are a score of

The unit on large language models ended with one sentence and no second half.
Give the model some text; it produces one number for every entry in its
vocabulary, saying how well that entry would follow.

Sit for a moment with what those numbers are _about_.

"How well it would follow" is a relationship between a candidate chunk and the
text in front of it, worked out from an enormous pile of writing. It is a claim
about text. Nothing in it reaches past the text to the thing the text is about,
and nothing in it could: there is no store of facts to consult, no index, no
field anywhere marked true. Truth was never one of the inputs to that score, so
it cannot be one of the outputs.

Which gives the sentence this unit exists for, and it is easier to say than to
believe. A true answer and an invented one are not two things the machine does.
They are one thing the machine does, meeting two different worlds.

There is no shortcut taken on the invented one and no extra care taken on the
true one. Same chunks, same stack, same column of scores, same arithmetic, same
electricity bill, same tenth of a second.

## How an invention gets committed to

Type this at an assistant:

_What did the Ferrers review conclude about school starting ages?_

There is no Ferrers review. You invented it while reading the line.

Nothing in the machine registers that, because registering it is not an
operation it has. Your sentence is cut into chunks, the chunks fetch their
positions, the stack runs, and a column of scores comes out — the same column,
at the same cost, as for a question about something that exists.

Now ask what sits at the top of that column. In the writing the machine was
tuned on, a well-formed question about a named review is followed, overwhelmingly,
by the opening of an answer. So the highest-scoring piece is the beginning of
one. Something in the shape of _The review found_.

And then that piece is glued on and the machine is run again, and this is the
part worth slowing down for.

On the second pass, the text in front of it includes its own first three words.
It is no longer scoring the opening of an answer about a review that may not
exist. It is scoring the continuation of an answer already under way. The most
plausible continuation of _The review found_ is a finding. The most plausible
continuation of a finding is a figure. Within a dozen pieces there is a review,
a conclusion and a percentage, and every one of them was the most reasonable
thing to write given everything written before it.

Nobody decided to invent anything. There was no moment of decision to have. What
there was is a ratchet: the first plausible piece makes the second plausible
piece more plausible still, and by the fourth the machine is being consistent —
with itself.

## The name is the worst thing about it

The behaviour is called **hallucination**, and the word gets two things wrong at
once.

It implies a fault: a machine that normally perceives the world correctly and
has, for a moment, stopped. And it implies a perceiver — something in there with
a view of the world that has slipped. Neither is present. Nothing malfunctioned,
nothing was misperceived, and the arithmetic that produced the Ferrers review is
the arithmetic that produces everything else, running exactly as built.

Some people prefer **confabulation**, borrowed from neurology, where it means
filling a gap with something plausible and not noticing. That is closer, because
it drops the idea of a glitch, but it still smuggles in somebody doing the not
noticing. The dull and accurate description is that the machine scored the next
piece, which is the only thing it has ever done.

Keep the word if you like — everyone uses it, and you will be understood. Just
do not let it carry the suggestion that somewhere there is a working version of
this that has stopped working.

## The branch that is not there

Most of the trouble people have here comes from a picture they are carrying
without having chosen it. It is worth drawing, because once you can see it beside
the real thing the argument is over in about four seconds.

The top half is the machine nearly everyone pictures. It has a lookup, and the
lookup can fail, and failing has somewhere to go. A check has an obvious home in
that drawing: straight after the lookup, before anything is said out loud.

The bottom half is the complete inventory, and you have already been shown every
box in it. Text in. One pass. A number on every entry. Take one, glue it on. Run
again.

Now go looking for the place to put the check. There is no lookup to put it
after. There is no branch for it to sit on. The question _did I find it?_ is not
answered no anywhere in that loop; it is never asked, because there is nothing in
the loop capable of asking it.

That is the whole unit, and it is why this is not a bug that anyone is going to
fix with a better version. It is not a defect in the mechanism. It is the
mechanism.

<Aside title="The number people mistake for confidence" tone="caution">
  There is a number, and it is the wrong number.

Every piece the machine produces comes out of a column that has been turned into
shares adding to one, so the piece it settled on has a share attached: 0.9, 0.4,
whatever it happens to be. Providers will hand you that number, and it is very
tempting to read it as how sure the machine is.

What it measures is how strongly this machine preferred this chunk given the text
in front of it. Halfway through a fabricated review, the text in front of it _is_
a fabricated review, and the next word of a sentence already under way is highly
predictable. So the shares are high, and they climb as the invention gets more
fluent — the exact opposite of what you wanted the number for.

It is a real measurement of a real thing. The thing is commitment, not
correctness.

</Aside>

## Why the invented ones sound so sure

Being unable to check is one problem. Sounding certain while not checking is a
second one, and it was installed separately, in two stages, by people who were
not aiming at it.

The first stage is the pile of text. Writing published on the internet is
overwhelmingly written in a confident voice. Reference pages do not hedge,
product descriptions do not hedge, and someone who does not know the answer
mostly does not write the page at all. So "I am not sure" is a rare continuation
in that material, and a rare continuation gets a low score. Hedging was
statistically discouraged before anyone tuned anything on purpose.

The second stage is preference training, which is where it gets sharper. People
are shown two answers to the same request and asked which is better. Set a clean,
direct answer next to a hedged one that declines to commit, and the direct one
wins, not because anybody was rewarding overconfidence, but because it reads as
more helpful, and helpful is the thing being judged. Millions of those
comparisons, and the dials move that way.

So confidence is not a readout from anywhere inside. It is a manner, and it is a
manner the machine was rewarded for. Which lands badly, because manner is exactly
what you had left to judge by.

## Four answers, and one clause with nothing behind it

So far the machine has been answering out of nothing. The version you will
actually meet is the other one, where somebody has put a document in front of it
and asked it to answer from that. It is the single most effective thing anyone
does about this problem. The panel below is what it still leaves you with.

Five lines about a furniture workshop that does not exist, and four answers built
from them. Three are made entirely out of what those lines say. One has a clause
in it the passage never mentions.

Read all four, commit to one, and only then check. Most people get it wrong, and
getting it wrong is not a failure of attention.

<SpotTheFabrication client:visible />

The invented clause is _the second Fenner Street workshop to take the award and
the first for about twenty years_. Every part of it is made of material that is
genuinely there — the award is in the passage, Fenner Street is in the passage,
a span of years is the kind of thing that belongs in a sentence like that one.
The only missing ingredient is the fact.

Then the panel does the thing prose cannot, and measures the writing, because the
writing is what you were reading it with. Hedging, figures, length, taken from
both ends: six ways of picking the odd one out, and all six of them accuse an
answer that is true. The invented one is not the most certain of the four, not
the most specific and not the longest. It sits in the middle of all three, which
is what four sentences produced by the same process look like, because they were.

Notice which answer the measures do land on. The one about the building is the
most hedged and the most figure-laden at once, the two things readers treat as
opposite giveaways, and it is entirely sound. Its 1930 is not written in the
passage, but the passage gives 1971 and forty years, and 1930 is what you get by
subtracting. An answer can go past its source by arithmetic without leaving it.
Reading for a suspicious surface catches that one and walks straight past the
other.

There is one honest limit on the exercise. Here you had the passage in front of
you, which is the good case. Most of the time nobody has put a passage in front
of anything, and then the comparison you would need is not merely tedious. It
does not exist.

## Where it concentrates

Four places, and they are all the same place seen from different angles: text
where the pattern is strong and the material is thin.

**Anything with a format.** Citations, case numbers, page references, quotations,
identifiers, URLs. The shape of a reference is one of the most learnable things
in the language, and the contents of any particular reference are among the
least. Producing something citation-shaped is nearly free; producing the right
one is a lookup, and there is no lookup.

**Anything rare or recent.** Where the material is thin, no continuation
dominates the column, so the machine takes whatever fits best, and something
always fits best. Thin evidence does not produce hesitation. It produces a
confident answer assembled from adjacent material.

**Anything with a well-shaped wrong answer.** If a false answer sits in the place
a true one would, it scores like a true one. That is the whole of the selection
criterion.

**Anything you asserted yourself.** Put a false premise in your question and the
machine will usually build on it, because your text is text: there is no step at
which what you wrote gets held up against anything. This one is worth watching
for, because it is the failure you cause.

## What helps, and what only feels like it

Everything on the working side has the same shape: it either narrows what counts
as a plausible continuation, or it puts a comparison outside the machine. Nothing
puts a check inside, because there is nowhere to put one.

### The things that work

**Give it the source, and keep the question inside the source.** Paste the actual
passage and ask for an answer from that alone. The machine does not become
truthful; the field of plausible continuations narrows to things the passage
says. As the panel above showed, that is a large improvement and not a guarantee.

**Ask for the quote, then check the quote.** Not because a quotation is evidence,
it is produced by the same operation as everything else, but because a
fabricated quotation fails a search against the source in about a second. The
value is in the search, which happens outside the model.

**Ask again in a fresh conversation.** Things with a lot of material behind them
come back the same way twice. Things being filled in wander. This is a smoke
test, not a proof, and it is nearly free.

**Reduce the number of questions it has nothing for.** The highest-risk question
in any system is one the supplied material does not answer, because there is no
outcome in which the machine declines by default.

### The things that do not

**Telling it not to make things up.** That instruction is text, added to the text.
It shifts which continuations score well, slightly and genuinely, and it does
not install a step that does not exist. A model that has just been told to be
accurate produces exactly the same kind of column it produced before.

**Asking whether it is sure.** This one actively misleads, and the mechanism is
worth having. You have appended text in which somebody expresses doubt about the
previous answer. What most plausibly follows text like that? An apology and a
revision. So it will often "correct" an answer that was right, and agree with you
when you push back on a true statement. You are not querying a belief. You are
continuing a conversation in which someone has raised an eyebrow.

**Turning the randomness down.** Making the machine take the top of the column
every time makes the same fabrication come out reliably. Consistent is not true.

**Trusting how it sounds.** Covered above, and it bears one more line: fluency is
the one thing this machine is guaranteed to have, and confidence is a manner it
was trained towards. Those are the two worst possible signals to judge it by, and
they are the two most available.

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    String answer = model.answerFrom(passage, question);
    String quote  = extractQuote(answer);

    if (!passage.contains(quote)) {
      return Result.notGrounded(question);   // the only real check here
    }
    ```

    The load-bearing call is `passage.contains`, and what makes it a check is
    that it is a string search over your own text. It never asks the model
    anything. Any "verification" implemented by sending the answer back with
    *are you sure?* is not a second opinion. It is the same next-piece scorer,
    now scoring a conversation in which doubt has been expressed, which is a
    known way to make it retract something correct.

    Worth knowing what this does not catch: a real quotation with an
    unsupported conclusion hung off it. The quote check proves the sentence is
    in the document. It proves nothing about the claim built on top of it.

  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    quote = extract_quote(answer)
    if quote not in passage:
        raise NotGrounded(question)
    ```

    `in` is an operation the model does not have and your program does. That
    asymmetry is the whole design: every reliable defence against this lives on
    your side of the call, working on finished text, comparing it against
    something you control.

    The other half of the job is the prompt that asks for a quotable span in the
    first place, and it has to survive the case where the passage does not
    answer the question. If nothing in your pipeline makes "the passage does not
    say" a cheap and acceptable output, the model has no route to it, and it
    will write you something instead, because writing something is the only
    thing in the loop.

A month later it tells a customer something the handbook does not contain, citing a clause that it does. The team's fix is to add a line to the instructions telling it to answer only from the handbook and to say so when it cannot. Say why that will help without solving it, and name the one thing in their system that was ever doing real work.">
  The instruction is text added to text. It genuinely shifts which continuations
  score well, so fewer answers will drift off the handbook. That is a real
  effect and worth having. What it cannot do is add a step. There is still no
  moment in the machine at which a claim exists and gets held up against the
  handbook, so the decline they asked for is not a route the machine has. It is
  a phrasing that now scores a little better than it did.

The clause number is the sharper half of the question. Nobody looked a clause up.
The citation and the claim came out of the same operation, separately, one piece
at a time — a clause-shaped run of characters that scored well, followed by a
sentence that scored well after it. A real clause number is not evidence that the
answer came from that clause. It is evidence that the machine has seen a lot of
clause numbers.

The only thing doing real work was the spot-check, because it happened outside
the machine, on finished text, against the handbook. And it was checking the
wrong half: that the clauses exist, rather than that the clauses say what the
answers claim. Move the comparison to the claim and it becomes a check. Leave it
on the citation and it is a check that a fabrication passes.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. Whenever someone proposes making a model verify
itself, the question that settles it is: at the moment of verification, where
does the thing being verified live, and what is it being compared against? If
both answers are "inside the model", nothing is being verified.

</Checkpoint>
