Somebody is running a demo, and it is a good one.

A forty-page contract goes into a window. Four seconds later there is a list of
six clauses that need attention, each with a sentence saying why, and the
sentence about the indemnity is the sentence a lawyer would have written.
Someone asks a follow-up question and gets a straight answer. Around the table,
people are nodding.

There are two honest reactions available in that room, and neither of them is a
judgement. Being impressed is a reaction to the four seconds. Being sceptical is
a reaction to having been sold something before. Neither one tells you whether
this will hold up on a Tuesday, on a contract nobody has seen yet.

There is a third move open to you now, and it is two questions long. Getting to
those two questions is what the rest of this site was for.

## What was given up on the first page

This site opened by taking something away.

It put a job in front of you that could not be done by writing down how to do
it, not a job whose instructions were long or fiddly, but one where the
instructions did not exist in any form a person could get at. Sorting junk mail
from real mail. Recognising a face in bad light. Hearing that a sentence is
wrong.

The way out was not a cleverer rule. It was a concession: stop describing the
job, gather a great many examples of it being done, and build a machine that
adjusts itself until its answers stop disagreeing with them.

Everything since has been the consequence of that one concession. Not a series
of separate inventions that happen to be filed under the same heading — a single
decision, followed all the way down, for sixty units.

## Two boxes, and the second one does all the work

Write the move out in the smallest form it comes in and it is two boxes with an
arrow between them.

**Make a guess. Be told how wrong it was.**

The unit on the training loop had four words for that cycle — guess, compare,
blame, nudge — and the last two are what a machine does about the answer to the
second box. Take those out as plumbing and a pair is what is left.

Now notice where the power sits, because it is not where people put it. Guessing
is free. Anything can guess; a coin can guess. What makes guessing worth doing at
all is that something is standing next to it holding the answer, able to say by
how much the guess missed.

Which means the second box is where every argument in the first half of this site
was actually taking place. Choosing what to count was an argument about the
second box — a machine becomes good at precisely what that box measures, and is
free to get worse at everything else. Holding a slice of the data back was an
argument about whether the second box had ever been telling the truth. Counting
two kinds of mistake separately was the discovery that one box can hide two
completely different failures inside a single flattering number.

None of that is four separate lessons about training. It is one lesson about the
second box, met from four directions.

## The guess that turned out to fit everything

The first box is more open than it looks. A guess can be about anything: which of
six trays an email belongs in, what a house will sell for, which square on a
board to play next.

Of all the things a machine could be asked to guess, one turned out to be worth
more than every other put together, and it is almost embarrassingly plain. Guess
the next piece of a piece of writing.

Two things made it the one.

The first is that the second box comes free. Take a sentence somebody already
wrote, cover the next chunk, ask for a guess, uncover it. The exercise and its
marked answer arrived in the same envelope, which is why the exercise book could
be an appreciable share of everything ever published, rather than however many
examples a company could afford to have labelled by hand.

The second is the part that surprised nearly everybody, including the people
doing it. Guessing the next piece well turns out to require a great deal more
than knowing about writing.

To finish _the capital of Peru is_ you need the capital of Peru. To finish _she
put the glass down carefully, because it was_ you need to know something about
glasses. To finish the last line of an argument you need the argument. Nobody sat
down to teach a machine geography, or how a joke is shaped, or what a Python
error message looks like. Somebody sat down to teach it to finish sentences, and
finishing those particular sentences requires all three.

So facts, grammar, register, the layout of code and a rough sense of how one
claim follows another all arrive as side effects of a single exercise, done a few
trillion times, with the answer key built in.

## Machinery for guessing well

Everything in the middle of this site is machinery for doing that one exercise
better, and it is worth seeing it that way, because listed as a set of inventions
it looks like five unrelated ideas.

Depth is there so an intermediate idea can be built once and used by everything
above it. Attention is there because the word that settles what another word
means may be forty words away, and a memory passed along a line has forgotten it
by then. Positions are stamped into the pieces because attention compares
everything with everything and never asks which came first. The block gets a
shortcut and a rescale wrapped round it so that ninety-six copies can be stacked
without training falling over.

Not one of those changed the exercise. Every one of them made the guessing
better, and then size did the rest, at which point the guessing got good enough
that people started talking to it.

There is one more turn of the same handle, and it produced the thing you actually
use. A machine tuned only to continue text will continue your question rather
than answer it, because a question followed by more questions is a perfectly
ordinary thing to find in writing. So the guess was aimed somewhere else: not at
what came next in a document, but at which of two answers a person would rather
have been given.

The second box changed hands. Instead of the next chunk of a real sentence, the
correction now comes from a model fitted to a pile of human comparisons. Same two
boxes. A different thing holding the answer, and, as the unit on preference
training spent its length arguing, a thing that knows a great deal about approval
and nothing whatever about truth.

## Then the second box is taken away

Here is the sentence the entire back half of this site hangs from, and it is
short enough to be underwhelming.

When training ends, the dials stop moving and the machine goes to work. It makes
exactly the guess it was making all the way through — score every piece in the
vocabulary, take one, glue it on, read the whole thing back, again.

The other box is gone.

Nothing is holding the answer. Nothing compares. Nothing can say how wrong the
guess was, because there is nothing present for it to be wrong against: the score
is a claim about how well a chunk sits after some text, worked out from a pile of
writing, and no part of it reaches through the text to the world the text is
about.

Every limitation in the unit before this one is that single absence, seen from a
different angle.

An invented answer costs the same as a true one, because they are the same
operation meeting two different worlds. It knows nothing that happened after the
pass over the text finished, because nothing has corrected it since. It cannot
tell your instructions from a sentence in a document you asked it to read,
because both are text in front of a machine that scores what comes next. It
cannot report how sure it is, because the only number available says how strongly
it preferred that chunk, and halfway through a fluent invention, that number is
high.

Not a list of separate problems. One hole, and a different name for each side of
it.

That hole is the shape of everything that follows. Every technique with a name,

slide: somebody rebuilding the second box by hand, outside the machine, because
there is no longer one inside it.

## Three places to stand

And there are only three places to put it, which is a surprising thing to be able
to say about a field this noisy. The reason is geometric rather than clever. The
box is closed. You can act before the guess, you can take the job away from the
guess, or you can act on what the guess produced. There is nowhere else.

**Before it guesses.** Narrow the field of things that could plausibly come next.
That is what the wording of a request does, and what a few finished examples do,
and what pasting in the passages you looked up does, and what a short training run
on your own examples does. Four techniques met under four names, all doing one
thing: making the gap the machine is guessing into a narrower gap, so that fewer
of the plausible things in it are wrong.

Be precise about what that is not. A narrowed guess is still a guess. Handing a
model the right passage does not install a step where it checks; it makes the
wrong answers less plausible, which is a real and large improvement and is not the
same kind of thing as a check.

**Instead of guessing.** Some parts of a job are not guesses and never were.
Today's date. The sum of a column. What is on page three of a file nobody has
opened yet. For those, the fix is to route around the machine entirely — a
search, a query, a calculator, a piece of code that runs. What the model
contributes is a request, written in text, that your program reads and decides
whether to honour. Nothing it writes ever runs itself. An agent is this same
arrangement with one thing
changed, the model choosing which request to write next instead of following an
order you laid down, and everything good and bad about agents comes out of that
single move.

**After it has guessed.** Take the finished text and hold it up against something
you control: the passage it was meant to answer from, a fixed set of real cases
with known-good answers, a person, a limit on what the system may do without one.
This is the only place a check has ever fitted, and the reason is not mysterious.
A check needs two things to compare, and this is the only point in the whole
arrangement where two things exist.

<Aside title="The fourth place, which is not a place" tone="caution">
  There is a move that looks like a fourth option and is not one: asking the
  model to check its own answer.

It reads as verification, and what settles it is where the two things being
compared live. The answer is in the conversation. The thing it is being held up
against is the same machine, scoring what comes next. What you have actually done
is append text in which somebody has expressed doubt, and the most plausible
continuation of that is an apology and a revision, which is why a model will
cheerfully retract an answer that was correct.

The unit on hallucination left a test behind for exactly this, and it is the one
to keep hold of. At the moment of checking, name the two things being compared
and say where each of them lives. If both live inside the model, nothing has been
checked.

</Aside>

Read the three lists and notice what is on none of them. Not one entry is a
change to the operation. Whatever you build, the machine goes on scoring the next
piece exactly as it did before you started.

Which is why _the model is the easy part_ is a fair description of nearly every
system that works. Choosing a model takes an afternoon. The search that decides
what it gets to see, the tools it is permitted to call, the limits on what it may
do unsupervised, and the fixed set of cases you measure every change against.
That is the year, and that is the product.

## The word doing the damage is “just”

There is a version of this unit's thesis that is worth refusing.

It goes: it is only predicting the next word. Usually delivered as though it
settles something.

This unit's claim is that the operation is simple to state. It is not that the
consequences are small, and those are not the same claim.

Guessing the next piece well — across a hundred thousand candidates, for any text
at all — turns out to demand whatever it demands, and nobody got to choose what
that was. What it demanded was a machine with a great deal of the structure of
the material somehow settled into it: which words behave like which other words,
which facts travel together, how an argument is assembled, what a stack trace
looks like. Nobody wrote any of that down. It ended up in the dials, in a form no
person can read, exactly as the first unit of this site said it would.

So the honest reading is the one that is harder to say quickly. The operation is
simple, the machine carrying it out is not, and a simple operation carried out
well enough is a machine that behaves as though it knows a great many things
nobody was ever able to tell it.

<Aside title="Where “the next piece” stops being literal">
  This unit has been describing language models, where guessing the next piece
  is not a metaphor. The family is wider than that, and the framing has to be
  held at the right height for the rest of it.

An image generator does not produce a picture left to right. It starts from noise
and takes a little of it away at a time, guessing what the picture would look
like with slightly less noise in it. Nothing about that is next-piece prediction.

Go up one level, though, and it is the same pair of boxes: make a guess, be told
how wrong it was against an example somebody already had, nudge, repeat a few
million times, and then, in use, the second box is gone and nothing checks the
result. What it is guessing changes from one family to the next. That there is a
guess, and that the correcting has stopped, does not.

</Aside>

## The two questions

Which finally gets us to the two questions from the top of the page, and they are
almost too plain to write down.

**What is it guessing?**

**What is holding the guess up?**

Take them to the contract demo. What is it guessing? The next piece of a document
that opens with a contract and the heading _clauses that need attention_. That is
a language job through and through — noticing that a paragraph resembles the
paragraphs that get flagged, and writing a sentence about it in the register a
lawyer would use. The operation is pointed straight at the kind of work the
operation is for, which is why the demo was good and why you should expect it to
go on being good.

What is holding it up? Now you have a list of things to ask, and every one of
them is about somebody else's code rather than about the model.

Does the whole contract fit in the window, or is something choosing which parts to
send, and what happens to a clause that did not make the cut? When it calls a
clause unusual, is that a comparison against a body of contracts, or a sentence
that scores well after the word _unusual_? Where did six come from? What has this
been measured on, other than the demo I am watching — is there a set of contracts
with known-good answers, and what does it score on them? And when it misses one,
who finds out, and how long does that take?

None of those is a trick question. A team that has built this properly has
answers, and the answers are the actual product. A team that has not will reach
for the model instead: it is a very good model, it is the newest one, it scored
well on a benchmark. Notice that not one of those replies is an answer to either
question.

    ```java
    List<Passage> found = search.top(question, 8);            // before
    String draft = model.complete(prompt(found, question));   // the guess
    Optional<Call> call = parse(draft);                       // instead of
    String answer = call.map(tools::run).orElse(draft);

    if (!checks.groundedIn(answer, found)) {                  // after
      return Answer.decline(question);
    }
    ```

    One line of that is the model. The rest is the three places to stand, in the
    order this unit put them, and all of it is ordinary code you write, own and
    can step through in a debugger. That ratio is not an artefact of a small
    example. It is roughly the ratio of every system that survives contact with
    real traffic. Swap the provider behind `model` and this method barely
    notices.

    The line worth staring at is the last one, because `decline` is a branch
    your program has and the machine does not. Delete it and nothing throws,
    nothing fails a test, and the system quietly starts answering questions your
    documents do not cover.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    passages = search(question, k=8)                    # before
    draft = model.complete(prompt(passages, question))  # the guess

    if call := parse_tool_call(draft):                  # instead of
        draft = model.complete(prompt(passages, question, run(call)))

    if not grounded_in(draft, passages):                # after
        return decline(question)
    ```

    Everything interesting that can go wrong lives in the lines that are not the
    model. What `search` failed to return is a ceiling on the answer that no
    model can lift. What `prompt` decided to leave out is invisible in the
    output. What `run` is allowed to do is the whole of your security posture.
    And what `grounded_in` actually compares — that a quoted sentence exists, or
    that the claim built on top of it holds — is the difference between a check
    and a check a fabrication walks through.

    There is a version of this file with the second `if` replaced by a loop, the
    model choosing what to call next and how many times. That is an agent, and
    it is one structural edit away from what is written here.

## Back to the room

The demo, the four seconds, the nodding.

You do not have to be impressed and you do not have to be sceptical. Both of
those are reactions to a performance, and you have spent sixty units learning
that the performance is the one part of this machine that was never in question.
Fluent was always guaranteed. Fluent is what it is for.

What you have instead is something you can ask about anything with a model in it
— a demo, a press release, a colleague's plan, a bill, a headline about what is
coming next year. What is it guessing, and what is holding the guess up?

Ask it out loud. Then listen to whether anyone in the room can answer.

<Checkpoint question="Two teams describe what they are building, and both plan to use the same model with much the same prompt. The first is an assistant that drafts replies to customer emails in the company's voice. The second is an assistant that tells a manager which of their invoices are overdue and by how much. One of these is nearly finished and the other has barely started. Which is which, and if the second team ships anyway, what will their assistant actually do?">
  Put the first question to each of them and the answer falls out. The email
  team is asking the machine to guess the next piece of a reply, given the
  incoming message and some examples of the house voice. A voice is a property
  of text, and matching the shape of text is the operation itself. That system
  is close to finished, and what remains is the third place to stand — somebody
  reading anything that commits the company before it goes out.

The invoice team is asking for facts about one particular world at one particular
moment. Whether something is overdue is a comparison between a date in their
ledger and today, and neither of those is in the machine. The pass over the text
ended before those invoices existed, and there is no today in there at all. So
almost the whole of that system is the parts that are not the model: a query to
the ledger, the arithmetic done by a program, and the model reduced to phrasing a
result it was handed. No prompt installs a ledger.

And if they ship without it, the failure is specific and worth being able to
predict. It will not decline — declining is not a route the machine has. It will
return a well-formed list of invoice numbers, amounts and dates, in exactly the
format they asked for, because a list of overdue invoices is precisely the kind of
thing that plausibly follows that question. Plausible was the entire criterion.
The output will look like the output of the finished system, which is why this
particular failure survives a demo.

Worth carrying past the last page. The two questions have a fixed order. _What is
it guessing_ tells you whether the job suits the machine at all, and _what is
holding the guess up_ tells you how much of the system is still unbuilt. A great
many disappointing projects got the first one wrong and then spent a year working
on prompts.

</Checkpoint>
