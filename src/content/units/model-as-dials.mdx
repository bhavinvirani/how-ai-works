Imagine an old radio. Not a modern one with a screen — the kind with a row of
knobs along the front. You cannot see inside it, and you would not understand it
if you could. What you can do is turn the knobs and listen.

Somewhere in that space of knob positions is a setting where the station comes
through clearly. You find it the way everyone finds it: turn a knob, listen, is
that better or worse, turn again. You never learn what the knobs are doing
electrically. You just find the setting that stops the static.

That is a model. That is the entire idea.

## A machine you can be wrong at

Take a job with a real answer. Someone gives you the floor area of a house and
you have to say what it will sell for.

Build a machine with two knobs. The first says how much each square metre is
worth. The second says what any house is worth before you have taken size into
account at all. Give the machine a size, it multiplies and adds, and out comes a
number.

Now the important part: you can _check_ it. These are houses that already sold,
so every guess can be compared against what actually happened, and the gap
between them written down. Guess £310,000 on a house that sold for £340,000 and
you were wrong by £30,000. Do that across every house and average it, and the
whole machine's performance collapses into a single number.

One number for how wrong you are. That is what makes the knobs turnable with any
purpose, because now "better" means something you can measure rather than
something you feel.

Two things are worth noticing, and the second one matters more.

The first: you cannot get to zero. There is no setting of these two dials that
passes through every point, because houses are not priced by floor area alone —
someone's kitchen was new, someone's neighbour was awful. The best possible
setting is still wrong by about ten thousand pounds a house. A machine that fits
its examples perfectly is usually a machine that has been given too many knobs,
which is a problem with its own name and its own unit later on.

The second: think about _how_ you turned the dials. You almost certainly nudged
one, watched the number, and if it went up you went back the other way. You did
not calculate anything. You did not need to understand houses. You just needed
to be able to tell whether you were getting warmer.

That procedure is mechanical. It can be automated. That is the next unit, and
honestly, that is the whole field.

<Aside title="Two knobs, or a trillion">
  The largest models being built today work exactly like this, with somewhere
  around a trillion dials instead of two. Nothing in the idea changes — what
  changes is that nobody turns them by hand, and nobody can look at any
  individual dial and say what it is for.
</Aside>

## Why the word "parameter" keeps appearing

The dials have a proper name: **parameters**. When you read that a model has 70
billion parameters, that is the number of adjustable settings inside it. Nothing
more exotic than that.

It is also why you cannot ask a model why it said something and get a real
answer. The reason is distributed across billions of numbers, none of which
means anything by itself. There is no line to point at. There was never a line.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    record Model(double perSquareMetre, double base) {
      double predict(double size) {
        return perSquareMetre * size + base;
      }
    }
    ```

    Two fields. Training does not change this class — it changes the two
    numbers you construct it with. A 70-billion-parameter model is the same
    record with 70 billion fields, which is why weights ship as an opaque
    binary blob rather than as source you could read.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    def predict(size, per_square_metre, base):
        return per_square_metre * size + base
    ```

    The function is fixed and trivial. Everything anyone means by "the model"
    lives in the two arguments you pass alongside the input.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="Two people train the same model design on the same houses and end up with different numbers in their dials. Has one of them made a mistake?">
  Not necessarily. There is usually more than one setting that fits the examples
  about equally well, and where you end up depends on where you started and how
  you moved. What matters is the wrongness score, not the individual numbers.

This is worth holding onto, because it means the dials are not facts about
houses. They are just a setting that happens to work, which is why reading
meaning into any single parameter is a mistake.

</Checkpoint>
