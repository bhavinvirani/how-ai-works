The coffee machine at the ferry terminal takes euros. It has taken euros since
it was bolted to the wall, and it takes nothing else, not a card, not a note,
and not the handful of pound coins in your pocket.

Forty metres away, there is a bureau de change.

So you get your coffee, and the machine is exactly the machine it was this
morning. Nobody opened it. Nobody rewired it. It still understands one thing
and one thing only, precisely as well as it ever did. What changed is that
somebody stood between you and it and turned what you were carrying into the
one thing it accepts.

That is very nearly the whole story of how a language model came to be able to
look at a photograph.

## The stack has never seen a letter

It is worth being exact about what the machine you have spent four units
assembling actually receives, because it is not text.

By the time anything reaches the first layer, the sentence has been cut into
pieces from a fixed list, and every piece has been swapped for its position — a
few hundred numbers saying where that piece sits on the map. The spelling is
gone. The words are gone. What arrives is a row of number-lists, in order, and
nothing else at all.

Everything after that point works on those lists and only on those lists.
Attention compares them, blends them, and hands on new ones. Nothing in there
ever asks what a word is, because by the time it runs, nothing is a word.

Which quietly changes what the job is. To feed this machine a new kind of
material, you do not have to make it understand pictures. You have to produce a
row of number-lists. That is a very much smaller thing to ask, and it is the
thing somebody did.

## Sixteen pixels at a time

Start with a photograph. Squash it to a standard size, 224 pixels each way is a
common one, because the machinery gets built once, for one shape, and every
picture after that has to fit it.

Now lay a grid over it and cut. Sixteen pixels across, sixteen down, and no
cleverness whatsoever about where the edges fall: the cuts go where the ruler
says, straight through faces and doorways and whatever else is in the way. A
224-pixel square gives you 14 rows of 14, so 196 pieces. Each one is called a
**patch**, and that is the entire sophistication of the step.

Every patch is already numbers, and always was. A pixel is three numbers saying
how much red, green and blue — that conversion happened in the camera, years
before anybody thought about machine learning. Sixteen by sixteen pixels, three
numbers apiece, is 768 numbers per patch sitting there for free.

Then comes the one learned step. Those 768 numbers are multiplied through a
small block of dials that squashes them down into a position — the same length
of number-list that a word piece already gets. Those dials are tuned by the
ordinary training loop, alongside everything else in the machine. That block is
the bureau de change, and it is the only new part in the building.

Notice what did not happen. The patch did not become a word. Nothing wrote a
caption, and nothing looked the patch up in the vocabulary, because there is no
entry in a vocabulary for the top-left corner of somebody's kitchen. It went
straight to a position, skipping language entirely. What the stack gets is not a
description of the picture; it is the picture, in the only currency the stack
has ever handled.

One thing is still missing, and it is the same thing that was missing for
sentences. A row of positions has no order in it — attention compares
everything with everything and would be just as happy with the squares shuffled.
So each patch gets stamped with where it sat, square 1 to square 196, read
across and down, exactly as the word pieces were.

And that is the end of the modifications. Hand the row over. The stack, entirely
unchanged, does what it always did.

## Sound is one dimension shorter

Sound gets cut the same way, along the single dimension it has.

A recording is already numbers too — how hard the air pushed on the microphone,
measured tens of thousands of times a second. Slice it into chunks a few
hundredths of a second long, push each chunk through its own block of dials, and
each one comes out as a position. A minute of speech becomes a row a few
thousand long.

Whether the pieces were cut off a grid or off a timeline makes no difference
downstream, because by then they are not pieces of anything. They are
number-lists in a row, which is the only material this machine has ever been
able to read.

A model that accepts more than one kind of input is called **multimodal** — a
mode here meaning a kind of material, so text, images and audio are three modes.
It is an imposing word for a stack that never found out.

## What the trick buys

The reason to do it this way is that the alternative is building everything
twice. Before this, a picture and a sentence meant two separate machines, two
separate training runs, and no way to ask a question in one about the other. Now
there is one machine and one run, and the expensive part — the stack, the
attention, the billions of dials — is shared.

**Paperwork in the state it actually arrives in.** A council tax bill
photographed on a kitchen table, a train timetable, a form somebody filled in by
hand. Where a thing sits on the page is half of what it means, and typing the
words out destroys that half before the machine has had a look.

**Screens.** Software that clicks around an application for you is not reading
its source code. It is looking at the same window you are, which is only
possible because a window is a picture.

**Listening and describing.** Turning an hour of recorded conversation into
writing. Reading the label on a jar in a language you do not have. Telling
somebody who cannot see a photograph what is in it.

**Running the conversion the other way.** If a picture can be turned into a row
of positions, a row of positions can be turned back into a picture, which is
what sits underneath every system that produces an image from a sentence.

## What it does not buy

Now the part the enthusiasm skips. It falls straight out of the same paragraphs
that made the whole thing work.

Show a model a photograph of a kitchen and it will describe it well: the light,
the clutter, the era of the cupboards, the cat on the windowsill. Ask it how
many chairs are at the table and it will answer at once, in exactly the same
confident tone, and be wrong far more often than anybody expects.

That is not a gap somebody forgot to fill. Think about what counting actually
requires. You have to hold a running total, find the next chair, add one, and
keep going until there are no chairs left, which means going round again, an
unknown number of times, with somewhere to keep the count while you do.

The last unit was blunt about that. There is no counter in the stack and no way
to write "go round again"; the stack has a length, and every question gets the
same number of blocks. What those blocks do is rebuild each position as a blend
of the others, which is a superb instrument for arriving at "kitchen, cluttered,
afternoon" and a poor one for arriving at "six".

The same reasoning covers the rest of the weak spots, and it is worth seeing
that they are all one weak spot. Getting a number off a chart with forty bars on
it. Text small enough to have landed inside a single square. Which of two
objects is to the left of the other. Whether two lines actually meet or merely
come close. Every one of those is a precise fact about quantity or position, and
every one of them has been blended.

So carry it as a rule of thumb: these models are reliable about what a picture
is, and unreliable about how much of it there is.

They are also not cheap. A photograph arriving as 196 squares is 196 pieces the
machine has to pay for, and a full-resolution screen grab is usually carved into
several times that. Pasting one image into a conversation routinely costs more
than the entire thread of writing around it.

<Aside title="The clean version and the real one">
  The account above is the tidy one, and real systems are messier in a way worth
  knowing about. Many do not push raw patches into the language stack at all:
  the picture goes through a vision model of its own, itself a transformer
  reading patches, trained separately on images paired with their captions, and
  a small learned bridge maps its output into the language model's space. Others
  convert each patch into an entry from a fixed visual vocabulary, so a picture
  really does arrive as something very like tokens.

The shape survives all of it. Cut into pieces, each piece becomes a position,
the stack is not modified. What the variants argue about is how much machinery
belongs in the bureau de change.

</Aside>

## The machine was never about language

Four units ago this looked like a device for reading English. Attention was
invented for a problem about sentences, every example was a sentence, and the
Part you are finishing is named after the thing that finally cracked language.

Read back what the machine actually does, though, and there is not a word in it
about words. It takes a row of positions. It lets every position consult every
other one. It rebuilds each of them as a blend of whatever it found, and it does
that the same fixed number of times, whatever it was handed.

Nothing in that description mentions language, and nothing in it ever had to.
English was simply the first material anybody happened to pour in.

So nothing here was ever taught to see. Somebody worked out how to cut a
photograph into pieces small enough to fit.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    float[][] fromText  = embed(tokenize("a dog on the sofa")); //   5 x 768
    float[][] fromImage = project(patches(photo));              // 196 x 768

    float[][] answer = stack(concat(fromText, fromImage));
    ```

    The declared type is the same on both lines, which is the entire unit. That
    is what makes `concat` legal, and it is why the stack needs no branch for
    one case or the other. Strip the comments and there is nothing in the file
    saying that one of those arrays came from a camera.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    from_text  = embed(tokenize("a dog on the sofa"))   #   5 x 768
    from_image = project(patches(photo))                # 196 x 768

    answer = stack(torch.cat([from_text, from_image]))
    ```

    Worth knowing what those two calls are. `patches` is a reshape — it
    rearranges the numbers already in the image and learns nothing. `project`
    is one `nn.Linear(768, 768)`. The famous "vision transformer" is close to
    those two lines plus the stack you already had.

  </Fragment>

<Checkpoint question="A model is shown a photograph of a car park. It correctly reports the weather, the time of day, and the make of the car nearest the camera — then says there are nine cars when there are fourteen. Given how the picture got in, why should the second answer have been expected?">
  Because the picture arrived as a fixed grid of squares, each squashed into a
  position, and the stack then rebuilt every position as a blend of the others
  in a fixed amount of work. Weather, time of day and the shape of one large car
  are properties of the blend — they survive it. A count is not. Counting needs
  a running total and a way to go round again for the next one, and the machine
  has neither. Nothing miscounted. Nothing counted.

Worth keeping, because the failure is silent and the shape of it generalises.
The number nine arrives in the same tone as everything that was right, and there
is nothing in the answer marking one part as the reliable half. That is the
trade the whole trick makes: a picture becomes readable by being turned into
something the machine can blend, and what will not survive blending is anything
that had to be exact.

</Checkpoint>
