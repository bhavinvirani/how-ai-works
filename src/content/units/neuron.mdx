Somewhere in a filing cabinet there is a scoring sheet. Three points for every
year of experience. Ten if the degree is relevant. Twenty if somebody on the
panel has worked with you before. Add it up, and anyone over sixty gets an
interview.

Nobody defends these sheets and everybody uses them, because they turn an
argument into arithmetic.

They also fail in a way you have probably watched happen. Somebody arrives with
forty years in an unrelated trade, scores a hundred and twenty on the first line
alone, and is through to the interview before the other two lines are even read.
The sheet has no way of saying that forty years is not eight times better than
five. It can only say that more is more.

Keep hold of that sheet. It is not an analogy for what the rest of this part is
built from. It is very nearly the thing itself.

## A scoring sheet with a name

Write the sheet out as arithmetic and it is one line. Each fact is multiplied by
a number saying how much it counts, the results are added together, and one more
number is added on top.

$$
\text{total} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b
$$

In words: take each fact, multiply it by its own dial, add up what comes out,
and then add one more number that arrives whatever the facts happen to be.

Those multipliers have a name you have already met under a different word. They
are **weights**, and they are dials in exactly the sense of the unit before this
one — adjustable numbers that nobody sets by hand and training moves.

The number added on top is called the **bias**, which sounds more sinister than
it is. It is simply where the sheet starts before it has been told anything at
all. On our hiring sheet the bar sat at sixty, so a bias of minus sixty puts the
bar at zero and lets everything after it be a comparison against nothing.

That much — a multiplication per fact, an addition, and a bias — is most of what
a **neuron** is. It is also the entire contents of a scoring sheet, which is the
point. Nothing here is unfamiliar and nothing here is clever.

<Aside title="About that name">
  These were named in the 1940s after a loose sketch of a brain cell. Signals
  arrive, they are weighed, and past some point the cell fires. "Loose" is doing
  an enormous amount of work in that sentence. A real neuron is a living cell
  with its own chemistry and its own timing, and neuroscience has not finished
  describing one. This is three multiplications, an addition and, in a moment, a
  corner. The name is a historical accident, and taking it literally costs you
  something real — it invites you to expect understanding where there is only
  arithmetic.
</Aside>

## The part that is not a scoring sheet

There is one more step, and everything built in this part of the site rests on
it.

Before a neuron passes its total on, it pushes it through a bend.

The bend in almost universal use today is close to embarrassing to write down:
if the total is below zero, send zero; otherwise send the total unchanged. That
is the whole of it. One corner, sitting at zero. It has a formal name, **ReLU**,
which is considerably grander than the arithmetic it stands for.

The bend in general has a name too. **Activation function** is the phrase you
will meet in every description of a model, and it means nothing more than the
shape a total is pushed through on its way out.

On its own that corner looks like a detail — a tidying-up step, the sort of
thing you would skip in a first draft and add later if it seemed to help.

It is the reason the field exists.

## Why a hundred sheets are still one sheet

Here is what you would want from stacking. Build a sheet whose inputs are the
outputs of other sheets, so the second layer works on judgements rather than on
raw facts, and you would expect the pair to say things neither could say alone.

Without the bend, they cannot. Take the smallest possible case. One sheet
doubles its input and adds three. A second sheet takes whatever the first one
produced, multiplies it by five, and subtracts one.

$$
y = 2x + 3 \qquad\qquad z = 5y - 1
$$

In words: the first sheet turns a fact into a judgement, and the second turns
that judgement into an answer. Two layers, honestly stacked.

Now put the first line inside the second and see what survives.

$$
z = 5(2x + 3) - 1 = 10x + 14
$$

The second sheet has gone. What is left is one multiplication and one addition
— two multiply-and-adds in a row are a single multiply-and-add, with different
numbers in it.

Nothing about that depends on the size. A hundred layers of weighted sums, wired
however you like, can always be multiplied out into a single weighted sum — one
multiplication per fact and one addition, a machine that fits on a postcard.

You might object that the hiring sheet already has a bend in it, and it does.
The bar at sixty is as sharp a corner as anyone could want. It is also in the
wrong place. A cut-off applied at the very end, once everything has been added
up, still has a single weighted sum underneath it — all the cut-off does is draw
a line across the answer that sum produced. The bend has to sit _between_ the
layers, on what each one hands to the next, or the layers below it flatten
exactly as before.

So depth without a bend is not slow, or wasteful, or hard to train. It is
pointless in the strict sense: the hundred-layer version can express exactly the
set of answers the one-layer version can, and not one thing more.

Put the bend in the middle and the substitution stops working, because a bend is
not a multiplication and cannot be multiplied out. The second layer is now
handed something the first layer could not have produced by scaling, and every
layer after it widens what the whole thing can say instead of restating it.

There is a plainer way to put what you have bought. A weighted sum is straight in
every fact it is given — more years pushes the total the same way, by the same
amount per year, forever. It has no vocabulary for _enough_. The bend is the
smallest possible thing that gives it one, and "enough" turns out to be most of
what judgement is.

## Take the bend out yourself

Below is a machine with four neurons in it. Three have already been tuned and
left alone; each reads the same three facts about a film and reports one number.
Your three dials sit on the fourth, saying how much each of those reports counts
towards recommending the film.

There are six films, and your own verdict on each. Three of them are identical
except for how much action they have. You would watch the middle one. You would
not sit through the quiet one, and you would not sit through the relentless one.

<TasteNeuron client:visible />

It opens on the sheet everybody writes first — action counts, nothing else does.
That machine agrees with you on three of the six, and the single film it
recommends is the one you said you would walk out of. It is not confused. It is
doing precisely what you told it — one report counts, that report climbs with
action, so the loudest film on the list is the best film on the list.

Getting all six needs one dial below zero. That is the move worth pausing on. A
neuron that only speaks up when a film never lets up, counted negatively, is a
machine saying _too much of this_. Every one of the 788 settings that gets all
six right has that dial below zero, and not one setting without it gets there at
all.

Then switch the bend off.

Nothing else changes — same four neurons, same weights, same six films — and the
line across the plot straightens into a single run from one edge to the other. A
straight line can cross the bar once. It can be under the bar and then over it,
which is "more is better", and it can be over and then under, which is "less is
better". It cannot be under, over, and under again, which is what your taste
actually is.

All 68,921 settings those three dials can take have been tried. With the bend in,
six out of six is reachable in 788 of them. Without it, the best that exists
anywhere on the grid is five, and not one setting gets all three of the
identical-but-for-action films right. Not because the good setting is hard to
find. Because it does not exist.

The readout will also show you the collapse itself. With the bend gone, whatever
your dials say, the whole stack reduces to one neuron reading the three raw
facts — three weights and a bias, printed on the page. Four neurons of
machinery, and four numbers are what is left of them.

## Where you actually meet this

You will never write one of these by hand. In any framework a layer of a
thousand neurons is a single line, and the bend is one word inside it, usually
`relu`. That word is the entire subject of this unit and it is very easy to read
straight past.

The place it becomes visible is when somebody leaves it out. A model built from
weighted sums with no bend anywhere has a name of its own, a **linear model**,
and it is a respectable, widely used thing for problems where the answer really
does keep going the same way as each fact rises. House prices against floor area
is one. What it cannot do is _enough_.

So when a deep stack performs no better than the plainest baseline anyone could
have written, a missing activation is a classic and very quiet cause. The code
looks deep. The machine is not.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    double total = bias;
    for (int i = 0; i < inputs.length; i++) {
      total += weights[i] * inputs[i];
    }

    double output = Math.max(0, total);   // the bend
    ```

    One loop and one `Math.max`. Delete that last line and nothing breaks:
    it compiles, it trains, the loss goes down. What it removes is the only
    reason there is more than one layer, and the symptom is a model that
    plateaus somewhere it never should have.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    total = bias + sum(w * x for w, x in zip(weights, inputs))
    output = max(0.0, total)   # the bend
    ```

    You will not write either line in practice. You will write something like
    `Dense(128, activation="relu")`, where the whole of the second line is that
    last argument, and where leaving the argument out is perfectly legal and
    gives you a layer that adds nothing at all.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="A colleague has stacked six layers of neurons and cannot work out why the model does no better than a single layer did. Nothing errors, training runs, the wrongness score falls. What would you check first, and why would it explain the result exactly rather than approximately?">
  Whether the layers have a bend on them at all. Without one, every layer is a
  weighted sum, and a weighted sum of weighted sums is a weighted sum, so the
  six layers can be multiplied out into one, and the stack can express precisely
  what a single layer can and nothing beyond it. That is why the answer is
  exact rather than approximate. It is not that depth helps less than expected;
  it is that depth is arithmetically the same machine.

It is worth keeping the shape of this failure, because nothing about it looks
like a failure. There is no error, no warning, and no obviously wrong number.
The model trains perfectly happily, all the way up to a ceiling it should never
have had.

</Checkpoint>
