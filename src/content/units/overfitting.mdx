Two people sat the same practice paper last night, and both came out with full
marks.

One had spent a month on the subject. The other found last year's paper online,
noticed the questions had not changed, and spent an evening learning which
letters went in which boxes.

The paper cannot tell them apart, because the paper gave them the same mark. The
only evidence anyone in that room has about who understands the subject points
at both of them equally. Tomorrow's paper will separate them in about ninety
seconds. Until then, nothing will.

Machines can do the second thing. Given the room, they will. And the score you
were watching does not so much as flicker.

## Half of what you measure will never happen again

Here is a small pile of the kind of data machines are actually handed. Twelve
houses on one street: how big each one was, and what it sold for.

Underneath those numbers is something real. Bigger houses cost more, and the
rate tapers off, so the fifth bedroom adds less than the second one did. That is
a pattern, and it holds for the next house too.

Sitting on top of it is everything else. The flat that went cheap because the
kitchen was thirty years old. The one that went high because two buyers wanted
it in the same fortnight. Both are real reasons, and neither will apply to the
next house.

That second layer has a name worth carrying: **noise** — the part of any
measurement that comes from something which will not happen again. Every table
of real examples is a pattern with noise sitting on top, and nothing in the
table marks which number is which.

The machine cannot tell them apart either. Some of the prices it is shown sit on
the pattern and some were shoved off it by a kitchen nobody recorded, and to the
machine every one of them is just a number that currently makes its score worse.

## Freedom is what you hand over

When you turned the dials by hand, the machine had two: one for how steep the
line was, one for how high it started. Two dials buy a straight line and nothing
else. A straight line cannot lean towards one particular house, however much
that would improve its score, because it cannot lean at all.

A third dial lets the line bow once. A fourth lets it bend twice. Every dial
after that buys another wiggle.

Now count. Twelve sales, twelve dials — one adjustable number for every example
in the pile. A curve with that much freedom can be made to pass exactly through
all twelve dots. Not near them. Through them. Its wrongness score on those
twelve sales is then zero: not small, not respectable, zero, with nothing left
anywhere to improve.

That machine has not learned the street. It has written twelve house sales down
in a very roundabout way. Ask it about a house it has not seen and it answers
instantly, confidently, and wrong by more than the house is worth.

## Turn the dial yourself

Below is that street. The slider hands the machine one more adjustable number at
a time. Start at the straight line and push right, watching the only score it
has: how far out it is on the twelve sales it studied. That score improves the
whole way across and never once gets worse.

Then switch on the eight sales that were held back — same street, same month,
never shown to the machine — and run the slider again.

<FlexibilityDial client:visible />

The second score does not slope. It sags in the middle.

At two dials the machine is £21,000 out on what it studied and £24,000 out on
the new sales. It memorised nothing; it never had the freedom to. It simply
could not follow the rise, so it is wrong everywhere at once. That failure has a
name too: **underfitting**.

Three dials is as good as this street gets — around £10,000 out on the twelve,
around £9,000 out on the eight. Roughly the same on both piles, which is what a
machine that has actually learned something looks like.

Push further and the two scores come apart. By seven dials the studied score has
improved again, to £9,000, while the score that matters has drifted out to
£13,000. By twelve the studied score is nothing at all and the machine is
£164,000 out, on average, on houses it has never seen. That is **overfitting**:
the machine fitted the noise along with the pattern, because fitting the noise
also made its number smaller, and nobody ever gave it a way to tell one from the
other.

The alarming part is the middle. Nothing announces the moment it starts going
wrong. From inside, from the only score the machine can see, every one of
those settings is an improvement on the one before it.

## What people actually do about it

The first defence is the one you just used. Keep examples back before training
starts, never let the machine learn from them, and score it on those. It is the
only score in this business that means anything, and every serious team has one.

Once you can see the damage, three things reduce it.

**More examples.** Twelve sales can be memorised. Ten million cannot. There are
not enough dials to hold them, so the only route left to a smaller score is to
find something true of all of them. The most reliable fix by a wide margin.

**Stopping early.** Watch the held-back score while training runs. It falls for a
while, then turns and climbs while the studied score is still improving. That
turn is the moment memorising took over. Stop there.

**Handicapping the machine.** Switch off random parts of it on each pass, or add
a penalty for extreme dial settings. Both make memorising harder without making
the pattern harder, so what survives the disruption tends to be the part that
was really there.

<Aside title="More dials than examples">
  This picture holds beautifully at twelve sales and gets stranger at the top
  end. A large language model has far more dials than its training pile has
  examples — by everything above it should memorise the lot and generalise
  nothing. It does not, and precisely why is still argued about in public. Take
  the sagging middle with you, and treat its far end as less settled than it
  looks.
</Aside>

<Checkpoint question="A colleague reports that their model is 99% accurate on the data it was trained on, and wants to ship it. What do you need before you can agree, and why is the 99% not it?">
  You need its score on examples it was never trained on. The 99% cannot tell a
  machine that found the pattern apart from one that memorised the answers,
  because both of them produce it, which is the one thing that number is
  structurally incapable of telling you.

Worth keeping, because the failure is quiet. A memorising machine does not
stumble or hesitate on a case it has never met. It answers in exactly the tone
it used for everything else, and it is the confidence that gets people, not the
error.

</Checkpoint>
