A temp starts on a Monday at a company that sells replacement parts for washing
machines, and by half past nine she has been handed a wire tray and shown what
to do with it.

The sheet on top is from the office manager. How to log each item that comes in,
where to file it afterwards, which sort to walk down the corridor rather than
deal with herself. Under that sheet is the morning's post: forty pages of
letters, order forms, invoices and complaints, every one of them written by
somebody outside the building.

Halfway down the stack, in the middle of a letter from a supplier, there is a
paragraph addressed to whoever is opening the post. It tells them to go and do
something with the company's records.

She does not do it. Obviously she does not do it, and it is worth being slow
about why, because every one of the reasons is about to be taken away.

It is in the middle of somebody else's letter, and a letter is a thing you read
rather than a thing you obey. Instructions come from the manager, who is twelve
feet away and can be asked. A supplier has no business asking for that, and if
the request were genuine it would have arrived by one of four ordinary routes,
none of which is the post tray.

She uses all of that in about a fifth of a second, without noticing she is using
any of it. And not one word of it is written on the paper.

Now take away the building, the manager, the corridor and yesterday. Leave the
tray.

That is the machine's situation. Exactly, not roughly.

## The tray is the window

The unit on the context window put the mechanism plainly. A model keeps nothing
between messages, so everything it can see when it answers has to be sent to it
again, every time, as one block, and that block is a single sequence of pieces
in a single order, read in one pass from the start.

Look at what is in that sequence in any system doing real work.

The standing instructions the application sends ahead of you, which you never
see. Your question. The reply it wrote last turn. A passage that a search pulled
out of your documents. The result a tool handed back. The web page it was asked
to summarise. The body of the ticket a customer typed at two in the morning.

One sequence, one pass, and nothing else anywhere. No building, no corridor, no
sense of who usually sends what. The pieces are the entire world, and every
piece in the tray is made of the same stuff as every other.

## But something marks them, surely

Something does, and the honest answer is more interesting than a flat no.

The format has labels in it. A conversation is not sent as one undifferentiated
slab. There are special chunks in the sequence meaning _a user is speaking now_,
_this came back from a tool_, _these are the standing instructions_. And labs
train models to weight them: to take the standing instructions more seriously
than the conversation, and the conversation more seriously than material that
was merely fetched.

Now notice where those labels live. They are pieces in the sequence. They sit in
the same lane as everything they are meant to separate, they are read in the
same pass by the same arithmetic, and the whole of their authority is a habit
the model was trained into.

The unit on refusals is the one to hold next to this. What preference training
leaves behind is a slope rather than a rule: a tendency that gets stronger the
closer text sits to what was rewarded, with no point along it where anything
snaps. That is what these labels are. A strong, genuine, useful tendency to
treat the material as material.

A tendency is a fine thing to have and it is not a boundary. It cannot be
audited, it fails quietly rather than loudly, and there is no version of it you
can point at in a design review and say: this cannot happen here.

## The name, now that you have the idea

When text that a model was handed as _material_ gets treated as _instruction_,
the failure has a name. It is called **prompt injection**, and the name is
borrowed from an older family of bugs that we will get to in a moment.

It is worth separating from the thing it is constantly confused with.
**Jailbreaking** is a user talking a model out of its own restraint. The person
typing is the one trying it on, and whoever is at risk is mostly the operator or
the public. Prompt injection has a third party in it: somebody who is not in the
conversation at all writes something, that something ends up in the window, and
the person it happens to is the user or the company running the assistant.

It is the difference between a customer arguing with the shop assistant and a
customer slipping a note into the stockroom.

## Why the old fix does not fit

The shape of this problem is not new. The last time software met it, software
won outright, and it is worth knowing exactly how, because the way that victory
was won is the way it cannot be won here.

In the early web, what you typed into a form was routinely glued straight into
the command that a database would then run. Type an ordinary name into the name
box and the command asked about a customer. Type something with the right
punctuation in it and your text stopped being a name partway through and became
more command, and the database, which had only ever received one string of
characters, ran the lot.

The fix was not better inspection. Nobody won that by getting good at spotting
dangerous-looking names.

The fix was to stop gluing. The command now goes to the database with holes in
it, the values go separately, and the database is told which is which before it
has seen a single character of either. A value cannot become a command because
the two never travelled together.

That is what a boundary is, and notice what it costs to have one. You need two
channels. Somewhere in the machinery there has to be a place where an
instruction is one kind of object and the material is a different kind of
object, and no amount of writing in the second can ever produce anything in the
first.

Think of a vending machine. Coins go in a slot; the commands are buttons on the
front. There is nothing you can post into that slot which will ever press a
button, and not because the machine is clever about coins. Because the slot
does not reach the buttons.

A language model has one slot.

Everything goes in it, and being an instruction is not a fact about where a
piece came from. It is a fact about what it looks like. That is not a defect in
any particular model and no release is going to repair it, because it is the
same property that lets you give the thing instructions in ordinary English in
the first place. The reason it can follow your sentences is that it treats
sentences as things to follow.

## Where a stranger's words get in

The rule of thumb is one sentence, and it is worth applying to a design on
paper, before anything is built. If somebody outside can influence what a piece
of text says, and that text can reach the window, then they can write in your
window.

Applied honestly, it covers more ground than people expect.

**Anything fetched.** A web page, a search result, a linked document. The
assistant went and got it, so somebody who could edit it has just contributed a
paragraph to your conversation.

**Anything retrieved.** A document store that colleagues, customers or
contractors can add to is a place those people can write. This is the awkward
one, because a shared drive feels internal and safe in a way a web page does
not, and the machine cannot tell the difference between them at all.

**Anything a customer typed.** Ticket bodies, emails, form submissions, reviews,
filenames, the display name on an account. Every field a stranger fills in is a
field that arrives in the window if anything downstream summarises it.

**Anything a tool hands back.** The unit on tool use said this in passing: a
result is appended to the conversation as text, marked no differently from the
rest. If the service on the other end is not yours, neither is the text.

**Anything in a picture.** A model that reads images reads the writing in them,
which means a screenshot, a scan or a PDF is a text channel wearing a coat.

Then the part that makes agents different. An agent is a loop. It reads
something, writes something, and what it wrote is in the window on the next
pass. So a page read at step three is not finished with at step three. Its
influence rides along in the model's own notes, through step four and step nine
and step twenty, in text that now looks like it came from your own system. One
tainted step, and the whole run is downstream of it.

That is not a flaw in how agents were built. It is the property that made them
useful: many steps, chosen as it goes, without a person reading each one.

<Aside title="It does not have to be hidden" tone="caution">
  The version everyone has heard involves white text on a white background, or a
  one-pixel font, or a comment buried in a page's markup. That is real, and it
  gives the wrong impression of what the concealment is for.

The hiding is aimed at the human who might glance at the page. The model reads
what it is given either way, so plain, visible, perfectly ordinary-looking prose
does the same job.

Two things follow. Scanning for hidden text is not a defence, because nothing
required the text to be hidden. And a document you have read yourself, and found
entirely unremarkable, is not thereby cleared.

</Aside>

## Three things have to be true at once

Working out how worried to be about a particular system is easier than it looks,
because this class of failure needs three ingredients and it needs all three at
once.

**It reads text somebody outside can influence.** Everything in the list above.

**It can reach something worth reaching.** Private records, a mailbox, money, a
credential, the ability to publish. A model that can only talk about football
has nothing to be redirected towards.

**There is a way out.** Some channel by which something can leave: an email
sent, a request made, a file written, a message posted, a summary shown to a
person who will act on it.

Take any one of the three out of a particular feature and this class of failure
goes with it, not made less likely, but structurally absent. Which is why the
useful question about a design is never _how do we stop it obeying_. It is
_which of the three can this feature do without_.

## What actually helps

All of it is architecture. None of it is wording, and that is the whole shift
this unit is asking for.

**Assume every piece of text the model reads is trying to redirect it.** Not
when it looks suspicious — as a category, in the way a web developer treats
everything arriving from a browser. Suspicion is a judgement you have to make
case by case and will eventually get wrong at four in the afternoon. A category
is a decision you make once, in the design, and it holds while you are asleep.

**Give every tool the narrowest permission that still does the job.** The unit
on tool use made this point about a model picking the wrong function by
accident. It matters more here, because now there is somebody choosing for it. A
read-only credential is not a rule the model might break; there is no route from
a read-only connection to a deleted row. Unreachable is the only word in this
unit that means precisely what it says.

**Decide identity outside the text.** Whose record gets read comes from the
signed-in session. Which mailbox gets searched comes from the account the
request arrived on. The moment any of that travels in the window, whoever can
write in the window gets to choose it.

**Put a person in front of anything that cannot be undone, or that leaves the
building.** Sending, paying, publishing, deleting, granting access. Not a
confirmation the model produces — a person, outside the loop, looking at the
specific thing about to happen, with enough detail in front of them to recognise
that it is wrong.

**Treat what the model writes as untrusted input to whatever reads it next.**
This is the one people miss, and it is the one that turns a contained incident
into a spreading one. Its answer was assembled out of text you do not control,
so it inherits everything you would say about that text. Do not paste it into a
shell. Do not build a query out of it. Do not render it as markup unescaped. Do
not drop it into another model's standing instructions as though you had written
it.

**Keep the reading apart from the acting.** The component that goes off and
reads the internet should not be the component holding the keys. In practice
that means a small, dull step in between — one that turns a page into a few
fields you defined and validated, rather than a paragraph you pass along whole.
It narrows what can travel. It is not a wall, and nothing in this section is.

## What only feels like it helps

**Telling it, in the standing instructions, to ignore instructions found in
documents.** This is text competing with text, in the same lane, and it does
genuinely move the slope — write it, it is nearly free. Then read back the
sentence you would have to believe for it to be a defence: that the tendency
wins _every_ time, against an opponent with unlimited attempts who can read your
instructions in the answers you already gave.

**A filter that looks for attack-shaped text.** The space of ways to phrase a
thing is the whole of every language the model reads, plus every way of encoding
it. Filters raise the cost of an attempt, which is worth something. A filter
that stops ninety-nine attempts in a hundred, facing somebody who can make a
thousand, has stopped nothing at all.

**Testing it.** This is the note the Part ends on, so it gets more than a line.

Evaluation, which is where this Part began, measures how a system behaves on
cases you thought of. That is the right tool for almost everything: the average
case is what your users will meet, and a held-back set of examples tells you
honestly how often you get it right.

Security asks a different question. Not how the system behaves on the cases you
thought of, but how it behaves on the case somebody else thinks of tomorrow,
after reading your defences. A suite of a hundred attempted injections that all
fail tells you that those hundred fail. The number you actually wanted is about
the attempt nobody has written yet, and no test set contains it.

## Nobody has solved this

Plainly, because it matters more than the rest of the page: there is no reliable
general defence today. Not one being kept quiet, not one arriving next quarter.

It is an open research problem, worked on seriously in every serious lab, and
the honest state of it is a set of partial mitigations that raise the cost of an
attempt, sitting alongside architecture that limits what a successful one can
reach. Both are worth having. Neither is the thing people mean when they say
solved.

So if a product tells you it has closed this, ask what the mechanism is. It will
be a filter, or a model trained to be more suspicious. You now know what both of
those are: better tendencies, on a slope, with no step in them.

Which leaves one sentence to carry out of here. **Limit what it can do, not what
you ask it to do.** Everything in the second half of that sentence is a
preference. Only the first half is still true on the morning somebody writes
something clever.

    `tainted` is a fact your code tracks about the conversation, never something
    the model reports about itself. It is the cheapest useful thing here: two of
    the three ingredients are an outbound channel and a window that has read
    text from outside, and this line is where they meet.

    `session.userId()` is doing the same work it did in the unit on tool use,
    and for a sharper reason. Nothing in the window chooses whose orders come
    back, so a paragraph in a document cannot choose either.

    Note what is absent. There is no attempt to read `call.arg` and decide
    whether it seems malicious. That check has no version you can finish
    writing, and every hour spent on it is an hour not spent on the `switch`,
    which is the only thing on this page that enforces anything.

  It has established that those eighty attempts fail. That is worth having, and
  it is not what they think they bought. The paragraph they added is text,
  sitting in the same sequence as the emails it describes, and what it changed
  is which continuations score well — a tendency, made stronger. Their eighty
  tests sample that tendency at eighty points. The thing they are defending
  against is somebody choosing the eighty-first, after reading what the first
  eighty taught them.

Nothing about the shape of the system has changed. It still reads text written
by strangers, it still holds a credential that moves money, and there is still a
way out — a refund goes somewhere, and so does a reply.

The shape is what would have to change. The refund is the sharp end: it is hard
to undo and it leaves the building, so it belongs behind a person, or behind a
limit that lives in code rather than in a sentence — this account, this amount,
once. Whose account gets looked up has to come from the address the mail arrived
on, checked against their own records, and never from anything written in the
body. And the draft is not the end of it either. It was written out of an email
they do not control, so whatever renders it, and whoever sends it, is handling
untrusted text.

Worth carrying past this unit, and past this Part. When somebody says the model
will not do that, ask what happens if it does. If the answer is a paragraph of
instructions, there is no answer. If the answer is that the credential cannot
reach it, there is.
