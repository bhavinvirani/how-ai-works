Somebody in the team chat pastes a screenshot with six words highlighted in
yellow.

_You are a world-class expert in_

Put that at the top, they say. The answers get better. They cannot tell you why,
and they are a little embarrassed to be recommending it — but they left it off
once, the answer was worse, and they have not left it off since.

This is roughly where people are with lucky socks.

The awkward part is that they are not wrong. It does often help, and you can
measure that it helps. So either these machines have a weakness for flattery, or
something a great deal duller is going on.

Something a great deal duller is going on. And once you have it, every piece of
prompting advice you will ever be handed stops being a list to memorise and
turns into something you could have worked out yourself.

## You never asked it a question

An earlier Part left you with a machine that does exactly one thing: read
everything in front of it, and produce the most plausible next piece of text.
Then read the whole lot again, one piece longer, and do it once more. Nothing
else happens in there — no plan, no intention, no moment where it registers that
somebody is waiting.

Now look at what that machine actually receives when you type at it.

Not a question. A run of text that has stopped. And the only move the machine
has is to carry on from where it stopped.

So here is the sentence to carry through the rest of this Part, and it is worth
reading twice: **your prompt is the opening of a document, and the reply is the
rest of that document.**

Not a request that gets fulfilled. An opening that gets finished. That is not a
figure of speech, either. The unit on models straight out of pretraining put a
question into one and got back the rest of a forum page, and a chat app is a
wrapper around that same machine — your messages and its messages flattened into
one transcript, with its own turn just started, handed over with the only
question anybody ever asks it: what comes next.

## Why a vague request gets you the average

Type _explain how a fridge makes things cold_ into an assistant and something
perfectly decent comes back. Heat is moved rather than made. A refrigerant goes
round a sealed loop. The coils at the back are where the heat is let go.
Correct, fluent, and completely flavourless.

It is tempting to read that as the machine hedging. It is not hedging. Those
seven words are the opening of a physics textbook, a children's encyclopedia, a
repair manual, a quiz answer, a lecture, a bedtime explanation and the first
paragraph of an article nobody finished. The same seven words could begin any of
them, and nothing you typed says which.

So what does a machine do when the opening it has been handed fits a thousand
documents? Not toss a coin. It does the only thing it has ever done, which is
produce the most plausible continuation — and the most plausible continuation of
an opening that fits a thousand documents is whatever those documents have in
common. The middle of the pile. Encyclopedic, addressed to nobody, one paragraph
long, for no stated purpose.

That is the correction worth making before any advice arrives. **A vague prompt
does not get you a random answer. It gets you the average one, reliably.**
People describe model output as bland as though blandness were a personality
trait. It is arithmetic.

## Four pieces of advice you can now derive

Here is the standard list. You have met it in a hundred blog posts as a set of
things that work, and a set of things that work has to be memorised, which is
why nobody remembers it. Derive it instead. All four are doing the same job:
removing endings.

**Say who is speaking, and to whom.** A page written for a nine-year-old and a
page written for a trainee engineer are not the same page — different words,
different length, different amount left out — and both are in the pile the model
learned from. Write _you are explaining this to a nine-year-old_ at the top, and
every ending of the second kind stops being plausible, because documents do not
usually announce their reader and then carry on in the register of a service
manual. Nothing was granted to the machine. Something was excluded.

**Say what shape you want.** A document that promises three numbered steps and
then stops has almost no plausible continuation that is not three numbered
steps. This is the bluntest lever there is, and the reason is worth naming: a
heading is a promise about what comes underneath it, and keeping promises like
that is most of what the machine spent its training doing.

**Say what it is for.** Purpose decides content, and it decides more of it than
register does. An explanation for somebody who has to retell it tomorrow opens
with the one sentence worth remembering. An explanation for somebody whose
fridge has stopped opens with the compressor. Same subject, same reader, barely
any overlap.

**Be specific** — and this is the one that always gets listed alongside the
other three as though it were a fourth technique. It is not a technique. It is
the name for doing the first three at all. _Specific_ means nothing more than:
an opening that few documents could plausibly follow.

Which gives you one move to carry instead of a list. Stop wording a request.
Start writing the top of the page the answer belongs on.

## What is real here, and what is drawn small

The panel below holds one request, fixed, and three levers. Each lever writes
one more line underneath the request, and nothing in the panel can edit the
request itself. That constraint is the whole point of the instrument: if the
answers change, the only thing that could have changed them is the document
those seven words are now sitting in.

Real: the mechanism. Naming one thing about a document removes every ending that
disagrees with it. The removal is not a rule being enforced anywhere — those
endings simply stop being plausible continuations of the text as it now stands.
And what gets written is always the commonest of whatever is left, which is why
the machine's answer changes without anything about the machine changing.

Drawn small: the counting. The panel treats the space of endings as three
questions with three answers each, twenty-seven documents in all, so that
narrowing is something you can watch as a number. A real space of continuations
has no number attached to it and no edges, and a real line of framing does not
divide it neatly into thirds — it re-weights it, and some endings that ought to
have gone stay stubbornly plausible. Take the shape of what happens here. Do not
take the arithmetic as a measurement.

## Pull the levers

Nothing is pulled to begin with, and the answer sitting there is worth reading
properly before you touch anything. It is correct. It is well written. It
mentions the refrigerant, the condenser coils and the set point. It is also
addressed to nobody, in a shape nothing asked for, for a purpose nobody stated —
and it is very close to what these tools have been handing you for months.

All twenty-seven endings still fit, so it wrote the middle of them.

<PromptLeverBoard client:visible />

Now pull the first lever, which writes one line: _You are explaining it to a
nine-year-old._ Twenty-seven becomes nine. Watch the top row of the board — the
other two readers go out, and they go out for no reason except that a document
does not name its reader and then ignore it.

Then notice what happened to the words. _Refrigerant_ is gone. Nobody says
_refrigerant_ to a nine-year-old, so every ending containing it stopped being a
plausible continuation of that line. Nothing translated anything. A different
set of endings survived.

Pull the second: _Write it as three numbered steps, one line each._ Nine becomes
three, and one paragraph becomes three numbered lines. The most literal lever on
the board, and the most reliable one.

Pull the third: _They have to explain it to their class tomorrow._ Three becomes
one. This is the lever people leave off, and it is the only one that changes the
content rather than the packaging. The answer now opens with the sentence worth
remembering — a fridge does not make cold, it steals heat — and ends by sending
the child to feel the back of the fridge, because a proof you can perform is the
part that survives until tomorrow.

Then look back at the top of the panel. The request is the same seven words it
was three levers ago. You never asked a different question. You wrote a
different document.

One more thing to notice, and it is the honest one. Pull a lever and **two**
settings on that row are struck out, not one. The board holds a trainee
engineer, a question-and-answer shape and a fault-finding purpose that no lever
on it ever selects — they are there so you can see that naming a thing removes
several alternatives rather than choosing between two. It is also why prompting
has such steep diminishing returns. The first line you add throws away eighteen
of the twenty-seven endings. The third is deciding among three.

## Two things it cannot do

Everything above is steering, and it is worth being exact about what steering is
not — because the most expensive mistakes people make with these tools are made
by prompting harder at a problem no prompt can reach.

**It cannot add knowledge.** Narrowing the plausible endings can only ever pick
among endings the machine could already have produced. If what you want was
never in the pile it learned from — your company's refund policy, what happened
last Tuesday, a paper published after training stopped — then no opening you
write will conjure it, because there is no ending containing it to narrow
towards. What a good prompt _can_ do is reach something the machine holds but
was not otherwise going to reach, and from outside the two are indistinguishable
right up until the answer is confidently wrong.

**It cannot make it check.** There is no step in the loop where the machine goes
and looks something up, and no line you write adds one. _Are you sure?_ does
something real, but not the thing you were hoping for: it produces a document in
which somebody is being challenged, and the plausible continuations of that are
a confident correction or a confident defence. Both are available regardless of
whether the original answer was right. You narrowed towards the shape of
checking, and the shape of checking is not checking.

It cannot make the machine more expert. There is no reserve of expertise being
unlocked, and nothing whatsoever about the machine changed between one prompt
and the next. What the line does is narrow towards documents written by
confident experts — which are, on average, better organised, more specific and
less padded than documents written by nobody in particular. That is a real gain,
and it is why the trick has survived years of people rolling their eyes at it.

The cost is rarely mentioned in the same breath. Documents written by confident
experts also state wrong things confidently. You narrowed towards a register,
and visible uncertainty was part of the register you removed.

</Aside>

## Where you are already doing this

Two places, and you are in both of them today.

Every assistant you use has a prompt of its own, written before yours arrives
and sitting above it in the same document: who it is, what it will not do, how
long its answers should run. Your words continue that. It is most of why the
same underlying model behaves like a different product in two different apps.

And any tool that puts a model behind a form is gluing your input into a
template somebody wrote. The template is the top of the page; whatever a user
typed is the middle of it. That is worth knowing for a reason well beyond
prompting: by the time the model sees it, there is one document, and nothing in
it marks which parts came from the people who built the tool and which arrived
from a stranger.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    String opening = """
        You are explaining it to a nine-year-old.
        Write it as three numbered steps, one line each.

        Explain how a fridge makes things cold.
        """;

    String reply = model.complete(opening + "1.");
    ```

    The interesting part is the `+ "1."`. You are not describing the document
    you want; you have started writing it, and the model has no way to continue
    except underneath a line that begins with a numeral. It is the strongest
    form of everything on this page, and it costs two characters.

    The uncomfortable part is the concatenation above it. Framing and request
    are the same kind of thing to whatever reads this — one string, no field
    marking one half as yours.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    messages = [
        {"role": "user", "content": opening},
        {"role": "assistant", "content": "1."},   # its turn, already begun
    ]
    ```

    That second message is not something the model said. It is the opening of
    the model's own turn, written by you, and it is the reason this technique
    is usually called prefill. Providers differ on whether they allow it, and
    the ones that do tend to reject a trailing space — because the whole
    conversation is one run of text, and a stray space at the end is a piece
    the model then has to continue from.

    Worth knowing that `role` is not a permission. It decides which markers get
    written into the transcript, nothing more.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

Answers get longer, read more authoritatively, and complaints about vagueness drop. Then somebody notices that a wrong answer which used to arrive hedged now arrives flat and certain. What did that line actually do, and what would have helped?">
  It narrowed towards documents that read as thorough and accurate. Every word
  in that description is a property of the writing: longer, more assured, fewer
  hedges, no visible doubt. The line worked exactly the way this unit says lines
  work, which is why the vagueness complaints dropped — that part was real.

What it could not do is narrow towards true. Truth is a property of the world,
and the opening of a document does not touch the world. And the side effect was
not bad luck: hedging was part of what got removed, so the wrong answer did not
get wronger, it lost the signals that used to give it away.

What would have helped is anything that narrows the content rather than the
tone — saying who the answer is for and what they have to do with it, and above
all putting the material the answer depends on into the document, so the ending
they want is a continuation of text that already contains the facts.

Worth keeping as a shape. Whenever a prompt asks for a quality, ask whether that
quality belongs to the writing or to the world. Only the first kind is something
an opening can narrow towards.
