The manuscripts room has closed stacks, which means you never fetch anything
yourself. You write what you want on a slip, hand it across the counter, and
twenty minutes later a runner comes back with a box.

Whatever is in that box is what you will write from. You are good at this — give
you a box and you will have four careful paragraphs out of it by lunchtime.

Which is the whole trouble on the morning the runner brings the wrong box. Right
shelf mark, wrong year. Everything in it looks like what you asked for and none
of it is, and you have no way to notice, because you have never once seen the
shelf.

You will write four careful paragraphs. They will be wrong. And nothing about
writing them will feel any different from the mornings they were right.

The person at the desk is not the weak link in that building.

## Give up on the inside

The unit before this one ended somewhere uncomfortable. There is no step anywhere
inside a language model at which a claim gets held up against anything — no
lookup, no branch, no route that ends in coming back empty-handed. An invented
answer runs through the same arithmetic as a true one, costs the same, and
arrives written just as well.

Nobody has fixed that from the inside, and there is a decent argument that nobody
can. So the thing everyone actually builds does not try. It gives up on the
inside entirely and changes the question instead.

Not _what do you know about the warranty on a Kestrel 400_. Instead: _here are
three passages from the service manual — answer from those._

Nothing about the model has changed. Same dials, same single pass, same column of
scores at the end of it. What changed is the text in front of it, and the text in
front of it was always the only thing deciding anything.

You will meet the initials constantly, so here they are spelled out once.
**Retrieval-augmented generation**, universally shortened to **RAG**. _Retrieval_
— go and find the material. _Augmented_ — the prompt gets that material added to
it, which is the whole of what the word means here. _Generation_ — the model
writes the answer in the ordinary way, from a prompt that now happens to contain
it.

Worth being blunt about what that is not. It is not a kind of model, or a
training technique, or something you buy. It is a search engine bolted to the
front of a prompt. Not one dial moves.

## One half you do once, one half you do every time

Two loops with very different clocks, and most confusion about these systems
comes from mixing them up.

The first happens before anybody has asked anything. Your documents get cut into
passages — a few hundred words each, roughly. Every passage is turned into a
position on the map of meaning, the same trick from much earlier in the site,
where a thing gets a place rather than a name so that things about the same
subject end up near each other. The positions are stored. That is **indexing**,
and the useful thing about it is that when one document changes you redo that one
document and nothing else.

The second happens on every single question, and it is five steps:

1. The question gets a position on the same map.
2. The nearest stored passages are found — usually a handful, sometimes a few
   dozen.
3. Those get narrowed down to the few that actually bear on the question.
4. A prompt is built out of them: your instructions, the passages, the question.
5. The model runs once, in the ordinary way, and writes the answer — ideally
   marking which passage each claim came from.

The model everybody means when they say _the AI_ appears only in the last of those
steps. Read the list again and notice how much of this architecture is not
intelligence of any kind — it is cutting, filing, searching and joining strings
together, with one ordinary call at the end of it.

## Whatever came back is the whole world

Here is the sentence this unit exists for. It sounds duller than it is, which is
why it usually gets nodded at and walked past.

Whatever the search hands over is the entire world the model has for that
question.

Not the main input. The whole of it. A model reads the text in front of it from
the top and produces a score for every chunk in its vocabulary; those passages
_are_ the text in front of it. There is no second source it consults, no memory
of the document it was not given, and — from the previous unit — no step at which
it could register that the passages do not answer the question.

Watch that play out once, in full, because the failure is much quieter than
people expect.

Ask a support assistant how long the warranty runs on a Kestrel 400. The search
returns the three passages nearest the question, and all three are about the
Kestrel 300 — because the two manuals are near-identical, the phrase _warranty
period_ appears throughout both, and the difference between the products is one
character in a passage where every other word matches.

The model reads three passages. All three say two years. So it writes two years,
and cites a page of a real manual that really does say two years.

The Kestrel 400 carries a three-year warranty. It is stated once, on a page that
never got into the box.

Now go through that answer looking for the flaw. It is grounded — every claim in
it traces to a passage that was genuinely supplied. It is cited, and the citation
is real, and if you follow it you will find the sentence. It is fluent, specific
and internally consistent. It is also wrong, and the only thing wrong with it
happened two steps before the model was called.

Try to fix it with a better model. A better model reads those three passages more
carefully, reasons over them more reliably, and writes a more useful summary of
the Kestrel 300's warranty. Nothing you can buy changes which box arrived.

**The search sets the ceiling.** Everything downstream — the prompt, the model,
the instructions, the settings — can lower the quality of an answer. None of it
can lift the answer above the material that came back.

## The runner who never returns empty-handed

The reading room has one edge worth naming, because it is exactly where the
machine is worse than the building.

A real runner can come back and say: we do not hold that. A search cannot. It
returns the nearest passages it has, and _nearest_ is a distance, not a judgement.
Ask about something your documents simply do not cover and you do not get
nothing — you get the three least-unrelated passages in the archive, handed over
in precisely the same way as three good ones, with nothing anywhere in the
pipeline marked _these are not close_.

And then a machine with no step that checks writes a confident answer out of
them.

Which is the previous unit's problem arriving through a new door, and arriving
better dressed. Before, an invention was a fluent answer out of nothing. Now it
is a fluent answer out of real material, with a real reference attached to it.
The defence you added is also, if you are careless, a new way to be wrong that
looks much more like being right.

All of which is why most systems described as _the AI keeps getting things wrong_
are search systems getting things wrong, with the model faithfully reporting what
the search brought it. The complaint arrives pointed at the last step and belongs
to the second one, and the part everybody worries about first is generally the
part that was already working. The model really is the easy part.

## Where the work actually goes

Two things follow from that, and between them they are most of what a team
building one of these spends its time on.

The first is step three of the list — the narrowing. Rather than take the three
nearest passages and hope, you retrieve widely and cheaply, say forty, and then
set something slower and sharper to work on those forty, keeping the four that
genuinely bear on the question and discarding the rest. That second stage is
called **reranking**, and the reason it is worth its cost is plain enough: judging
whether one passage answers one question is a far better-posed job than finding it
among thousands, so it pays to run the expensive version over a shortlist you
could never afford to run over the whole archive.

The second is a measurement that a surprising number of teams never take, because
it does not feel like measuring the AI.

Collect a hundred real questions. For each one, go into the documents by hand and
write down which passage actually answers it. Then run your search and count how
often that passage came back in the set the model would have been handed.

That number involves no model, no prompt and no generation. It is a fact about
the search alone, and it is the ceiling stated as a percentage: if the right
passage arrives 60% of the time, then four questions in ten are already
unanswerable before anything is generated, and no amount of prompt-writing will
move them. Readers of the unit on precision and recall will recognise the shape
of that count — of the things that should have been found, how many were.

Take that measurement first and you learn whether you have a search problem or a
model problem. Skip it and you will spend a quarter rewriting prompts.

<Aside title="Grounded is not the same as correct" tone="caution">
  Handing over the passage removes the largest failure. It does not remove the
  rest, and two survivors are worth naming.

The model can misread a passage it was given. Answering from a source is not the
same as answering it accurately — the previous unit's panel had an answer that
went past its source by subtraction and was perfectly sound, and the same route
runs the other way just as easily.

And a citation is not a check. It is an invitation to check, addressed to a
person who mostly will not. The reference is produced by the same next-piece
scoring as every other part of the answer, so it can name the wrong passage of
the three it was shown. A reference makes verification cheap. It does not perform
any.

</Aside>

## The door you have just built

One paragraph, because it is the failure with the worst consequences and it is
not a model failure at all.

The search runs before the prompt is built, which means the search decides which
of your documents are eligible to appear in front of a model, for anyone who can
type into the box. If that search does not know who is asking, you have built a
very fast way for any user of the assistant to read anything in the archive — the
salary review, the unredacted incident report, the acquisition memo. Nobody has
to ask for those directly. They only have to ask a question those documents
happen to sit near.

The permissions have to live in the search, applied before the nearest passages
are chosen. Filtering afterwards is far too late: by then the material is already
in the prompt, and a prompt is not a place you can take something back from.

## Why not simply train it in

An earlier unit sorted facts and habits into two piles and left you with a rule —
dials for how it acts, prompt for what it has to get right. It also promised that
choosing which of four thousand pages goes into the prompt was a real problem with
real machinery behind it. This is that machinery. Retrieval is the second pile
built out into an architecture, and four properties follow, none of them
available to a training run.

**You can edit it.** The warranty changes on the first of the month. You change a
sentence in a file and re-index that one file, and it takes seconds. In a set of
dials there is no sentence to change, because the fact was never in one place;
the repair is another training run.

**It can point at something.** The passage still exists, as a passage, in a
document with a name. That is what makes a reference possible at all — not the
model knowing where anything came from, but there being something on the other
end of the pointer for a person to open.

**It can respect who is asking.** A search can filter by permission before it
returns anything. A dial cannot be filtered. Whatever went into a training run
went in for everyone who will ever call that model.

**You can change your mind for nothing.** Delete a document and the system knows
nothing about it again, immediately. Nothing was installed, so there is nothing
to undo.

The costs are real and worth stating. Those passages are sent with every question
and charged for on every question, and they eat the same window that has to hold
the conversation. And every answer now has a search in front of it — more
machinery to run, to monitor, and to get wrong. What you are buying with all of
that is not intelligence. It is the ability to be corrected.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    List<Passage> found = index.search(question, 40);
    List<Passage> best  = reranker.top(question, found, 4);

    String prompt = template.render(best, question);
    Answer answer = model.complete(prompt);

    log.info("q={} retrieved={}", question, idsOf(best));
    ```

    That last line is the one you will live on. Every bug report about an answer
    is really a question about `best`, and without it recorded you are debugging
    a black box that is not the black box you think it is.

    The two numbers — 40 and 4 — are the most consequential settings in the
    system and the ones most often left at whatever a tutorial suggested. Raise
    the first and you widen what the reranker gets to consider; raise the second
    and you spend more of the window per call, on passages the reranker already
    ranked lower.

    Worth knowing what a useful test looks like here, because the obvious one is
    not it. Asserting on `answer` gives you a slow, flaky test of a sampler.
    Asserting that a known passage id appears in `best` for a known question is
    deterministic, runs in milliseconds, needs no model, and fails on exactly the
    thing that breaks these systems.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    passages = index.search(question, k=40)
    best = rerank(question, passages)[:4]

    prompt = TEMPLATE.format(
        sources="\n\n".join(f"[{p.id}] {p.text}" for p in best),
        question=question,
    )
    ```

    The bracketed id printed in front of each passage is doing more work than it
    looks. It is the only reason the answer can cite anything: the model cites by
    copying a label it can see in its own input, not by knowing where any
    sentence came from. Drop those labels and you have not made citation harder,
    you have made it impossible — and the model will still produce something
    citation-shaped, because that is what the previous unit was about.

    The other half of the job is the case your template has to survive, which is
    that the four passages do not answer the question. Nothing in this code makes
    _the sources do not say_ a possible output. If your prompt does not make that
    a cheap and acceptable thing to write, the model has no route to it and will
    write you an answer built out of the near-misses instead.

  Both changes act on the last step, and only on the last step. A rewritten
  prompt shifts which continuations score well, which is a real effect and worth
  a little. A larger model reads the supplied passages more carefully and reasons
  over them more reliably, which is also real. Neither one changes which passages
  arrived, and that is where the failure almost certainly happened.

The citation is the tell. If the right page never reached the prompt, the model
is being asked to write an answer that is not in front of it — and having no step
that checks, it writes one anyway, out of the near-misses that did arrive. A real
reference to a genuinely supplied but wrong passage is the signature of a
retrieval miss, not a generation miss. It is what the wrong box looks like from
the outside.

The measurement is retrieval recall, and it needs no model because it is not a
question about generation. Take fifty of the questions that went wrong. For each,
find by hand the passage that actually answers it. Run the search. Count how often
that passage was in the set the model would have been handed.

The value is that it splits the problem in two, and the two halves have nothing in
common. Passage absent — a search problem, and the levers are how the documents
were cut, what the index holds, how many passages are retrieved, whether anything
reranks them. Passage present and the answer still wrong — now it genuinely is a
prompt or model problem, and now the bigger model is worth its price.

Worth carrying past this unit. In any system that looks something up before
answering, the first debugging question is never _what did it say_. It is _what
did it see_.

</Checkpoint>
