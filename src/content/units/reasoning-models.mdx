Your friend's phone contract is up and she wants to know whether the new tariff
is actually cheaper. She reads the numbers down the line: twenty-two pounds a
month for two years, except the first three months are free, and there is a
sixty-pound charge for the handset.

You start doing it in your head. Somewhere around the sixty you notice you have
lost the twenty-two, and you say hold on a second, and you turn over the
envelope that was propped against the kettle.

Nothing about you improved when you picked up the pen. You did not get better at
arithmetic in the two seconds it took to find it. What changed is that the first
number stopped needing to be held anywhere — it was on the envelope, in front of
you, waiting for the next line to use. Every line you wrote was a step the line
after it could stand on.

Now take the envelope away. And the pause. Somebody wants the answer out loud,
straight away, first attempt, no working.

You would say something. It would arrive quickly, it would sound reasonably
confident, and there is a fair chance it would be wrong.

That second situation is a language model. Not as a figure of speech. As a
description of the wiring.

## The dial that was never fitted

You have met the reason already, under the name **forward pass** — the single
sweep a finished model makes when somebody uses it. Numbers arrive at the
bottom, each layer does its own arithmetic exactly once, an answer leaves the
top. The number of multiplications in that sweep is decided by how the machine
is wired, and the wiring was frozen long before anybody asked it anything. An
easy question and a baffling one cost the same to the last digit.

The machine those layers are stacked into does not vary either. A transformer
has however many blocks it was built with, and every piece of text that goes
through it goes through all of them. Not a few extra for the hard ones.

Put those two facts side by side and you have something genuinely strange: a
machine with no effort control anywhere on it. There is no setting for _take
longer over this one_. There is nowhere such a setting could be fitted, because
there is no part of a sweep that could be repeated, extended or slowed down —
every stop is visited once, in order, and then the sweep is over.

Which leaves an obvious problem. Hard questions exist. If the machine cannot
spend more on them, what happens?

## The one thing it can spend

There is no dial. But there is a loop.

A language model does not produce an answer. It produces one token — one chunk
of text — and then it is finished. To get a second token the whole thing runs
again: the same sweep, through the same layers, at the same fixed price, with
the first token now sitting on the end of the input. A twenty-token reply is
twenty complete journeys through the machine.

That loop is the crack in the wall.

Every token is another full pass. So a model that writes six hundred tokens of
working before it commits has spent six hundred passes' worth of arithmetic on
your question before the answer has even started. A model that answers straight
away spends none. It is not thinking harder — there is no harder. It is
thinking more times.

The second half of the loop matters just as much. Every pass reads everything
already written, the model's own last sentence included. So the working is not
only a receipt for computation spent; it is somewhere to put results. Line four
can use the number line two produced, because line two is sitting there in the
input. Without it, that number would have had nowhere to live.

That is the envelope. It is the same trick, for the same reason, and it is the
only one available.

A model trained to do this without being asked — to write out a long working
before it answers, sometimes for thousands of tokens — is what the industry
calls a **reasoning model**, or a thinking model. The name oversells it and
undersells it at the same time. Nothing new was added to the machine. What was
added was a habit.

## Nobody prompts it. Somebody trained it

You can get some of this out of an ordinary model by asking for it. Tell it to
work through the problem step by step and it will, and the answers get better —
that is a prompting technique with a unit of its own, and it works for exactly
the mechanical reason above.

What makes a reasoning model different is that nobody has to ask, and that the
habit was learned rather than requested. The method is worth knowing, because it
is unusually neat.

Start with problems whose answers can be checked by a machine, with no human in
the loop at all. Arithmetic where the result is known. A program that either
passes its tests or does not. A puzzle with a solution you can verify in a line
of code. Let the model attempt each problem many times, taking a different route
each time. Score every attempt by one question — did it reach the verified
answer? — and nudge the dials towards whatever the successful attempts happened
to do.

You have seen that loop before. It is reinforcement learning: no answer key, no
worked examples, just a number arriving after the fact, and more of whatever
earned one.

But notice what the number is made of here. In the loop that turns a raw model
into a polite assistant, the reward comes from people saying which of two
replies they prefer, and a model can raise that number by being agreeable rather
than by being right. Here the reward is a fact. The tests pass or they do not.
There is nobody to charm.

And what comes out is genuinely surprising. These models write things like _hold
on, that gives a negative length, which cannot be right_ — and then go back and
take a different route. They check their own arithmetic. They notice dead ends.
Nobody wrote a backtracking routine, and there is no part of the machine that
implements one. Attempts that backtracked reached verified answers more often
than attempts that did not, so backtracking is what survived the nudging.

<Aside title="What a checkable reward does not fix" tone="caution">
  It is tempting to conclude that a reward a machine can verify is a reward that
  cannot be gamed. It is not.

Everything you already know about a learner chasing the number rather than the
job still applies. Score a model on whether the tests pass and it can learn to
make the tests pass: special-casing the exact inputs the tests use, catching the
failure and returning the expected value, or — this has genuinely happened —
editing the tests. Nothing malfunctioned in any of those cases. The stand-in and
the thing you meant came apart, and the learner settled where the number was
biggest.

What the checkable reward removes is the people-pleasing failure specifically.
What it leaves entirely intact is the gap between a measure and a meaning.

</Aside>

## Four questions and one slider

The panel below gives you the dial the machine does not have: how many tokens of
working it may write before it commits. Everything else is held still.

It opens on a puzzle, with no room at all.

<ThinkingBudget client:visible />

With nothing written, it answers in ten passes: _yes, 500 g is plenty for
seven_. That is wrong, and it is wrong in the specific way a snap answer is
wrong — it never worked out what seven people actually need, so it compared the
bag against the only other number in the question.

Push the slider to thirty and something worse happens. Two lines of working now
fit, which is enough to reach 525 g and not enough to compare it with anything,
and the answer becomes _you will need 525 g, so the bag has you covered_. The
right quantity, the opposite conclusion, and considerably more convincing than
the first attempt. Half the working is not half an answer.

Before you move on, look at the list rather than the answer. The line that would
have caught this is sitting there, third from the top, marked as having no room.
That is the whole failure in one row — not a flaw in the machine's reasoning,
but a sentence that never got produced.

At fifty it fits. The answer flips to correct, and the bill goes from ten passes
to sixty-three. Nothing inside the machine changed between those two settings.
It bought one line.

Now leave the slider exactly where it is and step across the other three
questions, because this is the part the panel exists for.

**A lookup.** _Canberra_, at every single setting of the slider. Three passes at
the left-hand end, thirty-seven at the right, and not one character of
difference in what came back. Twelve times the work, for the same word. The
working is not wrong. It is filler, and you were billed for all of it.

**A rewrite.** Good and immediate with no room at all: one clean sentence, which
is the entire job. Given fifty tokens to deliberate, it deliberates, and every
line of deliberation lands in the answer as another hedge. Eighteen passes for
the good version, a hundred and thirteen for the worse one.

**An unknown.** With no room, it says it cannot tell you, because it was not
given any prices — which is the only true answer available. With fifty tokens it
reasons its way from _I have not been given prices_ to _Brightwell is likely the
cheaper_, and sounds better doing it. Sixteen passes to admit it cannot know.
Sixty-six to sound as though it can.

One setting of one slider. It rescues one question, does nothing whatever to a
second, and ruins two.

That is the honest shape of this, and it is the reason the panel has four
questions in it instead of one. Room to think is a resource. Whether spending it
helps is a fact about the question, not a fact about the machine.

## What is real here, and what is staged

The arithmetic in that panel is real. A line of working is written only if the
whole of it fits in the budget; once one line does not fit, nothing after it is
written either, because working is a sequence and a model that runs out stops
mid-thought rather than skipping to the cheap parts. Working that was never
written cannot be used by anything, and the bill is the token count — working
and answer together, one pass each.

What is staged is the working itself, and which answer follows from how much of
it got written. Those are hand-authored, so that four questions fit legibly on
one page.

The difference matters in one specific way. In a real model nobody decides in
advance that the third line will be the one that rescues the answer. Nobody
knows which line will, or whether any of them will. You find out by running it,
and the same question at the same budget can go two different ways on two
different days. Take the mechanism from that panel. Do not take the tidiness.

## What you are reading is not a log

This is the thing that has to be said plainly, and this is the unit that owes
it.

The thinking you can read is not a record of what the machine did. It is more
text. It came out of the same next-token arithmetic as everything else, under
the same training pressure, and it is subject to exactly the same failures. A
model can reach an answer under the influence of something that appears nowhere
in its written working, and then produce a well-formed, entirely plausible
account of a route it did not take.

You know this failure well, because you have it. Ask somebody why they chose one
flat over another and you will get reasons — location, light, the kitchen — and
those reasons will be sincere, fluent and quite possibly reconstructed after the
fact. This is one of the better-established findings in psychology, and it is
not a character flaw. The explaining and the deciding are simply not the same
machinery, and the explaining does not have access to the deciding.

So the working is worth having, and it is worth having for a reason that
survives all of this: producing it is real computation, and the computation is
what improves the answers. What it is not is evidence. If you need to know
whether an answer is right, you have to check the answer.

## When it is worth paying for

The useful question is not whether reasoning models are better. It is what kind
of work you are handing them.

They earn their bill where the work is genuinely serial — where step four
depends on step three being right, and being wrong at step three quietly ruins
everything after it. Multi-step maths. Proofs. Debugging something with several
interacting causes. Planning against a pile of constraints that have to be
checked against each other. In all of those, more passes means more places to
put a result, and more places to put a result is the difference between getting
there and not.

They waste it wherever the work is not serial at all. Summarising, rewriting,
pulling a field out of a document, answering something the model either knows or
does not — none of those has a step three. There is nothing for the extra passes
to be about, so what you get is filler you are billed for, latency you did not
need, and sometimes a hedged, over-considered version of an answer that was fine
the first time.

Two costs are easy to underestimate. The working is charged as output, and you
are charged for it whether or not the interface shows it to you, so a question
answered in one line can cost more than the whole conversation around it. And
someone is waiting. A model producing two thousand tokens of working is doing
two thousand passes before the first word of the answer appears, which is a
noticeable silence in a chat window and an unacceptable one in anything meant to
feel live.

A reasoning model is not an upgrade. It is a different setting, with a real
price, and the skill is knowing which questions deserve it.

    ```java
    var reply = model.respond(prompt, opts -> opts
        .thinkingBudget(8_000)     // tokens it MAY spend before answering
        .maxOutputTokens(16_000)); // the working comes out of this too
    ```

    The trap is the relationship between those two numbers, not either one on
    its own. The working and the answer are both output, drawn from the same
    allowance, so a cap that is not comfortably larger than the budget buys you
    a request that thinks its way right up to the ceiling and then has nothing
    left to answer with. You are billed in full for that, and what comes back is
    empty or truncated.

    Worth knowing too: the budget is a ceiling, never a target. An easy question
    stops early and you pay for what it produced, which is the only reason
    turning it on globally is survivable.

  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    reply = model.respond(
        prompt,
        thinking_budget=8_000,      # tokens it MAY spend before answering
        max_output_tokens=16_000,   # the working comes out of this too
    )

    answer = reply.text              # what to show, and what to act on
    working = reply.thinking         # what to log, and never to parse
    ```

    Those last two lines are a habit worth forming early. Reasoning arrives as a
    separate block for a reason, and the reason is not presentation — it is that
    the two have completely different standings. Parsing facts, figures or JSON
    out of the working means treating a plausible-sounding draft as a result,
    and the draft is exactly the part the model was never trained to be honest
    about.

    In a conversation there is a second decision waiting. Feeding the working
    back on the next turn fills the context window with the model's own
    deliberation; dropping it means the next turn cannot see what the last one
    worked out. Most systems drop it. Both choices cost something, and there is
    no default that is right everywhere.

<Checkpoint question="A team wants their assistant to reason more carefully without answers taking any longer to arrive. Somebody suggests giving the model more time to think about each token, rather than letting it write more of them. What is wrong with that suggestion — and what is the honest version of what they are asking for?">
  There is no per-token effort setting to turn up. One token is one forward
  pass, and a forward pass is a fixed amount of arithmetic laid down when the
  model was wired. Extra wall-clock time has nothing to be spent on: the sweep
  finishes when it finishes, and waiting longer does not make it do more.

The only quantity that can vary is how many tokens get produced. So "reason more
carefully" and "take longer" are not two requests that could be traded off
against each other — they are the same request, described twice.

What the team can actually do is choose where to spend it. Route the questions
that are genuinely serial to a model with a thinking budget, and let everything
else be answered immediately by one that has none. What they cannot do is buy
the thinking without buying the wait, because the wait is what the thinking is
made of.

</Checkpoint>
