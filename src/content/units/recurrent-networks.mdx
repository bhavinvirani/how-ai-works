Your flatmate rings from the letting agent's office and reads out the reply to
your complaint. It arrives as one long sentence, and they are not going to stop
in the middle of it so you can catch up.

You are not writing any of it down. What you have, while they read, is a single
running impression of the message — updated on every word, and never any bigger
than it was a word ago.

They finish. You have the end of it, which was something about the boiler, and a
general sense of whether this is good news. The name of the street, which was the
third thing they said, has gone. You did not mishear it. It was written over by
everything that came after.

For most of the time anyone has been teaching machines to read, that was the
method. Not a rough approximation of the method — the method, the best there
was, and it worked.

## The machine that reads out loud

A machine has a fixed shape. So many inputs, so many dials, all decided before it
has seen a single example. A sentence refuses to co-operate with that: this one
is nine words long and the next one is sixty.

The obvious way out is the way you would do it yourself. Do not try to take the
sentence in all at once. Take it one piece at a time, and keep a running sense of
what you have read so far.

That is exactly the design. The machine is handed one piece of text — a word, or
a fragment of one — along with its own summary of everything before it, and it
produces an updated summary. Then the next piece. The same machinery does every
step, so nothing is added for a longer sentence; it just goes round more times.

The loop is where the name comes from. A **recurrent neural network** is a
network that feeds its own output back into itself, so what it worked out at word
four is part of what it is looking at during word five. Almost everybody shortens
it to RNN.

The running summary has a name too, and it is worth knowing because it is where
all the trouble lives. The **hidden state** is a fixed-length list of numbers —
five hundred of them, say — holding everything the machine still knows about
everything it has read. Not a transcript. A compression, rewritten from scratch
at every word.

Fixed-length means what it says. Five hundred numbers for a four-word sentence.
The same five hundred for a four-hundred-word one.

## The beginning is the first thing to go

Folding a new word into that summary is not free. The result has to come out the
same size it went in, so making room for the newest word means giving something
up, and what gets given up is whatever has already survived the most foldings.

Nothing dramatic happens at any particular moment. A word simply accounts for a
little less of the memory after every step, and a little less again, until what
is left of it is too faint to act on.

Here is the shape of the damage. Take the sentence:

_The keys to the cabinet in the hall by the back door **were** missing._

One word in there decides whether the second-to-last word is "was" or "were", and
it is **keys** — the second word. By the time the machine reaches the point where
it has to choose, that evidence is eleven steps back. Worse, every noun since has
been singular: cabinet, hall, door. A reader working mainly off the last thing it
saw will write "was", confidently, and be wrong.

The information is not gone. It is in there, thinned out and blended with eleven
other things — which is a different problem from being gone, and in practice not
much of an improvement on it.

That picture is a rough one — the memory is not really sliced into per-word
strips, and no two sentences fade at the same rate. What is exactly right about
it is the shape: same box, more claimants, and the oldest claimant loses.

This was diagnosed early and nobody left it alone. In 1997 the **long short-term
memory** network — LSTM, and you will still meet the acronym — added a set of
small learned switches at every step. One decides how much of the incoming word
is worth writing down, one decides what of the existing memory to keep, one
decides what to pass on. Once a machine can choose to hold on to something, that
thing stops fading by default.

It worked, and it is worth being clear about how well. LSTMs ran machine
translation, speech recognition and phone keyboards for the better part of two
decades. They were not a stopgap that everybody was embarrassed by. For a long
stretch they were simply the best that anything could do with a sentence.

What a switch cannot do is make the box bigger. It changes what gets thrown away.
It does not change that something has to be. Hand an LSTM a paragraph instead of
a sentence and you are back in the same arithmetic, conducted with better
manners.

<Aside title="The same problem, seen from the training side">
  Training runs backwards. The machine makes a mistake at the end of the
  sentence, and the blame for it is handed back down the chain, one step at a
  time, to everything that contributed. Each handover multiplies that blame by a
  number. Multiply by something under one, eleven times over, and what reaches
  the second word is not a correction — it is a rounding error. So a long-range
  connection is not only hard to use at reading time. It is hard to *learn* in
  the first place, because the lesson never arrives intact.
</Aside>

## The half that decided it

The forgetting is the famous complaint. The other one is the reason none of this
is what your phone is running today.

Look at the loop again. Word five cannot start until word four has finished,
because word four's output is part of word five's input. That is not a slow
implementation waiting for somebody clever. It is what the method is. Take the
ordering away and there is no method left.

Now look at what the hardware became. The chips that made modern AI affordable
are not one very fast processor. They are thousands of small ones on a single
card, and they only repay their price if you can hand them thousands of
independent jobs at the same instant. That is the bargain on offer: enormous
throughput, on condition that nothing waits for anything.

A sentence read one word at a time takes that bargain and turns it down. However
many processors are standing by, the words go through one behind the other.

There is a partial answer, and people used it constantly: read many different
sentences side by side. That does keep the card busy, and it is the reason these
models could be trained at all.

What it never does is finish one sentence sooner. The number of steps in a
training run is the number of words in the text, taken in order, and that number
does not fall when you buy more hardware.

Sit with what that meant for a field where nearly all the progress was coming
from making things bigger. Every other part of the work got faster when the chips
got faster. This part did not — and the long inputs, which were the ones people
most wanted to handle, were the ones that waited longest.

So the ceiling was never really about accuracy. It was about cost. You could not
push enough text through one of these, fast enough, to find out what a very large
one would have been like, and by the middle of the 2010s that had become the only
question anybody wanted answered.

## Where you would still meet one

None of this makes recurrence a bad idea, and it did not retire when it stopped
being the headline.

Where input arrives one piece at a time and never stops — a sensor on a factory
machine, the chip in a speaker listening on a battery for its own name, a reading
you have to answer before the next one lands — a small memory that updates once
per step is exactly the right shape. It costs the same on the millionth reading
as on the tenth, and it never has to hold the whole history at once. That is the
one thing the method which replaced it cannot say for itself.

The trade is live enough that recurrence has been coming back, in newer designs
that keep the running memory but make each step far cheaper, and can be
rearranged into something parallel for training. The idea was never the problem.
The shape of it was.

## Both complaints, one cause

Everything the machine knows about word three reaches word forty by being passed
through thirty-seven intermediate summaries. The passing is what costs. It is
what thins the information out, and it is what forces the queue.

Which invites a question that took a surprisingly long time for anyone to take
seriously. Why is word forty only allowed to look at the summary? Why can it not
look at word three?

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    double[] state = new double[512];      // the whole memory, sized once

    for (String token : sentence) {
      state = cell.step(state, token);     // same weights on every pass
    }

    Answer answer = readout.apply(state);
    ```

    Three facts are visible in six lines. The array is sized at its declaration
    and nothing about a longer `sentence` enlarges it. `cell` is the same object
    every time round, so a longer sentence buys more passes rather than more
    machine. And the loop body reads `state` and writes `state` — which is why
    there is no version of this that becomes a `parallelStream()`. It is not a
    tuning decision somebody got wrong; there is genuinely nothing to hand to a
    second thread.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    state = zeros(512)              # the whole memory, sized once

    for token in sentence:
        state = cell(state, token)  # same weights on every pass

    answer = readout(state)
    ```

    The `for` is not a convenience here. It is the architecture. Anything you
    would normally reach for to make a loop like this go faster — vectorising it,
    handing slices to a pool — needs the iterations to be independent, and these
    are the opposite of independent by construction.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="Two teams have the same budget and buy the same thousand processors, and both train on the same pile of text. One team's model reads a sentence one word at a time; the other's can look at all the words at once. Why does only one of them get most of what it paid for?">
  Because the first team's method has a chain of dependencies inside every
  sentence. Step five cannot begin until step four has produced its summary, so
  those steps cannot happen at the same moment however many processors are
  standing idle. The extra hardware can only be spent on reading more sentences
  side by side — never on finishing one sooner. The second team's work has no
  such chain, so every processor goes straight into the same job.

The general shape is worth keeping. When a method stops improving it is often not
because the idea was wrong, but because the shape of the idea does not fit the
machine it has to run on. The version that wins is very often the one that can be
cut into pieces.

</Checkpoint>
