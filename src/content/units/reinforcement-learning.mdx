Your neighbour's puppy jumps up at everyone who comes through the front door.
Yours used to. It took about three weeks to stop.

You never explained anything. You and the dog share no language in which "keep
all four paws down when a stranger arrives" could even be said. What you had was
a doorway and a pocket of cheese.

The dog did something. Four paws down, cheese. Otherwise you turned away and it
got nothing. Twenty times a day, for three weeks.

It was never told the rule and never shown a correct example. It tried things,
and noticed which of them were followed by cheese.

## No answer key, and no pile of examples either

That is a third way of learning. No table of examples with the right answer in
the last column, and no dataset at all — just a situation, some things the
learner could do, and a number that turns up afterwards saying how it went.

It has a name, **reinforcement learning**, and five plain words for its parts.
The learner is the **agent**. The world it acts in is the **environment**. What
it can do are its **actions**. The number coming back is the **reward**. Its
habit of choosing — what it tends to do, and when — is its **policy**. In chess:
the player, the board, the legal moves, plus one for a win and minus one for a
loss, and however it makes up its mind.

## The number arrives late, and it covers everything

You win a chess game on move sixty. Which move won it?

Move eleven might have been the quiet one that made everything afterwards
possible. Move forty might have been a blunder your opponent failed to punish.
The score says nothing about either. It is one number for the whole game.

Untangling which decisions earned the credit is the central difficulty here, and
its name says exactly what it is: **credit assignment**. Something you did
worked. Good luck finding out what.

## Look around, or cash in

There is a second difficulty, and unlike the first it never gets easier.

Say the learner has found something that works. Keep doing it and it will never
discover the thing that works better, because the only way to find that is to do
something else and see. But every turn spent doing something else is a turn not
spent on the good thing.

The two sides of that have names — **exploring** and **exploiting**: trying
something new, and taking the best you currently know about. No learner ever
stops having to balance them.

New job. Four lunch places on the street, twenty-four lunchtimes, and one
decision — how many of those lunchtimes go on trying places in turn, before you
settle on whichever looked best. It opens on four, one lunch each, which feels
thorough.

<ExploreExploitBandit client:visible />

One lunch each is not thorough. It is one sample, and one sample is mostly luck.
The Corner Kitchen had a good day and scored 8; the Noodle Stall had a bad one
and scored 5. On that evidence you hand twenty lunchtimes to the second-best
place on the street, and the best one carries on being best without you.

Push the slider up and nothing improves, not at five, not at eight. At nine
there is finally enough evidence to overturn one good day, and the average jumps
from 5.92 to 7.17 in a single notch. Keep going and it falls away again, because
every extra day of looking is a day not spent eating well. At twenty-four you
never settle at all and score 6.00: exactly what you scored by barely looking,
for the opposite reason.

Even the best setting reaches 7.17, against the 7.96 you would score if somebody
told you the answer on day one. That gap is what not knowing costs, and no
strategy takes it to zero.

<Aside title="This street is the easy version">
  There is no credit assignment problem in it — one choice, one score, and which
  caused which is never in doubt. Real reinforcement learning rarely looks like
  that; a robot arm makes two hundred decisions before anything measurable
  happens. What does survive into the hard cases is the tension you just felt —
  finding out and cashing in always come out of the same budget.
</Aside>

## What it is actually getting better at

The learner does not want what you want. It has no idea what you want. Nothing
about your intention ever reached it — the only thing that crossed the gap was a
number. So it does not get better at the job. It gets better at making the
number large.

Most of the time those are the same thing, which is precisely why this is
dangerous. In 2016 a learner was set loose on a boat racing game and scored on
points collected — a reasonable stand-in for racing well. It found a lagoon
where three pickups kept regenerating, and spent every race turning circles
there, repeatedly catching fire, never finishing the course, and outscoring
every human player. Nothing malfunctioned. It did what it was asked.

That is **reward hacking**. The number is always a stand-in for the thing you
care about, because the thing you care about is usually not measurable, and a
learner free to act will find where the stand-in and the real thing come apart.
It settles there, because that is where the number is biggest.

You have met a milder version: whatever you measure is what the machine becomes
good at. It bites harder here because a machine fed a fixed pile of examples can
only be wrong about that pile, while a learner that acts decides what happens
next. It can go looking for the gap.

## Why this sits in a guide about chatbots

You would think it belonged to robots and board games. It is in fact why a
modern assistant feels helpful rather than like a stretch of the internet read
aloud.

Nobody can write down the correct reply to your question; there are millions of
good ones and no way to specify them. But a person shown two replies can say
quickly which is better, and that comparison is a score. Feed enough of them
through this loop and you shape behaviour nobody could have described.

The warning comes with it. You get a model tuned to produce replies that people
pick, and whether the reply people pick is the reply that is true is a separate
question, which nothing in this loop is asking.

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    while (true) {
      Action action = policy.choose(state);        // try something
      double reward = environment.step(action);    // a number comes back
      policy.update(action, reward);               // do more of that
    }
    ```

    Look at what `step` returns. One `double`, not the action you should have
    taken, not a gap to close, not an explanation. Every difficulty in this unit
    follows from that return type.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    while True:
        action = policy.choose(state)
        reward = env.step(action)
        policy.update(action, reward)
    ```

    Same three lines. `env.step` is where the entire world lives, and a single
    float is everything it ever says back.

<Checkpoint question="A cleaning robot is scored on how much dust it collects, and it starts tipping the bin out onto the floor. What has gone wrong?">
  Nothing, which is the uncomfortable part. Nobody told the robot to keep the
  house clean. Nobody could write that down, which is why it was given a score
  instead. It was told collected dust is worth points, and tipping the bin out
  is the cheapest way to collect dust.

The score was a stand-in for what you wanted, and the robot found where the
stand-in and the real thing come apart. Expect this rather than being surprised
by it: anything that learns by acting ends up wherever the number is biggest,
and the number is never quite the thing you meant.
