Somebody is in the room asking you for a few hundred million pounds.

The money is for a machine that does not exist yet. Building it will take
months, and enough electricity that the site had to be picked with a particular
substation in mind. When it is finished, nobody in the room can tell you what it
will be able to do. Not approximately — at all. The strongest sentence anyone
can offer is that the last few have come out better than the ones before them.

That is a bet, and nobody places it twice.

So the question that mattered was never whether bigger machines are better. It
was whether anyone could say **how much** better, and say it before the money
went out of the door. Around 2020 several groups went looking, and what they
came back with is most of the reason the last few years happened.

Start with what they measured, because it is narrower than it sounds. Not
intelligence, and not usefulness. One number: how surprised the model is by the
next chunk of text in a document it has never seen. Cover the next token, ask
for a guess, and score how much probability it put on the piece that actually
came. That is the same wrongness score training runs on, taken on held-back
text — and it is the only quantity in this unit that anybody can measure
cleanly.

Three things were varied against it. How many dials the model has. How much text
it was shown. How much computing time went into the run. Push any of the three
up and the number comes down, which surprises nobody.

What was worth an announcement is the shape.

## Multiplying, not adding

Add ten per cent more dials and essentially nothing happens. The number twitches
in the fourth decimal place, and you would struggle to tell it from noise.

Multiply the dials by ten, and it drops by a definite amount. Multiply by ten
again — a hundred times the machine you started with — and it drops by very
nearly the same amount a second time. Then a third.

That is a different claim from "bigger is better". It says the return is not
tied to how much you add but to how many times you multiply, and that equal
multiplications buy equal improvements all the way along. A relationship of that
shape is called a **power law**, and it has one property worth all the rest:
draw it on a graph whose axes multiply rather than add, and it comes out as a
straight line.

A straight line is the only shape in the world you can extend with a ruler.

Fitted to real models and real piles of text, those lines are what the field
calls its **scaling laws** — a rather grand name for a handful of measured
curves which turned out to hold over four or five multiplications, and which
nobody has a first-principles explanation for even now.

$$
\text{wrongness} \;\approx\; \text{floor} \;+\; \frac{c}{\text{size}^{\,\alpha}}
$$

In words: how wrong the machine is comes to a floor it will never get under,
plus a second part that shrinks as the machine grows. The $\alpha$ tucked in the
corner is the exchange rate — it says how much of that shrinking part one
multiplication of size buys you, and $c$ is just the scale the whole thing is
measured in. Neither was derived from anything. They were read off real runs,
the way you would measure the stretch of a spring.

The floor has earned a paragraph of its own. Some of what comes next in a piece
of text is genuinely unguessable: the name of a person you have not met, the
figure on somebody's receipt, which of two equally good words a writer happened
to reach for. A flawless machine would still be wrong about all of it. The curve
is heading somewhere, and that somewhere is not zero.

## Nobody builds the big one first

Here is how that line is actually used, and it is more mundane than the numbers
around it suggest.

You do not start with the expensive thing. You build a ladder of cheap ones —
four or five models, each deliberately small, each finishing in hours rather
than months — and you measure every one. Then you plot the points, check that
they sit on a line, and read the line forwards to the size somebody is thinking
of paying for.

That is the whole method, and it is also what keeps it honest. If the points do
not fall on a line, you have found that out before spending anything, which is
the cheapest bad news on the market.

And it works. The final wrongness score of at least one frontier model was
published as a prediction before the expensive run began — worked out from small
models using something like a ten-thousandth of the computing — and the finished
machine came in on the figure.

The line settles a second argument too, and this is the one that decides
budgets. Computing time is what you are really buying, and there are two things
to spend it on: a bigger model, or more text through a smaller one. The two
trade directly against each other, because a larger model reads fewer words in
the same hour.

For about two years the field got that trade wrong. The early work leaned
towards size, so models were built enormous and fed comparatively little. Then
the same measurements, done more carefully, said the split had been badly off: a
model four times smaller, shown more than four times as much text, on an
identical bill, came out better. Nobody had built a worse machine through bad
luck. They had spent the money in the wrong proportions, and the correction was
worth more than a great deal of extra hardware.

The rule of thumb that came out of it was roughly twenty tokens of text for
every dial in the model. It has moved again since, for a reason worth carrying:
that rule makes _training_ as cheap as possible, and training happens once.
Answering happens every time anybody asks a question, and a smaller model
answers cheaper for ever. So the habit now is to overshoot deliberately — take a
smaller model and pour far more text through it than the training-optimal split
calls for — because paying extra once beats paying a little extra a billion
times.

## The part the line said nothing about

Now the awkward half.

That curve is about one number, and the number is how surprised a machine is by
text. Nobody wants that. What people want is a list of things the machine can
do — and when the field went and checked those, the tidiness fell apart.

Some abilities did not creep in. They were missing at one size and present at
the next, with nothing in between. Adding two three-digit numbers and getting
the whole answer right. Carrying out a task in a notation that was invented in
the prompt itself, three lines earlier. Transliterating a name from one alphabet
into another. On each of these, model after model scored nothing at all — and
then one scored.

The name for that is **emergence**: an ability that is absent below a certain
scale and present above it, with no gentle fade in the middle.

It left the field in a strange position. The forecast worked beautifully for the
one number nobody cared about, and said nothing whatsoever about the things
everybody cared about. You could promise a lower wrongness score at a given size
and mean it. What the machine would be able to _do_ at that size was not
something anyone could work out on paper, and the only instrument that could
answer the question was the machine itself.

A floor you could guarantee, and a ceiling nobody could see. That is a fairly
precise description of why so much money moved so quickly.

## Or is it the way we count?

Here is the argument against, and it is a good one.

A child does not learn to walk on a Tuesday. What happens on the Tuesday is that
she crosses the room, and crossing the room is the thing somebody writes in a
book. The six weeks before it — hauling herself up on the sofa, sidestepping
along it, letting go for a second and sitting down hard — were the ability
arriving, at an entirely ordinary speed. The date in the book is a threshold in
the record-keeping.

Now make that precise. Take a task with five steps in it, where the answer only
counts if all five are right, which describes an enormous number of the tests
these models are scored on. Suppose that across five sizes of model the chance
of getting any one step right climbs perfectly steadily: 20%, 36%, 52%, 68%,
84%. Sixteen points at every size. There is nothing sudden anywhere in that
sequence.

Now mark the whole answer instead. If the steps are roughly independent of one
another, the chance of all five surviving is that figure multiplied by itself
five times — which gives 0%, 1%, 4%, 15% and then 42%.

Three sizes of nothing, and then a cliff. Same models, same answers, same
afternoon. The only thing that changed is where the marks came from.

So the sceptical case is that a fair number of reported emergent abilities are
manufactured this way — the ability climbing smoothly all along, and an
all-or-nothing score converting a slope into a step. Re-score several of the
famous examples with partial credit and the suddenness does soften.

The case on the other side has two halves. Not every example softens: some
capabilities still show a sharp change when they are measured with graded
scores, and nobody has explained those away. And there is a fair objection to
the whole framing, which is that an all-or-nothing score is not a measurement
error when the job itself is all-or-nothing. If your system needs the entire
answer right, then the size at which entire answers start arriving is a real
event in your life, whatever was happening smoothly underneath it.

Where that leaves you is not a verdict, and you should be wary of anyone
offering one. The wrongness curve moves smoothly; that half is not in dispute.
Whether anything else does is genuinely unresolved, and it is quoted with total
confidence in both directions by people who know that it is.

The transferable part is smaller than the debate and more useful than either
side of it. A threshold in the measuring turns a slope in the world into a cliff
on the chart, and it does it silently — it appears nowhere in the numbers,
nobody consciously chose it, and it stays invisible until somebody goes and
looks at what is being counted.

## What scaling did not fix

Worth being specific about, because "bigger models are better" gets used to
imply that everything about them got better.

**It did not make them truthful.** The thing being improved was the ability to
guess what text comes next. Text that sounds right and text that is right are
two different targets, and only one of them was ever on the scoreboard. Bigger
models did become more accurate about the world — there is more of the world in
more text — but they became fluent faster than they became correct, which means
the gap between how right an answer sounds and how right it is got wider rather
than narrower.

**It did not give them a source.** Nothing in the training ever asked a model to
keep a note of where anything came from, so no amount of size produced a machine
that can tell you which page a fact is on. What size produced instead is a
confident-looking citation, composed on the spot in exactly the manner of every
other plausible continuation.

**It did not teach them where their knowledge stops.** A machine that has never
been asked to notice the edge of what it knows does not pick up the habit by
getting larger.

**And it did not make them usable.** A model straight out of a big training run
is a machine that continues text. Hand it your question and it will cheerfully
continue with five more questions, because a page containing one question
usually contains several. Everything that turns that into something you can talk
to happens afterwards, and none of it is scale.

<Aside title="Where the curve ran out" tone="caution">
  Everything above describes a period rather than a law of nature, and the
  period has largely closed.

The text ran short first. The curves want ten times more of it at every step,
and there is not ten times more good writing to be had — the supply of careful,
edited, non-duplicated text is finite, and it was already being scraped hard.

The bill did the rest. A power law with a small exponent is a polite way of
saying that each further multiplication costs ten times as much for the same
sized drop, and sooner or later the drop stops being worth the cheque.

Most of the improvement of the last couple of years has come from somewhere
else: better and better-filtered text rather than simply more of it, better
training methods, and getting a model to do more work on each question it is
asked instead of building a bigger machine to answer it. The curves are still
true. They are just no longer the cheapest thing to buy.

</Aside>

<Checkpoint question="A team swaps in a model ten times the size, and their score on a benchmark goes from 4% to 42%. Someone says the larger model has unlocked an ability the smaller one did not have. Before you agree, what is the cheapest thing you could check — and what would each result mean?">
  Re-mark what you already have, with partial credit. Nothing needs retraining
  and nothing needs rerunning; the smaller models' answers are still on disk.
  Score them per step rather than whole, and look at the shape.

If the graded score was climbing steadily the whole way — roughly the same
amount at every size — then the ability had been growing all along, and the jump
happened in the marking rather than in the machine. If the graded score is flat
and then jumps too, the sudden reading has survived its cheapest test and is
worth taking seriously.

Either way the team's own experience really did jump, because their benchmark
demands whole answers and so does whatever they are building. That is the part
worth keeping separate: how the ability moved is a claim about the model, and
when it became useful is a claim about the threshold somebody set.

Worth keeping, because the shape recurs everywhere. When something appears to
switch on all at once, ask what is being counted before you ask what changed.
