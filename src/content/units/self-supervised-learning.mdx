The job advert asked for patience and good eyesight. Six hours a day at a
screen: a photograph appears, you draw a box round every car in it and type what
each one is, and then the next photograph arrives. On a good day you get through
nine hundred.

Somebody has to. A machine that learns from examples cannot begin until the
examples have their answers written on them, and writing them is hand work: one
person, one picture, one answer at a time. That is where the money in these
projects goes.

Now suppose what you want is not cars in photographs but language. Not fifty
thousand examples — something closer to every sentence anybody has ever written
down. There is no team, no budget and no year long enough, and for a long while
that was where the road ended.

## The thumb trick

Take an ordinary sentence. Not one written for a machine; one somebody wrote
because they had something to say.

_She put the milk back in the fridge._

Cover the last word with your thumb.

You have just written an exam question. You are also holding the marked answer,
because it is under your thumb. Nobody wrote that answer, nobody was paid for
it, and nobody had to be asked. The word was there all along. You only hid it.

Move your thumb one word to the left and you have a second question, with its
answer, out of the same eight words. There are eight questions in that sentence
— one for every word — and the sentence cost nothing, because it was never
written for you in the first place.

## Where the answer key came from

Keep counting and the arithmetic stops being a parlour trick. A paragraph is a
hundred questions. A book is a hundred thousand. Every news report, every
recipe, every forum reply and every set of assembly instructions ever typed is a
stack of questions with the answers already attached.

The last unit left you with a table, and a person pointing at one column of it
and saying: this one is the answer. Here nobody points. Cover whichever word you
like and that word becomes the answer — and the moment you uncover it again, the
marking is done.

The move has a name, and it can have it now you have already done it.
**Self-supervised learning** is training where the correct answer comes out of
the data rather than out of a person. "Supervision" is this field's word for
having something to mark a guess against; here the text marks itself.

It is not a gentler kind of training. The machine still guesses, still gets
scored on how far off it was, still has its dials nudged by the same arithmetic.
One thing changed: where the answer key came from. That change took the ceiling
off.

## What the guessing game drags in with it

Filling in blanks sounds like a game, and for years it was treated as one. Why
would a machine that plays hide-the-word end up able to hold a conversation?

Because of what it takes to win. Look again at what you needed in order to put
"fridge" back: that milk is kept cold, that a kitchen has a cold box in it, that
"back in" means it had come out of there earlier. The sentence says none of
that. You supplied it.

Other gaps ask for other things entirely.

- _The pavement is still wet, so it ——— last night._ Cause and effect, run
  backwards. You never see the rain, only the mark it left.
- _The first people walked on the ——— in nineteen sixty-nine._ A fact, though
  nobody ever wrote it down as a fact.
- _He read the letter twice, folded it, and began to ———._ Something about
  people, and about what letters do to them.

Nobody set out to teach a machine kitchens, or history, or grief. Each one got
in through the back door, because each one makes the guessing less wrong — and
less wrong is the only direction training ever pushes.

Both kinds cost the same to make, which is nothing. That is the part worth
sitting with. The hard gaps — the ones needing a kitchen, or a cause, or a date
— are no rarer and no dearer than the easy ones, and a machine chasing a lower
score has to get better at all of them.

## What this looks like in a real system

One detail worth having straight, because it is the version you will actually
meet. Most systems do not hide a word in the middle. They hide the end: show the
machine everything up to some point in the text, make it guess what comes next,
then move along one word and do it again.

Same trick, one position — and it has a convenient side effect. A machine
trained to guess what comes next can be handed the beginning of something and
asked to keep going, which is what you are doing every time you type into a chat
box.

<Aside title="It is not learning without answers">
  You will sometimes see this filed under learning with no answers at all. It is
  not. Every gap has one correct word, checked against the text the instant a
  guess arrives, and marked as strictly as anything a person had written. What
  is missing is the person, not the answer.
</Aside>

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    String[] words = Files.readString(Path.of("corpus.txt")).split("\\s+");

    for (int i = 1; i < words.length; i++) {
      String question = String.join(" ", Arrays.copyOfRange(words, 0, i));
      String answer = words[i];        // the label, and nobody wrote it
      train(question, answer);
    }
    ```

    There is no second file. No `labels.csv`, no annotation tool, no queue of
    people. That loop is the entire data-labelling pipeline, and it runs at the
    speed of reading a text file.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    words = Path("corpus.txt").read_text().split()

    for i in range(1, len(words)):
        train(question=" ".join(words[:i]), answer=words[i])
    ```

    Same shape, fewer characters. The most expensive line in a supervised
    project is the one that obtains `answer`, and here it has become an array
    index.

<Checkpoint question="Somebody tells you a large model was trained on 'unlabelled' text. What is misleading about that word?">
  Nothing about it was unlabelled. There was a correct answer for every word —
  billions of them — each checked as strictly as a human-written one. What was
  missing is the person who would have had to write them.

That distinction is the whole reason this worked. Calling the text unlabelled
hides the trick: the method kept the sharp, checkable feedback you only get from
having a right answer, and paid none of the cost of producing one.
