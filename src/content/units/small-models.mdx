Six emails came in overnight. Put each one in a box — billing, delivery,
returns, technical, account, other.

- _You charged my card twice on Tuesday._
- _Tracking says delivered and it is not here._
- _Wrong size, I need to send this back._
- _The app closes itself when I open settings._
- _Please change the email address on my login._
- _Do you donate prizes to school raffles?_

That took about eight seconds and you were not concentrating.

Now notice what you did not use. Not a word of French. Nothing about mortgages,
nothing about the offside rule, no opinion on the causes of the First World War.
The job asked for one narrow thing and left the rest of everything you know
sitting idle.

Most of the work people actually hand these machines looks like that. Route the
ticket. Pull the date out of the email. Decide whether this sentence is a
complaint. Tag it, sort it, extract it, wave it through.

And the machine most teams reach for to do it knows all the other things too,
and charges for them.

## The curves never said what people heard

Everything so far has pointed one way. More dials, more text, more electricity,
a better machine — measured, plotted, and straight enough to extend with a
ruler.

Read what was on the axis, though. One number, and it was how surprised a model
is by the next chunk of text, averaged over an enormous pile of writing.
Averaged over _everything_: legal drafting, physics, recipes, chat, source code,
poetry. That average came down as machines grew, and most of the coming-down
happened where there was somewhere to come down from.

Which is the sentence worth slowing over. Improvement needs room, and room is a
property of the job rather than of the machine.

Take the six boxes. Suppose a small model gets the email into the right one
ninety-one times in a hundred. Multiply its dials by a hundred and the very best
machine anyone has built gets ninety-three. Two points. Not because the big one
is not better — it is enormously better — but because it is better at things
this job never asks about. Nothing can be more right than right.

Now take a forty-page contract and the question of which two clauses contradict
each other. A small model manages about a third of those. There are two thirds
sitting on the table waiting for something, and that is where a hundred times
the machine shows up in the numbers. That is where the curves were pointing all
along.

Bigger is better at the hardest tasks. Nobody ever showed that you need the
hardest-task machine for an easy task, and it does not follow.

The figures in that drawing are shaped rather than measured — your own job will
give you different ones, and getting your own is the point. What is not
illustrative is the shape. Easy jobs have narrow gaps. Hard jobs have wide ones.
The gap is what you are buying when you pay for a bigger machine, and on a great
many real jobs there is barely a gap to buy.

## Small enough to keep

The term is **small language model**, and in practice it means one thing: small
enough to run on hardware you already have. A laptop, a phone, one ordinary
server in a cupboard — rather than a rack of specialist chips in somebody else's
building.

In dials that is roughly a few hundred million to a few tens of billions,
against hundreds of billions for the largest hosted models. The boundary moves
every year and nobody defends it very hard. The test underneath it does not
move: can you run the thing yourself?

## Why they stopped being toys

A small model from a few years ago was a demonstration. A small model now does
work, and the change did not come from adding dials.

Some of it is diet. Early training runs took roughly whatever text could be
scraped. Current ones are far fussier — deduplicated, filtered, weighted towards
writing that actually explains something — and a model shown better text for
longer arrives somewhere that a larger model on worse text did not.

The rest of it is that the big models taught them. You can have a large model
produce a great many answers and train a small one on those instead of on raw
text, and what comes out is far better than its size would suggest. That is the
next unit's entire subject.

## Three things that are not money

The bill is what sends people looking. It is rarely what keeps them there.

**The data never leaves.** A hosted model means your text goes over a wire into
somebody else's building. For plenty of work that is fine. For medical notes,
legal files, unreleased results, or anything covered by a rule that names which
countries data may be processed in, it is not a preference — it is the thing
that decides whether the project happens at all. A model running on your own
machine turns the question off. There is no request to log, no retention policy
to read, no region to check, and no conversation with your own lawyers about a
supplier's subprocessors.

**The answer arrives in a different amount of time.** A hosted call is a network
round trip, plus a queue you are sharing with strangers, plus the generation
itself. A small model on the machine doing the asking has none of the first two.
The gap is usually the difference between something you wait for and something
that happens while you type — and that changes what you can build rather than
making the same thing nicer. At a fifth of a second you can put a model inside a
loop: one call per row of a spreadsheet, one per pause in typing, one per field
of a form as it is filled in. At two seconds you build a thing with a spinner in
it, and every decision downstream of that is different.

**It can be tuned for your job, cheaply.** This is the one people do not expect.
Training a small model a little further on examples of one narrow task —
**fine-tuning** — costs an amount that would not buy the team lunch, and the
examples are usually already sitting in a database. Ten thousand support tickets
that humans have been routing correctly for three years is a training set
nobody had to build. What comes out has your product names in it, your
abbreviations, the way your customers actually write. On that one job it will
frequently beat a general model a hundred times its size which has seen none of
that. Not because it is cleverer. Because the giant is working it out from the
prompt every single time, and the small one has it in the dials.

<Aside title="The version that stays where you put it">
  There is a quieter benefit that only shows up months in. A hosted model is
  updated by the people who host it, and the update arrives whether or not you
  were ready for it. One morning the same prompt returns something differently
  shaped, and your carefully measured accuracy has moved for reasons entirely
  outside your building.

A model you run yourself is a file on a disk. It does not change unless you
change it, which means a result you measured in March still holds in November,
and a regression is always something you did. For anything you have to certify,
audit or defend, that is worth more than the price difference.

</Aside>

## What you give up, said plainly

A small model is genuinely worse at some things, and the honest list is short.

**It holds less of the world.** Facts thin out first, and the obscure ones go
first of all. Ask about a small town, a minor statute or a mid-list novel and a
small model has much less to draw on.

**It holds fewer steps together.** Chains of reasoning come apart sooner. Three
linked deductions are usually fine; eight are usually not.

**It frays at the edges faster.** Long inputs, unusual formats, sloppy
instructions, anything outside the shape it was tuned for — a small model
degrades more sharply than a large one when conditions get strange, and the
degradation starts earlier.

And it will be exactly as confident about all of it. Nothing here repairs a
machine that has no step for checking anything; a smaller one simply gives you
more occasions to wish it had one.

So the useful question was never small against large. It is which requests go
where.

## The arrangement production systems actually use

Send everything to the small model first. Most of it stops there. Then have
something read what came back and decide whether this particular request needs
to go on to something larger.

That is a **cascade** — or just routing, which is what most people call it. A
great many shipped systems are doing it behind a single address that looks, from
the outside, like one model answering everything.

The arithmetic is worth writing down once, because there is a term in it that
people leave out:

$$
\text{bill} \;=\; n \, c_{\text{small}} \;+\; m \, c_{\text{large}}
$$

In words: all $n$ requests pay the small model, and the $m$ that got sent on pay
the large one on top. The small model's price appears once for the whole batch —
including for the ones that ended up escalating anyway, which were run through it
first and then run again.

Put numbers on it. A hundred requests, and the large model costs fifty times the
small one. If the gate sends twelve of them on, the hundred cost two units at the
small model plus twelve at the large one — fourteen, against a hundred if
everything had gone straight to the giant. Just over seven times cheaper, and
that is before anything is said about where the data went.

Read the two things that got worse, because they are not accidents and they do
not go away with better engineering.

The escalated twelve are now **slower**, not faster. They waited for the small
model, failed, and then waited for the large one. Routing buys an enormous
saving on the common case by making the rare case worse, and if the rare case is
the one your most important customer is sitting in front of, that is a decision
somebody should make on purpose rather than discover.

And the gate is not perfect. Some of the eighty-eight were beyond the small
model and were answered anyway. Nothing anywhere in that diagram raises a hand
about it.

## Everything rests on the gate

Detecting "this one is beyond me" is itself a judgement, and the obvious way to
make it does not work.

The obvious way is to take the small model's own confidence and escalate
anything below a threshold. But that number measures how strongly the model
preferred what it said, given the text in front of it — not whether it was
right. A small model can be entirely wrong and entirely confident, and those are
precisely the cases the gate existed to catch.

So a gate that works is built out of things you can actually check, and most of
them never ask the model anything:

- The **shape of the request** before it is answered. How long it is, whether it
  contains a table, how many separate questions it asks, whether it is in a
  language you tuned for.
- Whether the answer **parses**. If you demanded four named fields and got three,
  that is a fact about a string, and it is free.
- Whether **two runs agree**. Ask twice and compare. Things with plenty of
  material behind them come back the same way; things the model is filling in
  wander.
- A **held-out set of cases where you know the right answer**, scored on every
  change to the gate. This is the only one that tells you whether the gate is
  working at all, and it is what the unit on evaluation is entirely about.

Get the threshold wrong in one direction and you escalate nearly everything,
which is the large model plus the overhead of having asked the small one first.
Get it wrong in the other and you have built a system that quietly answers the
hard questions badly and reports that all is well. Both of those are cheap to
detect with the last item on that list and impossible to detect without it.

<Aside title="Running it yourself is not free" tone="caution">
  "No bill" is the wrong summary and it catches people out at exactly the wrong
  moment. There is no invoice per request, which is a real and large saving.
  There is still hardware to buy, a machine to keep running at three in the
  morning, models to update, and somebody whose job it now is.

The sharper trap is that "runs on a laptop" and "serves four hundred people at
once" are two different sentences. One model on one machine answers one request
at a time reasonably quickly and a queue of forty very badly indeed. Serving is
its own engineering problem, and it is the part that surprises teams who
prototyped happily on a laptop and then met a Monday morning.

The rule of thumb that survives contact with reality is that self-hosting wins
on volume and on data that cannot leave, and loses on spiky, occasional traffic
where somebody else's idle machines are cheaper than your own.

</Aside>

## Where this leaves the decision

Start from the job rather than the model.

Ask how much room the job leaves — how far a small model already gets, and how
much is left above it. On the narrow, high-volume, well-defined work that makes
up most of what anyone builds, the honest answer is: not much room, and the
small model is already in it. Ask what the data is, because if it cannot leave
the building the question is settled before cost is mentioned. Ask what the
latency has to be, because a fifth of a second and two seconds are different
products.

Then, for whatever is genuinely hard, keep something large in reserve and route
to it deliberately.

The reflex worth breaking is reaching for the largest available model by
default, on the grounds that it cannot hurt. It can. It costs fifty times as
much, it answers ten times slower, it takes your data somewhere else, it changes
under you without notice — and on an easy job it is right about two per cent
more often.

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    Answer answer = small.answer(request);          // every request, always

    if (gate.beyondIt(request, answer)) {
      answer = large.answer(request);               // paid for twice, by design
    }
    return answer;
    ```

    The line that decides whether any of this was a good idea is
    `gate.beyondIt`, and the tempting implementation of it —
    `answer.confidence() < 0.8` — is the one that fails silently. That number
    says how strongly the model preferred what it produced, not whether it was
    correct, and a small model that is out of its depth is frequently very sure.

    Build the gate out of things you can assert on instead: whether the answer
    deserialises into the record you demanded, whether a required field came
    back empty, whether the label is one of the six you allow, whether the
    request is longer than anything in your tuning set. Then fix the threshold
    against a labelled holdout rather than by feel, and re-fix it whenever
    either model changes.

    Note also what the second call does to your timeout budget. The escalated
    path costs `small + large`, so a timeout sized for the large model alone
    will start failing the very requests you routed most carefully.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    scored = sorted(holdout, key=lambda case: gate_score(case))
    for cut in thresholds:
        escalated = [c for c in scored if gate_score(c) < cut]
        print(cut, len(escalated) / len(scored), accuracy(scored, cut))
    ```

    Three lines, and they are the whole of the work. You are not choosing a
    threshold, you are reading a curve: what fraction gets escalated at each
    cut, and what the end-to-end accuracy is once the escalations are answered
    by the large model. The cut you want is where the accuracy curve flattens,
    and it is almost never the round number somebody would have guessed.

    Two things this makes visible that intuition does not. The escalation rate
    at your chosen cut is your bill, directly — it is the `m` in the formula
    above. And the accuracy at 100% escalation is the ceiling you are comparing
    against, which is the large model's own score and not 100%.

  Run both over the same set of emails whose correct department is already
  known. They have years of routed tickets, so the set costs nothing to build
  and nobody has to argue from first principles. The colleague's claim is true
  in general and it is not a claim about this job — how much room this
  particular job leaves is a measurement, and it takes an afternoon.

Say the small model comes back at 91%. That is three points, and the argument
has changed shape completely: it is no longer whether a smaller model is worse
but what three points of routing accuracy are worth against a fiftieth of the
bill, an answer that arrives while the email is still being read, and customer
correspondence that never leaves the building. Those are business questions with
owners, and the technical dispute is over.

Two things they should do before concluding anything. Compare like with like —
the small model should be tuned on their own routed tickets first, because that
is the version they would actually run, and it is the version most likely to
pass 94%. And look at _which_ emails each one gets wrong. If the small model's
mistakes are concentrated in one department that happens to be the expensive
one, the headline number is hiding the thing that matters, and the fix is a
gate that escalates that department rather than a bigger model for all eleven.

Worth carrying beyond this unit. Whenever somebody argues from model size, the
question that settles it is not how capable each model is. It is how much room
the job leaves — and that is a property of the work, measurable on data you
already have.
