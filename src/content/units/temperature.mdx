A colleague sends you a screenshot. They asked the assistant for the three
biggest risks in a supplier contract, and it came back with a clean, sensible
list. You paste the same question into the same assistant four minutes later.
Same words. Same document.

You get a different list. Two of the three match. The third is something else
entirely, and it is worse.

Nothing changed in between. Not the question, not the document, not the model.
Nobody deployed anything.

The natural conclusion is that it thought about it again and came out somewhere
else. It did not. Run that question a thousand times and the machine works out
the same opinion a thousand times — it is the same arithmetic over the same
numbers, and arithmetic does not have moods.

What differs is one step that happens **after** the opinion, and there is a
single dial controlling it.

## Something still has to choose

The unit before this one ended on a row rather than a word. At every step the
model does not produce the next piece of text; it produces every piece that
could come next, each with a share of the probability, all of them adding up to
one.

A row of percentages is not text. Something downstream has to turn it into one
piece, and there are only two honest ways to do that.

**Always take the largest.** Straightforward, and completely repeatable: the
same question produces the same answer today, tomorrow and next year. It also
has a specific failure that anyone who has tried it will recognise. Text written
this way has a habit of getting stuck — repeating a clause, then repeating it
again — because the piece that most often follows a phrase is sometimes the
piece that starts the phrase over, and a machine that always takes the largest
has no way to break out. The deeper problem is that such a model has exactly one
answer to any question, for ever. Ask it for a subject line ten times and you
get the same subject line ten times.

**Draw one at random, in proportion to the shares.** A piece holding 55% of the
row gets picked 55% of the time, one holding 5% gets picked 5% of the time.
Variety comes free, several attempts at the same question are genuinely
different attempts — and every so often something from the bottom of the row
gets through.

Neither is right for everything, which is why the choice was never made for you.
It was left on a dial.

## The dial is not where you think it is

Here is the part almost every explanation gets wrong, so it is worth being
slow about.

What the model hands over is not percentages. It is a **raw score** for each
piece — an unbounded number, higher meaning "more like what usually comes next
here". Those scores are turned into percentages by one standard step called
**softmax**, which takes any row of numbers and returns a row of shares: all
positive, the largest still the largest, adding to exactly one.

There is one number in the request that acts on all this, and it does exactly
one thing. Every raw score is divided by it, before softmax runs.

That is the whole mechanism. The number is called the **temperature**, borrowed
from physics, where a hotter substance jostles about more — an analogy worth
exactly that much and no more.

Divide by something small and the scores are pulled further apart, so the leader
runs away with the row. Divide by something large and they are squeezed closer
together, so the row flattens out towards giving every piece an equal chance.

Now notice what dividing cannot do. Divide every number in a list by the same
amount and the biggest is still the biggest, the smallest is still the smallest,
nothing has been added and nothing has been removed. **The order is identical at
every setting of the dial.** A piece the model rated third best is third best at
0.1 and third best at 2.

So "turn the temperature up to make it more creative" is describing something
other than what it sounds like. Nothing in the machine has become more
imaginative, more knowledgeable or better. The model's opinion was finished
before the dial was consulted. All that changes is how much of the row is left
over for the pieces it did not rate first.

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{s_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{s_j / T}}
$$

Read in the words above: a piece's share $p_i$ is its own raw score $s_i$
divided by the temperature $T$, run through the exponential, and then divided by
that same quantity added up across every piece so the row comes to exactly one.
$T$ appears in one place only, and it is the same $T$ for every piece — which is
precisely why it cannot promote anything.

Divide one piece's share by another's and everything except the difference
between their two raw scores cancels out, leaving $e^{(s_1 - s_2)/T}$. The odds
between any two pieces are the gap between their scores, divided by the
temperature. That is the entire effect of this dial, in one expression.

Put numbers on it. In the panel further down, the model scores _mild_ at 3.1 and
_cold_ at 2.4 — a gap of 0.7 that it produced once and never revisits. At a
setting of 1, _mild_ is twice as likely as _cold_. At 2, 1.4 times as likely. At
the very bottom of the dial, about eleven hundred times as likely. The same two
scores in all three cases.

## Turn it yourself

Everything in the panel below is the real arithmetic: raw scores, divided by the
temperature, through softmax, drawn in proportion. What is staged is where the
raw scores come from, and the previous unit said so plainly — a real model works
them out by running the whole machine over everything written so far, across a
vocabulary of about a hundred thousand pieces. This table is written by hand so
that one whole row fits on the page.

<TemperatureDial client:visible />

It opens midway, with the model continuing _The weather today is_. Five pieces:
_mild_ at 55%, _cold_ at 27%, _unsettled_ at 12%, _glorious_ at 5%, and
_aubergine_, printed as 0.

**Take the slider to the bottom first.** Four bars vanish and _mild_ goes to
100%. Now run it four times. The same sentence four times — _The weather today
is mild and dry_. Nothing has been switched off; the machine is still drawing at
random, exactly as before. There is simply nothing left to draw.

This end of the dial stops at 0.1 rather than 0, because dividing by zero is not
a thing a dial can do. Interfaces that offer you a 0 are not dividing by it
either — they quietly switch to always taking the largest, which is what this
end is doing anyway. Either way it is where anything that has to be reproducible
gets run.

**Leave it on four runs and slide back to the middle.** Four different
sentences. These four draws happen to walk straight down the row — the first
takes _mild_, the second _cold_, the third _unsettled_, the fourth _glorious_ —
which is what a row of percentages looks like when it is genuinely being
sampled. One opinion, four outcomes, and not a single number in that row changed
between them.

**Now push the dial up to 1.1 or beyond.** The fourth run opens with
_aubergine_. At 1.2 the model rated that piece at 0.8%, and 0.8% of the time is
0.8% of the time. Watch what it does next: it does not stop, apologise or start
the sentence again. It writes _aubergine coloured and…_, because continuing is
the only operation it has.

Two honest caveats about that. The four runs are four fixed rolls of the dice,
so that this page can point at them and so that you see what the sentences above
describe; four is not a survey, and at the top of the dial the model is giving
that piece about three chances in a hundred. And the row prints whole
percentages, so at the middle setting _aubergine_ shows as 0 — it is 0.4%, which
is not never. It is about once in every two hundred and seventy presses.

## What the two ends are actually for

**Turn it down** for anything with one right answer and a fixed shape. Pulling
fields out of an invoice, classifying a ticket, converting a date, writing code
where one wrong token stops the file compiling. Two reasons, and the second one
matters more than it looks. Where there is a single correct answer, the
second-best piece is not a pleasant variation, it is simply wrong. And a system
that returns the same output for the same input is a system you can test; one
that does not is a system where every failed test might be bad luck.

**Turn it up** for anything you are going to sift through yourself. Names,
subject lines, opening paragraphs, ten different angles on a brief. Here a dud
costs you nothing but the second it takes to skip it, and the whole value is in
the spread. If you want six genuinely different attempts, this dial is how you
get them; asking the same question six times at the bottom of the range will
hand you the same answer six times.

The dial has no idea which of these you are doing. It is set per request, and
most chat interfaces set it once, on your behalf, for everything you will ever
ask.

## Turning it down does not make it true

At the bottom of the range the model always takes its top-scored piece. If the
top-scored piece is wrong, you now get the same wrong answer every single time,
delivered with total steadiness. Consistency is not accuracy. Nothing anywhere
in this dial has access to whether the favourite is correct; it only knows which
score was largest, and it was going to know that at every setting.

The other direction fails for the same reason. Turning the dial up cannot make
the model reach a fact that was not in the row, because temperature never adds
anything to the row. It only makes it likelier to pick something it already
rated badly.

There is one genuinely useful thing to do with all this. Because the variation
comes from the draw and not from the model reconsidering, running the same
question three times tells you something real about the row underneath. Where
the three answers agree, the row was sharply peaked and the model was confident.
Where they wander off in different directions, the row was flat — no strong
opinion, and what you are reading is a guess wearing the same fluent prose as
everything else. It is not proof: three agreeing answers can be three samples of
one confident mistake. But as a thirty-second check on whether you are looking
at knowledge or at a guess, nothing else is that cheap.

<Aside title="The other control, which does something different" tone="note">
  Alongside temperature, most APIs and playgrounds offer a second control,
  usually called **top-p** (or nucleus sampling) and sometimes **top-k**. It is
  easy to assume it is a variant of this one. It is not, and the difference is
  the distinction this whole unit turns on.

Top-p sorts the row, keeps adding pieces from the top until their shares reach
some total — 90%, say — and throws everything below that away. Then it draws
from what is left. That is not a change to the gaps. It is a change to the list.

Which makes the two complementary rather than redundant. A high temperature with
a tight top-p gives you variety among the plausible options while making the
piece rated at four in a thousand strictly unreachable rather than merely
unlikely. If you want adventurous prose without the occasional _aubergine_, that
combination is how it is done.

</Aside>

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    var reply = model.complete(prompt, Options.temperature(0.0));
    ```

    Temperature belongs to the request, not to the model, so two callers of the
    same deployment can be running at different settings and neither can see the
    other's. It is worth logging next to the output; a bug report that says "it
    gave a different answer" is unanswerable without it.

    And zero is not the guarantee it looks like — this is the one place the
    page above simplifies. Hosted models batch requests together and
    floating-point addition is not associative, so the raw scores can come out
    fractionally different depending on who else happened to be in the batch,
    which is occasionally enough to swap the top two. If you need the exact
    bytes again, store them rather than planning to regenerate them.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    reply = model.complete(prompt, temperature=0)
    ```

    The trap this sets is in the test suite. An assertion comparing the reply to
    a fixed string will pass on your machine for a fortnight and then fail on a
    run where nothing changed. Assert on properties the answer has to have — it
    parses as JSON, it contains these three keys, the total matches the invoice
    — not on the exact sentence.

    The mirror-image trap is asking for variety *across* requests while leaving
    the dial at zero. Within one reply the model can still vary, because every
    piece is chosen knowing what it has just written — but five separate calls
    asking for a different angle each time will hand back the same angle five
    times, because two identical requests at zero have nothing to differ with.

  </Fragment>

<Checkpoint question="An extraction tool occasionally pulls the wrong field out of an invoice. Someone proposes setting temperature to 0 so that it stops making mistakes. What will that actually change, what will it definitely not change, and what would you have to look at to find out whether it helped?">
  It changes the variability and nothing else. The same invoice will now always
  produce the same extraction, which removes one whole source of error — the
  runs where the tool drew the second-best field instead of the best one.

What it cannot change is which field the model scored highest, because that was
decided before the dial was consulted and is the same at every setting. Every
mistake where the wrong field was the favourite survives untouched, and now
arrives identically every time, which makes it look considered rather than
accidental.

So the only way to know is to compare against invoices whose correct answers you
already have, before and after. If the errors were coming from the draw, they
disappear. If they were coming from the row, the score does not move at all.

Worth keeping as a shape. Consistency and correctness are separate axes, and a
change that improves one says nothing about the other — with the extra sting
that making a system consistent can make its remaining errors harder to notice,
because they stop looking like noise and start looking like a decision.

</Checkpoint>
