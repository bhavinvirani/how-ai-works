Ten people round a table, one postcard, and a rule.

The card reaches you with a few words already on it. You read the whole card
from the top — not the last word, the whole card — add exactly one word to the
end, and pass it on. You do not get told what the sentence is about. You do not
get to cross anything out. Neither does anyone after you.

By the third lap something is happening on that card. It has a subject and a
tone and it holds together well enough to read aloud. Nobody wrote it. Nobody
could have said at the start what it would end up saying, least of all the
person who put down the first word.

Then somebody, having read a perfectly sensible card, writes _aubergine_.

The next person does not get to rub it out. They get one word, on the end, after
the aubergine.

That game is not an illustration of how a language model writes. It is, almost
exactly, the thing itself.

## Four steps, and then the same four again

A trained model does not hand back a sentence. Ask it anything and what comes
out of it is one row of numbers: a score for every piece in its vocabulary —
every chunk it knows, around a hundred thousand of them — saying how well each
one would sit next.

A row of scores is not an answer. So something has to turn one row into a
paragraph, and there is only one thing that does it:

1. Read everything written so far, from the very first piece.
2. Score every piece in the vocabulary, and turn those scores into percentages
   that add up to one.
3. Draw a piece, in those proportions.
4. Glue it onto the end — and go back to step one.

It stops when the draw lands on a particular piece that means _done_, which the
model learned to produce the way it learned everything else.

The name for a machine that works this way is **autoregressive**, which is
uglier than it needs to be. _Auto_ is itself and _regressive_ is reading back
over: a machine whose input, at every step, is its own output so far.

Notice which seat that puts the model in. It is not the author of the postcard.
It is every one of the ten people, in turn — because nothing survives from one
pass to the next except the card. There is no scratchpad it keeps between
passes, no note of what it was going for, no memory of having written any of it.
Each pass is a stranger picking up the card and reading it from the top.

## Nothing was decided in advance

When a model writes the first piece of a paragraph, the paragraph does not
exist. Not in draft, not in outline, not as an intention. There is one piece,
and then a fresh reading of a card one piece longer.

Which raises an obvious objection: models write jokes that land, arguments that
close, sentences that clearly needed the ending to be planned from the start.

They do, and no ending was planned. What happens instead is that by the time the
machine reaches the last piece of a joke, the setup is not a memory of a plan —
it is text, sitting there, being read from the top, narrowing what could
plausibly come next until very little else fits. Coherence is not imposed from
above. It accumulates, because every pass is scored against everything already
on the card.

<Aside title="But surely it is thinking ahead?">
  Something has to be said carefully here, because the honest answer is not a
  flat no.

One pass is an enormous amount of arithmetic, and there is good evidence that
some of it concerns where the sentence is going rather than only the next piece
— a model writing a line of verse appears to settle on a rhyme several words
before it needs one.

What is certain is what survives. Whatever a pass works out, it works out from
the card and throws away when the pass ends. The next pass begins from the text
alone. So "it has no plan" is exactly true in the sense that matters here: there
is no draft anywhere in the machine, nothing written down but the output itself,
and nothing that carries an intention forward except words you can read.

</Aside>

## What is real here, and what is hand-written

The panel below runs a real version of steps two, three and four, and it is
worth knowing exactly which parts of it are the real thing before you draw
conclusions from it.

Real: the raw scores are divided by a setting called the temperature, then
squashed by **softmax** — the step that takes any row of numbers and hands back
a row of shares, all positive, the largest still the largest, adding to exactly
one. It is the same operation that gave every word its single unit of attention
to spend. Then a number between 0 and 1 is drawn, and whichever piece's slice
of the row it lands in is the piece that gets written. Every one of those steps,
in that order, is what actually happens inside a production model. Here the
temperature is fixed at one, and dividing by one changes nothing, so what you
see is the model's own row untouched. Turning that dial is the next unit's
entire subject.

Hand-written: where the raw scores come from. A real model computes them by
running the whole transformer over everything on the card, and its row is a
hundred thousand pieces long — you would see the top five and none of the rest.
The scores behind this panel are typed into a small table so that a whole row
fits on a screen and you can watch it change. Take the loop from it. Do not take
the vocabulary, and do not read the percentages as measurements of anything.

## Turn the handle yourself

The opening is fixed — _The weather today is_ — and the counter at the bottom of
the panel writes one piece per press. Leave the other control alone for now.

<NextPieceLoop client:visible />

Press it once. The model has read the four pieces it was given and produced a
row: _mild_ at 55%, _cold_ at 27%, _unsettled_ at 12%, _glorious_ at 5%, and
_aubergine_ at what prints as 0. It drew _mild_.

Press again, and again. It reads five pieces and writes _and_ at 58%. It reads
six and writes _dry_ at 53%.

Now the fourth press, which is the interesting one. Having read seven pieces,
the row it produced was headed by _across_ at 51% — and it wrote _again_, which
held 11% of that row. Nothing chose. A number between 0 and 1 fell inside the
slice belonging to _again_, and that slice is eleven hundredths of the row wide,
so it happens about one time in nine. The favourite took only half the row, so
roughly half of all draws from it land somewhere else entirely.

Now read the four lines of the trail in order and watch one number only: it read
**4** pieces, then **5**, then **6**, then **7**. The amount of reading went up
by exactly one every pass and never by anything else, because the only thing
that ever changed was that one more piece had been glued on the end. That is the
first claim of this unit, and it is the one thing on the page you can check by
counting.

## It cannot take anything back

Now switch the panel to _After one bad piece_.

This picks the run up one press later, after the draw has landed on _aubergine_
— the piece at the bottom of the opening row, worth 0.4%, which prints as 0 only
because the bars round to whole numbers. It is not zero. It is about one run in
270, and one run in 270 is not rare at all if you are doing this a few million
times a day.

Look at the row the model then produces: _coloured_ 36%, _which_ 26%, _and_ 22%,
_in_ 16%. Four perfectly ordinary continuations. Read them again and notice what
is missing. Not one of them removes anything. There is no piece anywhere in that
row that shortens the card, or clears it, or starts the sentence over, because
appending is the only operation this machine has. The vocabulary contains no
delete key — not as an oversight, but because there is nowhere for it to act.

So it writes _coloured_, and then _throughout_, and what comes out is _The
weather today is aubergine coloured throughout_ — fluent, grammatical, correctly
punctuated, and about the colour of a vegetable.

The last thing to notice is the numbers themselves. The top of that row holds
36%, which is an unremarkable figure for an unremarkable row. Nothing anywhere
in the panel dips, or flags, or reads differently. From the inside there is
nothing to notice, because "this sentence has gone wrong" is not a quantity the
machine computes.

You can wind the counter back. The model cannot.

## Why an apology is not a correction

The mechanism is worth stating flatly, because a great deal follows from it. A
piece that has been written is part of the input to every pass after it. Not a
record of a decision the model made — the text itself, read from the top, every
single time.

Which brings us to the objection everyone has, because everyone has seen a model
say _sorry, that was wrong_. It does, and that is not taking anything back. An
apology is a few more pieces glued onto the end. The wrong words are still
there, still being read from the start on every pass that follows, still pulling
on every percentage in every row. The model has not removed a mistake. It has
written a correction underneath one it can still see.

This is the explanation for something you have almost certainly experienced. A
model that opens an answer badly tends to keep going badly, and it is not being
stubborn — the bad opening is now part of the question. Telling it _no, try
again_ appends your complaint to a card that still has the wrong answer sitting
on it, in full view, being re-read on every pass.

Hence the least satisfying advice about these tools, which is also the most
reliable. When an answer starts wrong, throw the whole thing away and ask again
from an empty conversation. That is the only move that actually takes the wrong
text out of the input, and it is why starting over so often beats arguing.

<Aside title="What the loop costs">
  Every single piece is a complete pass through the machine. A thousand-piece
  answer is a thousand passes, so both the time and the bill grow in direct
  proportion to how much is written — which is also why answers arrive in
  bursts rather than all at once. Streaming is not a feature somebody added.
  It is the loop showing through.

It is why the price lists quote two different rates, too. Everything you send
can be read in one pass, all of it at the same time; everything that comes back
has to be produced one piece at a time, one pass each. Output is the expensive
half because output is the sequential half.

There is one shortcut worth knowing about, since it sounds like it contradicts
all this. The machinery keeps the working from the previous pass rather than
recomputing it from scratch, which makes each pass much cheaper than a first
reading of the same text. The answers are identical either way, and nothing
extra survives between passes — it is a cache, not a memory.

</Aside>

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    List<Integer> pieces = tokenizer.encode(prompt);

    while (pieces.size() < maxPieces) {
      float[] row = model.next(pieces);   // one full pass over all of it
      int drawn = sample(row);            // a draw, not a maximum
      if (drawn == END) break;
      pieces.add(drawn);                  // the only mutation in generation
    }
    ```

    Worth noticing what is not in that loop, and is not in any library that
    wraps it: there is no `pieces.remove`. Nothing rolls back a piece, because
    a piece is not a decision that could be reversed — it is an element of the
    input to everything after it. Changing what has been generated means
    changing the input and running the loop again.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    pieces = tokenizer.encode(prompt)

    while len(pieces) < max_pieces:
        row = model(pieces)[-1].softmax(-1)        # one full pass over all of it
        drawn = torch.multinomial(row, 1).item()   # a draw, not a maximum
        if drawn == END:
            break
        pieces.append(drawn)
    ```

    Two details do a lot of work here. `multinomial` rather than `argmax` is
    what makes the same prompt give different answers, and swapping one for the
    other is the whole of the next unit. And `[-1]`: the model scores every
    position in the sequence on every pass, and generation throws all of that
    away except the last row.

<Checkpoint question="A support bot answers a customer with a wrong first sentence. The customer replies that it is wrong; the bot apologises, then produces almost the same wrong answer. A colleague says the model is being stubborn and suggests wording the correction more firmly. What is actually going on, and what would you do instead?">
  Nothing is being stubborn, and nothing about the wording is the problem. The
  apology did not remove the wrong sentence. It is still in the conversation,
  and every pass the model makes from that point reads it from the top —
  alongside the complaint and the apology — before producing the next row of
  percentages. The mistake stopped being something the model did and became
  part of what the model is reading.

The fix is to take the wrong text out of the input, and the only way to do that
is to start again: same question, empty conversation. A firmer correction adds
more text to a card that still has the wrong answer on it, which is the opposite
of what is needed.

Worth keeping as a shape. Anything already in the conversation is input, not
history. From the model's side there is no difference between the words you
wrote and the words it wrote — it reads both the same way, from the beginning,
every time.

</Checkpoint>
