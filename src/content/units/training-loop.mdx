Somewhere near you there is a patch of grass with a track worn across it. Not a
path anyone drew — a shortcut cutting the corner the paving forgot.

Watch one person walk it. Nothing happens. The grass bends and springs back up,
and nothing about that one crossing would let you predict a path.

Ten thousand crossings later there is bare earth, and it runs exactly where
people actually wanted to go. Nobody surveyed it or approved it. It is made
entirely of events too small to notice.

That is what happens inside a machine while it learns. Not the walking — the
arithmetic. One event that visibly does nothing, repeated until it has done
everything.

## The four steps

Last unit you turned two dials by hand until a machine's guesses about house
prices stopped being so wrong. You moved one, watched the wrongness, and went
back the other way when it got worse. You never needed to understand houses —
only whether you were getting warmer.

Nothing in that procedure needed you. Here it is without you.

The machine picks up one **example** — one house, with the price it really sold
for attached. Examples always come in that shape: something to look at, and the
correct answer beside it. Four things then happen.

**Guess.** The floor area goes in, the dials do their multiplying and adding, and
a number comes out. On a 74 square metre house, £180,000, say.

**Compare.** Look up what that house actually sold for. £219,000. The guess was
£39,000 too low, and that gap is the only feedback the machine ever gets.

**Blame.** Work out which dials pushed the answer the wrong way, and how hard
each one pushed. This is not a figure of speech: every dial ends up with a
number saying how much of this mistake is its doing. The dial holding
the value of a square metre had its number multiplied by 74 on the way to the
answer, so on a house this size it did most of the pushing, and it takes most of
the blame.

**Nudge.** Turn every dial a hair in the direction that would have made this one
guess less wrong. Not enough to fix it. Deliberately, absurdly, not enough.

Then drop that house, pick up the next one, and go back to step one.

## Why the nudge is deliberately too small

The size of that last step is the part people find hardest to accept, so it is
worth being blunt about it. If the machine corrected itself fully for each
house, it would spend its whole life being right about whichever house it saw
last and wrong about every other one. Each example would undo the one before.
Tiny nudges let the evidence pile up instead of taking turns.

So one pass through those four steps teaches the machine nothing you could point
at. The dials move by amounts you need four decimal places to see. It is one
person walking across the grass.

Running that cycle over and over is what the word **training** means. When you
read that a model "was trained", this is what happened to it, an extremely large
number of times.

## Watch it get boring, then watch it work

Below is the machine from last unit — the same eight houses, the same two dials
— except this time nobody touches the dials. Pressing the counter runs the loop.
Start with one pass at a time.

<TrainingLoopRunner client:visible />

Three things worth doing, in order.

Press it once. The wrongness shifts by about £470 and the line does not move at
all. Press it again. And again. Nothing you do one pass at a time makes a
visible difference to a machine out by a quarter of a million pounds a house —
though every one of those presses ran all four steps, correctly.

Now switch to 250 at a time and press twice. The line swings up underneath the
houses and the wrongness collapses from £245,000 to about £11,000. Nothing
changed except how many times the same four steps ran.

Then take it out to twenty-five thousand. It settles at roughly £6,800 a house —
within a couple of hundred pounds of the best setting those two dials have,
which the instrument found by trying all thirty thousand of them. Watch the
bottom line as you go: that last pass, on its own, moved the wrongness by about
five pounds.

No single nudge is worth anything. The pile of them is worth everything.

<Aside title="Two words that will come at you">
  One complete trip through every example you have is an **epoch**, and models
  are normally trained for several. The number of examples looked at before
  anything is nudged is the **batch size** — real systems average the blame over
  a few dozen or a few hundred rather than nudging after every single one,
  because it is steadier and much faster on the hardware. Neither word changes
  the four steps. You do not need to remember them; now they will not stop you.
</Aside>

## Where you meet this

This is why training is the expensive part and using a model is the cheap part.
Answering your question runs the machine forwards once. Training ran it forwards
and backwards over an enormous pile of examples, for weeks, across thousands of
machines — and that bill was paid before anyone typed anything into it.

It is also why a model cannot simply be told a new fact. There is no slot to put
a fact in — only dials, and the only tool for moving them is this loop, which
wants examples and a great many passes. Correcting a model is not editing. It is
more training.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    for (int epoch = 0; epoch < EPOCHS; epoch++) {
      for (Example e : examples) {
        double guess = model.predict(e.input());  // 1. guess
        double error = guess - e.answer();        // 2. compare
        Gradient blame = model.blame(e, error);   // 3. blame
        model.nudge(blame, LEARNING_RATE);        // 4. nudge
      }
    }
    ```

    Two loops and four lines. Every training framework you will ever read is
    this, with the middle two lines expanded into a great deal of linear algebra
    and the whole thing moved onto a GPU. `LEARNING_RATE` is the size of the
    hair, and it is a constant a human picked.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    for epoch in range(EPOCHS):
        for x, answer in examples:
            guess = model(x)              # 1. guess
            loss = (guess - answer) ** 2  # 2. compare
            loss.backward()               # 3. blame
            optimiser.step()              # 4. nudge
            optimiser.zero_grad()
    ```

    `backward()` is step three for every parameter at once, and `step()` is
    step four. Those two calls hide almost all of the difficulty and none of
    the shape.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="A model has been training for hours and its wrongness has stopped falling. A colleague suspects the most recent pass through the loop went wrong. Why is that the wrong place to look?">
  Because no single pass was ever doing enough to matter. Each one nudges the
  dials by a fraction of a fraction, on the evidence of one example; the fall
  they were watching was thousands of those adding up. If it has stopped,
  suspect the pile — what is in the examples, or how big the nudges are — never
  the last pass.

Keep hold of this one. Nothing a learning machine does can be explained by one
step of anything. Every property it has, impressive or embarrassing, is an
accumulation.

</Checkpoint>
