A recipe goes round an office.

The first person is asked to tidy the wording, and does. The second is asked to
make it clearer, and works from the first person's version, because that is the
sheet on the desk. The third makes it friendlier, working from the second's.
Nobody sees the original again after Tuesday.

Twenty desks later the document is fluent, confident, and no longer a recipe.

Nobody was careless. Nobody disagreed with anybody. Each person did one small
sensible job on the only thing in front of them, and the only thing in front of
them was somebody else's rewrite of somebody else's rewrite.

That is what you get if you take attention and simply run it forty times in a
row. And you do have to run it many times in a row, which is the awkward part.

## One round of attention is one conversation

You know what a single round does. Every word turns itself into a question and a
label, every question is held up against every label, and each word rebuilds
itself out of whichever words answered. A rank of heads does that side by side,
each one free to watch a different kind of relationship. Position has already
been stamped into the numbers, so the sentence is not an unordered pile.

That is a great deal of machinery, and it buys exactly one round. Here is what
one round cannot reach.

When the round begins, every word means what it means on its own. So the blend
each word comes back holding is a blend of _isolated_ meanings — what the other
words are before anybody has looked at anything.

Run it a second time and the material has changed. Every word now carries what
it picked up the first time round. So the second round relates words to each
other's _settled_ meanings rather than their dictionary ones, and it can reach
relationships that were not previously there to be found, because one of the two
things being related had not been worked out yet.

A small case of that. To know that _it_ refers to the parcel rather than the
doorstep, you first have to have worked out that _the one left out in the rain_
is describing the parcel. That is itself a relationship between words. It has to
be settled before the pronoun's can be, and one pass cannot do both, because the
second question needs the answer to the first.

So you want many rounds. Which brings you back to the office.

## Three things to add before you can stack it

Stacking attention is not a matter of writing the same line forty times. Three
plain, unglamorous parts have to go around each round, and each of the three is
there because something specific breaks without it.

Attention with those parts around it makes a unit called a **block**. The block
is the thing that actually gets stacked, and it is what the rest of this unit is
about.

### A shortcut past each stage

The office failed because each person replaced the document. The fix is not
better editors. It is to change what gets handed on: keep the original, and pass
it along with your changes written into it.

That is the first added part, and it is one line of arithmetic. Whatever a stage
produces is **added to** what that stage was given, rather than taking its place.
Write the numbers arriving as $x$, and what the stage makes of them as $f(x)$:

$$
\text{what leaves} = x + f(x)
$$

In words: what comes out is everything that came in, plus whatever this stage
thought was worth changing about it.

The field calls that a **residual connection**, where _residual_ means
leftover — the stage is now only on the hook for producing the leftover bit,
the correction, instead of a whole fresh answer.

Two things break without it, and the second is what stopped people building deep
networks for years.

The first is the office. Ninety-six stages that each overwrite everything leave
nothing of the original by the time you reach the top.

The second is training. Every nudge in this business comes from a correction
that starts at the output and travels back down through every stage to reach the
dials at the bottom. Each stage it passes through multiplies it by something,
and a chain of ninety-six multiplications does not come out roughly the size it
went in. It comes out enormous, or, far more often, at nothing. The bottom of
the stack then receives no correction worth the name, so its dials never move,
so the part doing the most basic work is the part that never learns.

The shortcut fixes precisely that. It lays a road from the top of the stack to
the bottom that the correction can travel down without being multiplied by
anything at all.

### A rescale between stages

Now look at what the shortcut does over ninety-six blocks. Every block adds into
the same running total, and nothing anywhere holds that total to any particular
range. The numbers drift — usually upward, sometimes not, and never
predictably.

So a second part is added, in the plainest possible spirit. After each stage,
every word's list of numbers is put back onto a standard scale: shifted so its
average is zero, then stretched or squashed so its spread is one. Two dials of
its own come afterwards, so the model can undo the whole thing wherever it turns
out to prefer the numbers where they were.

The name is **layer normalisation**, in which _normalisation_ means nothing
grander than "put back on a standard scale".

What breaks without it is training, again, and it breaks in a way you have
already watched happen. The step size is chosen once and applied everywhere. If
the numbers at block ninety are living on a scale ten times the numbers at block
three, then a step that is sensible down there overshoots up here, and an
overshoot lands on steeper ground, which makes the next step bigger still.

This is the least interesting idea in the unit. Take it out of a hundred-block
model and the model does not train slightly worse. It does not train.

### A step where each word thinks on its own

Attention moves information between words. That is the only thing it does. Each
word ends up holding a blend of the other words, and a blend is an average — a
very cleverly weighted one, but an average.

Stack averages on averages and you get more averaging. What you never get is a
word doing anything with what it has just been told.

So after the mixing, each word goes off on its own through a small ordinary
network of exactly the kind you met before language entered the picture:
multiply by some dials, add up the results, put the total through a bend, and do
it again. The same network for every word, run on each of them separately, with
no reference to its neighbours at all. Its name is a **feed-forward network** —
_feed-forward_ because the numbers pass through it once, in one direction, and
never come back.

Take it out and something specific happens: the words start converging. Every
round of pure attention pulls them a little closer together, nothing ever pushes
them apart again, and after enough rounds a whole paragraph arrives at the top
meaning approximately one thing. Blending is the only move on the board, so
blending wins.

It is also where the bend lives — the small non-straight-line step you met when
layers were first stacked, without which a tall pile of multiplications is
arithmetically a short one. Most of a transformer's bending happens right here.

And it is where most of the model is. Of the adjustable numbers in one block,
the mixing holds about a third and this step holds the other two thirds. The
part that reads like an afterthought is twice the size of the part everybody
talks about.

## Now stack it

Look at what leaves the bottom of that drawing. One list of numbers per word —
the same count of words, the same length of list — as arrived at the top. The
shape did not change.

That is not a coincidence. It is the constraint the whole design was built
around, because a machine whose output has the same shape as its input can be
handed straight to a copy of itself. So you build one block, then ninety-five
more with dials of their own, and you run the words through all of them in
order.

That stack is a **transformer**. The definition really is that short, and there
is no part of it you have not now met.

Ninety-six is not a number picked for the sound of it. GPT-3 has ninety-six
blocks, ninety-six heads inside each block, and carries every word as a list of
12,288 numbers. Count the dials in one block and you get about 1.8 billion.
Ninety-six of those comes to about 174 billion. The published size of the whole
model is 175 billion, and the remainder is mostly the lookup table that turns
pieces of text into numbers before any of this starts.

There is nothing else in there. The stack is the model.

One more thing becomes visible only once it is stacked, and it should change how
you picture the machine. Because each block adds to what it was given rather
than replacing it, those ninety-six shortcuts are not really ninety-six separate
things. They are one continuous lane running from the bottom of the model to the
top, which every block in turn reaches into, reads, and writes a change back
into. People who work on these call it the **residual stream** — the
leftovers lane, and a _stream_ because nothing along its length interrupts it.

## Always ninety-six

Ask it what two and two make. Ninety-six blocks. Ask it to find the hole in a
page of contract law. Ninety-six blocks, and the same number of multiplications,
to the multiplication.

There is no test anywhere in that stack, no counter, and nowhere that "if this
one is hard, go round again" could be written. The stack has a length, and the
length was fixed before anybody typed a question into it.

You met the shape of this claim with the forward pass, on a small machine that
read handwritten digits. This is the same claim about the machine people
actually use, and it is worth planting firmly, because a great deal of what
looks strange about these systems later on is this constraint being worked
around rather than removed.

What does vary is how many words come out. A model produces one word per journey
through all ninety-six blocks, and each new journey gets to read everything
written so far, including the words the model itself wrote a second ago. So a
model can be made to look as though it is deliberating, but the deliberating is
spread across many passes, in writing, where you can watch it. It is never
packed into one pass that worked harder, because there is no such pass.

<Aside title="When a spec says “96 layers”">
  The word _layer_ does two jobs in this field, at two very different sizes.
  Earlier on it meant one rank of neurons. In anything written about a
  transformer it almost always means one entire block — attention, shortcut,
  rescale, per-word step and all.

So a model card advertising 96 layers is counting 96 copies of the drawing above,
not 96 rows of neurons. Small models sit around a dozen; large ones run past a
hundred. It is the single most useful number on the card, because multiplied out
against the width it is very nearly the size of the model.

</Aside>

<Aside title="Where the facts seem to live">
  Something odd has come out of people taking these apart. Attention looks like
  machinery for moving information about — it decides what each word gets to
  hear. The per-word step looks like where a good deal of the model's factual
  knowledge actually sits, and researchers have edited individual facts by
  changing numbers there and nowhere else.

"Looks like" is doing real work in those sentences. This is an active argument
and the picture is not settled. But it is worth carrying, because it inverts the
obvious reading of the diagram: the stage that looks like plumbing is holding
most of what the model knows.

</Aside>

## What else got built out of it

The block is not a language part. Nothing in the drawing knows what a word is;
it takes a list of things, each carried as a list of numbers, and lets them
consult each other.

So the same block, essentially unchanged, now reads images, cut into squares
instead of words, and audio, and sequences of amino acids. That is why this one
picture is worth the effort of learning properly. Almost everything in the rest
of this site is a stack of it, trained on something different or wrapped in
something new.

<DevAside>
  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    double[][] block(double[][] x, Weights w) {
      x = add(x, attention(rescale(x), w));    // the words talk
      x = add(x, thinkAlone(rescale(x), w));   // each word alone
      return x;
    }

    for (int i = 0; i < 96; i++) {
      x = block(x, weights[i]);
    }
    ```

    Three things are worth noticing. `x` is never assigned anything that does
    not still contain `x`. That is the shortcut, written down; delete the two
    `add(x, ...)` wrappers and you have deleted it.

    The rescale sits *before* each stage here. The 2017 paper put it after the
    addition instead, and almost everything since has moved it in front, because
    this ordering is what makes a hundred-block stack train reliably. It reads
    like a detail and is not one.

    And the loop bound is a literal. No argument reaches it.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    def block(x, w):
        x = x + attention(rescale(x), w)
        x = x + think_alone(rescale(x), w)
        return x

    for w in weights:          # len(weights) == 96
        x = block(x, w)
    ```

    The whole architecture is those two `x = x +` lines, and most of this unit
    is about why the two `+` signs are there rather than what is either side of
    them.

    The loop has no `break` in it, nothing that could grow one, and nothing it
    could branch on. Profiling this is oddly restful: a run that comes back
    slower has told you something about your hardware and nothing whatsoever
    about the question.

  </Fragment>
</DevAside>

<Checkpoint question="A team strips a transformer back to what they call “the part that does the work” — ninety-six rounds of attention, one after another, with the shortcuts, the rescales and the per-word steps taken out as plumbing. Training goes nowhere, and the little it does produce has every word in a paragraph meaning roughly the same thing. Which removal caused which failure?">
  The words all meaning the same thing is the per-word step. Attention only
  blends, so a stack of nothing but attention pulls the words together on every
  round with nothing ever pushing them apart — after ninety-six rounds a
  paragraph has been averaged into one meaning.

Training going nowhere is the other two. Without the shortcut, the correction
sent back from the output has to survive ninety-six multiplications before it
reaches the bottom of the stack, and it arrives at nothing, so the lowest blocks
never learn. Without the rescale, the numbers drift in size from one end of the
stack to the other, so no single step size is right in more than one place.

The word to argue with is "plumbing". Attention is the only part of the block
that does anything you could describe as interesting, and it is also the only
part that cannot be stacked on its own. The three dull additions are not
supporting the idea. They are what makes the idea usable more than once.

</Checkpoint>
