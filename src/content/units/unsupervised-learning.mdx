A supermarket chain sends you a file and one line of email: what kinds of
shopper do we have?

The file is a year of till receipts boiled down to two numbers per customer —
how often they came in, and what they spent on a typical visit. Forty thousand
rows, and not one of them says what kind of shopper it is.

Read the question again, because the trouble is inside it. There is no column
marked _kind_. Nobody has been down the rows deciding who belongs where, and
nobody could have: what the kinds are is the thing being asked for, not the
thing being handed over.

Every machine so far has quietly assumed otherwise. A column of yeses and noes,
a price that should have been guessed — something to be wrong about. Take that
away and there is nothing left to be wrong about, which means nothing to improve
against.

And yet the file is not empty of information. You already suspect what is in
there. Somebody calling in every other day for milk is not the same kind of
shopper as somebody who arrives once a week and fills the boot.

## Alike is the only thing it can measure

What a machine can do with a file like this is narrower than it sounds. It
cannot tell you what a shopper _is_. It can work out how alike any two of them
are — near each other on the chart, or far apart — and that is the whole of its
equipment.

Which turns out to be enough for one specific job. Put every shopper on a chart,
visits along the bottom and spend up the side. Pick a few spots to act as
middles. Give each shopper to whichever middle is nearest, then move each middle
to the average of whoever it caught, then do it again. After a handful of rounds
nothing moves any more, and you have piles.

Learning from examples that carry no answers is called **unsupervised
learning**, and the supervision it does without is exactly what it sounds like: a
person going through beforehand, marking the right answer on every row.
Gathering like with like has its own name too, **clustering**, and it is the
most common thing anybody does with it.

Now notice what did not happen. Nobody told the machine how many piles there
ought to be; that number was handed to it. And nobody told it what a pile means.
_Group two_ means group two, and nothing else. That group two is the weekly big
shop is a sentence you supply afterwards, by looking at where it sits and
deciding what to call it.

Two related jobs run off the same measurement. The first is **odd ones out**:
learn the shape of ordinary, then flag whatever sits far from all of it. That is
how a bank catches a kind of fraud nobody has seen before. It never needed an
example of the fraud, only a great many examples of normal.

The second is **simplification**: squash rows carrying two hundred measurements
into two or three that keep most of what mattered. That sounds like
housekeeping, and it is not. Reducing something complicated to a short list of
numbers that preserves its character is how machines end up holding the meaning
of words, and it returns later as one of the largest ideas here.

There is a price for working without answers, and it is worth being blunt about.
With an answer key you can count how often you were right. Here there is nothing
to count against. Ask for four groups and you get four, whether or not four kinds
of shopper exist. The machine does keep a score of its own, how tightly its
piles hold together, and that score has a habit that is easier to watch than to
describe.

## Ask it for a different number

Below are forty-two of those shoppers, each one a position on the chart: how
often they came, what they spent. Start with three groups. Then ask for four,
and watch the score rather than the picture.

<ClusterFinder client:visible />

At three it finds roughly what you would have found by eye. Fourteen frequent
shoppers, in about twenty times a month for around £9 a go. Twelve who come
about once a week and spend nearly £60. Sixteen somewhere in the middle.

Now ask for four. The spread score drops from 11.3 to 9.8, which by the
machine's own reckoning is an improvement. Look at what the improvement bought.
No fourth kind of shopper has appeared. It has sawn the frequent shoppers in
half at twenty visits a month and called the halves two groups. Ask for
six and the score falls again. It always falls. Hand it forty-two groups and
every shopper would sit alone in the middle of their own, spread score nought:
perfect, and worth nothing. **The machine's own number can never tell you how
many groups there are.**

Then leave the number alone and change only where the search started. At three
groups the second starting guess reaches an identical answer and the third moves
two shoppers — that structure is solid enough to survive being approached from
somewhere else. At four, ten of the forty-two change hands, and the second guess
does not even cut the same group in two; it splits the middling shoppers
instead. Same data, same instruction, two equally confident and quite different
answers.

<Aside title="Alike, in what units?">
  Before a machine can say which two shoppers are more alike, somebody has to
  decide what one visit is worth in pounds. Here both measurements were squashed
  onto the same nought-to-one scale, which quietly declares that the full range
  of visiting habits counts for exactly as much as the full range of spending.
  Change that exchange rate and the groups change with it. It is another human
  judgement wearing a technical hat.
</Aside>

  <Fragment slot="java">
    ```java
    // Nothing in here is the answer, because there is no answer.
    record Shopper(int visits, double spend) {}

    List<List<Shopper>> groups = cluster(shoppers, 4);
    ```

    The `4` is the whole argument of this unit, sitting there as an ordinary
    parameter with no sensible default. Nothing in the types, and nothing in the
    data, will tell you it should have been 3. Whatever you pass, you get back
    that many lists — each one unnamed, because there is nothing to name it
    after.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

    labels = KMeans(n_clusters=4).fit_predict(rows)
    # array([2, 0, 3, 1, 2, 0, ...])
    ```

    Note what comes back: one integer per row. No names, no confidence, and no
    complaint if `n_clusters` was wrong. The variable name is doing a lot of
    quiet work — these are not labels in the sense the earlier units meant.
    Nobody wrote them down. The machine invented them a moment ago.

<Checkpoint question="A tool splits your customers into five groups, and the slide names them Bargain Hunters, Loyalists, Big Spenders, Browsers and At-Risk. Which part of that came out of the machine?">
  The five piles, and nothing else. The machine produced group 1 to group 5 and
  stopped there. Somebody looked at where each pile sat, recognised a story, and
  wrote the names on top, and _five_ was asked for rather than discovered, so
  there would have been six equally confident piles, with a better score, had
  somebody typed six.

Which gives you the question worth asking before anyone builds a campaign on it:
do the same five survive a different starting point, and a different half of the
customers? The machine hands back five either way, and it will never once
mention that it was not sure.

</Checkpoint>
