There is someone in the next team who can follow you across a loud open-plan
floor and cannot follow you at all on the phone.

Over a few months you collect other things about her, without meaning to. She
asks you to repeat words like _bat_ and _pat_. She loses the thread whenever you
turn to the whiteboard mid-sentence. She will not take the chair with its back
to the window. She dislikes the dim end of the pub on a Friday.

Five quirks. You file them as five quirks, because five separate things is what
they look like.

Then somebody mentions that she reads lips.

Now it is not a list. It is one fact, and every item is that fact seen from a
different angle. Lips carry across a noisy room and do not travel down a phone
line. _Bat_, _pat_ and _mat_ are made with the lips closed in the same shape, so
they arrive identical. A whiteboard turns a face away. A backlit window and a
candlelit pub do the same thing in the end: they take the light off a face.

And you can now do something you could not do an hour ago. You can name the
sixth item before you ever meet it — a surgical mask, a heavy moustache, a video
call with the camera switched off. You have stopped collecting quirks. You have
the mechanism, and the quirks are consequences of it.

Only the shape of that reasoning carries across to what follows, and it is worth
saying so before we go on: nothing in a language model resembles a person doing
anything at all. What carries across is the move. A scattered list of failures
collapses into a small number of properties, and those properties then predict
failures nobody has told you about yet.

## The question that replaces "when will they fix it"

Complaints about these systems arrive in a remarkably consistent form. It cannot
count. It invented a court case. It has never heard of the thing that happened
last month. It agreed with me when I was wrong. It got the arithmetic in the
invoice wrong and did not flinch.

The instinct is to ask when each one gets fixed, and that instinct smuggles in an
assumption: that a complaint is a **defect** — something that got in by accident,
sits on a list somewhere, and can be taken out again by people who are working on
it.

Ask something else instead. Not _when is this fixed_, but: what, exactly, in the
way this thing was built, produces that?

Two things happen when you do.

The complaint stops being mysterious. It becomes an entry in a chain of
reasoning that starts from a property you can name and ends where you are
standing.

And you get a prediction. Anything else that leans on the same property will
fail too, and you can say what before you meet it, which is the difference
between knowing how something works and having memorised a list of times it went
wrong.

That is why this unit exists, and the alternative is worth picturing. Without the
mechanism you are left collecting anecdotes, and anecdotes go stale. Half of them
stop being true when a model ships, the other half were never quite right, and
neither the person who over-trusts this technology nor the person who dismisses
it can tell you which is which. Both of them are reading the list.

You have already read everything needed to stop doing that.

## Four complaints, traced back

### It cannot count the letters in a word

The one everybody has seen. Ask how many times the letter _r_ appears in
_strawberry_, and for a long time the answer came back as two — from a model that
could, in the next breath, discuss where the word came from.

The cause is the very first thing that happens to your sentence, before any of
the interesting machinery gets involved. Text is cut into chunks, the vocabulary
of roughly a hundred thousand pieces that everything the model reads is made of,
and each chunk arrives as one entry, a number standing for the whole piece.
_Strawberry_ is not delivered as ten letters. It is delivered as two or three
items, each of them atomic.

So the question asks the model to count something it was never sent. Not
something it sees badly or attends to poorly. Something that is not in the input.

It answers anyway, because answering is the only operation in the loop. What
comes out is a plausible number for a question of that shape.

Now put the example down and use the property, because the property is the part
that travels. It predicts a whole family at once: reversing a word, taking the
third letter of something, counting syllables, an acrostic where the _last_
letters have to spell something, a simple letter-shift cipher. Same failure,
every time, and nobody had to tell you about any of them.

It predicts where the failure stops, too. Ask what a letter is, or how English
spelling drifted, or where the word _strawberry_ came from, and the answers are
fine — none of that needed the letters to arrive separately.

One honest note, because models do now get _strawberry_ right and it looks like a
counterexample. Either that exact question has appeared often enough in training
text for the answer to come back as a memorised phrase, or the system quietly
wrote a line of code and ran it. Neither of those is the model counting. Change
the word to one nobody has written a blog post about and watch it go again.

### It states false things in the voice it uses for true ones

You had a whole unit on this one, so the trace is short, and it is two
properties stacked rather than one, which is why it is so much worse to live with
than any single item here.

The first: there is no step anywhere in the machine at which a claim is held up
against anything. A true answer and an invented one are one operation meeting two
different worlds, at the same cost, in the same tenth of a second.

The second was installed separately, by people aiming at something else. Writing
on the internet is overwhelmingly confident, so hedging scores badly before
anybody tunes anything on purpose. Then preference training, people shown two
answers and asked which is better, rewards the direct answer over the one that
declines to commit, because direct reads as more helpful and helpful is what was
being judged.

Stack those two and you get the specific shape of the problem. It is not that the
machine is sometimes wrong; everything is sometimes wrong, and a source that is
wrong a tenth of the time can still be enormously useful. It is that being wrong
has no tell. Fluency is guaranteed by the design, confidence is a trained manner,
and those are the two signals a reader naturally judges by.

### It has never heard of last month

Everything the model brought out of training is in its dials — the billions of
adjustable numbers whose settings are the entire product of that process. Then
training ends and the dials stop moving. Nothing that has happened since is
anywhere in them, and nothing you say in a conversation moves one.

Which makes this the least mysterious item on the page, and the one whose two
consequences still catch people out.

The first is that it cannot reliably tell you where its own line falls. Its
cutoff is a fact about itself, and there is no route to facts about itself, so
asking produces a plausible date rather than a reported one. It may well be
wrong, and it will be stated in the same voice as everything else.

The second is about the fix people reach for. Retraining on newer text does work,
and it moves the line without removing it: a month later you are in the same
position, having paid a great deal for the privilege, with no way to point at
where any particular answer came from. The move that actually holds is the other
one. Knowledge sits either in the dials, where it is fixed and unciteable, or in
the window it reads each time, where it is current, swappable and quotable, and
putting the fact in the window is what retrieval, tools and half of the last few
Parts of this site have been about.

### It cannot try harder on a hard question

This is the one that sounds least mechanical and is the most mechanical of all.

A pass through the model is a fixed amount of arithmetic. The same operations, in
the same order, through the same layers, whatever you happened to ask. There is
no dial for effort, because there is nothing in the design for such a dial to
turn.

So _what is twelve times twelve_ and _here are six people, six seats and nine
constraints, work out who sits where_ receive exactly the same amount of
arithmetic per piece produced. Which is why a model with nothing else bolted on
hands back an immediate, confident seating plan that breaks two of the
constraints. Nothing in it noticed that the second question was harder. Noticing
that a question is hard is not one of the operations available.

And this is the item that visibly moved in the last few years, which makes it the
best test of whether reading limits as mechanisms actually survives contact with
progress.

Reasoning models write a long stretch of working before they answer, and they are
genuinely, substantially better at hard problems. Not one thing about a single
pass changed. What changed is how many passes the system spends: every piece of
that working is another complete pass, and the working is then sitting in the
text that the next pass reads. Effort became something you buy, in the only
currency the machine has — more pieces, more passes, more seconds, more money, in
direct proportion.

Notice what that is and is not. The limit was not removed. It was paid around.
Which is a real improvement, and it is also why hard questions now cost visibly
more than easy ones, and why nobody can promise you a fixed price for a hard
question.

Read the two columns as a piece of arithmetic. Eight things go in on the left and

Which is the practical reason not to memorise the left column. It is long, it
grows, and it dates: some of those rows behave differently on a model released
next spring. The right column is short, and it changes only when somebody builds
a different machine.

## Placing a complaint you have not been given

The test of whether this has landed is not whether you can recite the drawing. It
is whether a complaint you were never given has an obvious home. Here are three,
and not one of them is on it.

_It agreed with me when I pushed back, and I was the one who was wrong._ Property
B, it scores plausible continuations and nothing consults a fact, plus the same
preference training that installed the confident register. Your objection is text
appended to text, and what most plausibly follows a complaint is an apology and a
revision. Nothing anywhere compared the new answer to the old one, because
comparing is not an operation it has.

_It told me its knowledge runs to a date, and the date was wrong._ Properties C
and B stacked. The dials froze, which is C; and where they froze is a fact about
itself, to which it has no more access than to any other fact, which is B. So it
produced a date-shaped answer.

_It obeyed an instruction that was written inside a document I asked it to
summarise._ This one is not on the drawing at all, and noticing that is the
useful part. It comes from a sixth property: everything arrives as one sequence
of pieces with nothing in the machinery marking which of them has authority. Five
properties explain those eight complaints. A longer list of complaints needs one
or two more properties, and the ratio still holds. That is the claim, not the
number five.

## Which of these go away

Sort the whole list by what actually happens to a limit, and there are only three
outcomes.

**Moved outside the model.** Arithmetic goes to a calculator. This month's facts
go to a search over documents. Remembering you between conversations goes to an
ordinary database, the way it always did. Be precise about what has happened
here: the model's limit did not move a millimetre. The system stopped depending
on it. That distinction sounds pedantic until the tool call fails and the model
cheerfully answers from its own head instead.

**Bought with more of the same mechanism.** Reasoning models, and a good deal of
prompting besides. The machine is unchanged and it is run more, so the gain is
real and it arrives with a bill attached.

**Still there, because they _are_ the mechanism.** Nothing checks a claim against
the world. Nothing produces a faithful account of its own reasoning. Nothing
separates an instruction from a document. These are the ones with no fix on the
table, and the honest reason is structural: a fix would have to change one of the
properties rather than build around it.

## The sentence people want, and the honest one

The sentence people want is either _these are permanent limits of artificial
intelligence_ or _these will all be gone within two years_. Both are forecasts
about a research field, and neither is a thing anyone can hand you.

Here is what can be handed to you, and it is the stronger claim of the two
because it is the one you can check.

These are consequences of **this design**: a machine that scores the next chunk
against dials frozen at the end of training, reading one undifferentiated
sequence, at a fixed cost per pass. Every row in that left column follows from
those properties the way the phone call follows from lip-reading. As long as that
is the thing you are buying, that is the thing you get, which is not a
prediction, it is an inspection.

Something built on a different mechanism would have a different left column. It
would not have no left column.

So the test to put to any claim that a limit has been solved is a single
question: which property changed? If the answer is _we gave it a tool_, the
property is intact and the system routed around it — often exactly what you
wanted, and worth saying out loud so that nobody plans as though the model got
better. If the answer is _we trained it to be more careful_, nothing changed but
a tendency, and tendencies are not boundaries. And if somebody really has changed
one of the properties, they have built a different machine, and the first
interesting question about it is what its own left column looks like.

<Aside title="Two ways to hold this list, and both are wrong" tone="caution">
  The first is to treat it as a temporary embarrassment — real today, gone by the
  next release, so there is no point designing around it. Systems built that way
  work beautifully in the demo, because the demo is the average case, and then
  fail in the specific ways written above, at which point somebody adds a
  sternly-worded instruction and ships.

The second is to treat it as a verdict. The list is long, some of it is
embarrassing, and it is tempting to conclude that the whole thing is a parlour
trick. That reading has to explain why so many people who understand the
mechanism in detail use it every day.

Both are the same mistake in opposite directions: reading the list instead of the
mechanism. The list is a symptom, it is incomplete, and it will look different in
a year. The mechanism is what you were actually taught.

</Aside>

## The shape that is left

None of this makes the technology less useful. It makes it useful in a particular
shape, and the shape is the thing worth leaving with.

Look at where the five properties came from. Reading chunks is what lets one
modest vocabulary cover every language including ones nobody planned for.
Scoring plausible continuations is what makes it write at all. Frozen dials are
what make it cheap enough to answer a million people at once. Fixed work per pass
is what makes the answer arrive in a second. Every limit on that page is the
underside of something that had to be true for any of it to exist.

What the shape leaves is a machine that is excellent at language, pattern,
transformation and synthesis — rewriting, summarising, translating, extracting,
drafting, explaining, turning a vague description into a specific one — and
unreliable at facts recalled from memory, at arithmetic, at self-report, and at
anything requiring a check.

Systems that work are built to that shape. Supply the facts rather than trusting
recall. Hand the arithmetic to something that does arithmetic. Put the check
outside the model, where a check can actually exist. Give it the least
independence the job needs, and earn the rest by measurement.

Systems that disappoint were built as though the shape were not there. Usually by
someone who saw a very good demo and drew the wrong conclusion from it, not that
the machine is capable, which it is, but that it is capable in a different shape
than the one it has.

    `clock.today()` exists because there is no clock in a model. A date only
    reaches it if you put one there, and a system that does not will produce
    confident answers anchored to whenever training stopped.

    `pricing.total(...)` exists because arithmetic left with the model is
    arithmetic left with a next-piece scorer. What the model is good for in this
    function is working out which line items the customer meant, the
    language-shaped half, and everything downstream of that belongs in code
    that adds up.

    Worth stating what remains after both. The draft is still text produced by
    scoring continuations, so it is still capable of a confident sentence about
    a discount that does not exist. Nothing in this snippet is a check; they are
    two limits routed around, and the third one is still open.

  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    answer = model.reply(prompt, response_format=Answer)

    answer.confidence   # a number the model wrote
    answer.verified     # a boolean the model wrote
    ```

    Structured output is genuinely useful and these two fields are worth
    understanding before anyone builds on them. Asking for a schema does not
    create a measurement; it constrains the shape of the text. A field called
    `confidence` is filled by the same operation that fills every other field —
    what plausibly follows a request for a confidence figure is a confidence
    figure, usually a reassuring one.

    The tell is that `answer.verified` can be `True` in a program that contains
    no verification. Nothing was compared to anything; a well-formed boolean was
    produced.

    The useful version of both fields lives in your code and is computed from
    something outside the model — a lookup that either found the record or did
    not, a quote that either appears in the source text or does not, a number
    that either reconciles or does not. If you cannot say what the comparison
    was and where the other side of it came from, the field is decoration with a
    trustworthy name.

<Checkpoint question="A team's assistant keeps quoting last year's prices. One person proposes retraining the model every month on current company data so that it knows the prices. A second proposes leaving the model alone and putting the current price list into every request. A third says the real fix is an instruction telling it to quote a price only when it is certain. Rank the three, and for each one say what it does to the mechanism rather than to the symptom.">
  The third does nothing structural, and it is worth being exact about why.
  Certainty is not a quantity the machine computes, so there is nothing for the
  instruction to consult. What it does is shift which continuations score well,
  which is a real effect and a very small one. You will get slightly more
  hedging, including on prices that were right. No step has been added, because
  there is nowhere in the loop to add one.

The first genuinely changes the dials, which makes it the only proposal that
touches the property at all, and it still does not fix it. It moves the freeze
date rather than removing the freeze, so the problem returns on a monthly cycle.
It is expensive and slow. And it leaves every answer unciteable: nobody can say
whether a quoted price came from this month's training or last year's, because a
dial setting has no provenance.

The second never touches the model, and it is the one to ship. It puts the price
list in the window, where facts are current, swappable and quotable, and it
converts the job from recalling a price to reading one, which moves it from the
half of the shape the machine is unreliable at to the half it is excellent at.
The limit is untouched. The system stopped depending on it.

Two things to carry past this. Ranking them is easy once you ask what each does
to the mechanism, and impossible if you only look at the symptom, because all
three make the wrong prices less frequent in a demo. And the winning move here is
the same one as for arithmetic, for last week's news, and for remembering a
customer. The answer was never a better model. It was declining to ask the model
for the one thing it was never built to supply.
