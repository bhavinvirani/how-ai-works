Everything you have just read about — layers stacked on layers, the sweep
forwards, the blame pushed back — was written down, published and understood
before 1990.

It sat in textbooks. Anyone with a maths degree and a winter to spare could
code it up, and plenty of people did. Then they ran it on the photographs they
had, and it did not work. Not badly, not nearly. It ground away for a week and
came out no better than a method you could write in an afternoon.

By the middle of the nineties most of the field had drawn the sensible
conclusion and gone elsewhere. That is not stupidity. A technique that clever
people have tried properly for a decade, and that keeps losing to something
simpler, is a technique that does not work.

They were right about the results and wrong about the reason, and the gap
between those two things cost roughly twenty years.

## Three things arrived, and none of them was an idea

Nothing was missing from the instructions. What was missing was everything the
instructions quietly assumed.

**Examples, by the million.** In 1990 a large labelled collection of
photographs meant a few thousand pictures, gathered by hand by whoever's
postgraduate students could be persuaded. A deep stack holds an enormous number
of dials, and you already know what happens when a machine with that much
freedom meets a few thousand examples: it memorises them and learns nothing
worth having. Then the internet happened to everybody. By 2010 there was a
freely downloadable collection of more than a million photographs, labelled by
hand into a thousand categories. The instructions had always needed a pile like
that. Nobody had ever had one.

**Machines that could do the arithmetic.** Training is one tiny sum — multiply,
add — repeated a preposterous number of times, and the sums mostly do not
depend on each other, so they can be done all at once rather than one after
another. That is also, by complete coincidence, the shape of drawing a video
game. Graphics cards were built to perform millions of small independent sums
simultaneously because that is what shading a screenful of pixels requires, and
somebody noticed they would do a network's arithmetic just as happily.

The size of that change is easy to understate. It is not that training got
faster. It is that an experiment which used to take a month took a day — and a
field where you can try an idea before lunch behaves nothing like a field where
you get four attempts a year. What limits progress is how often you can afford
to be wrong.

**Fixes nobody would put on a poster.** The specific way deep stacks failed was
this. Blame travels backwards from the answer, and at each layer it is
multiplied by a number smaller than one. Do that twenty times and a correction
that started out respectable arrives at the bottom as a rounding error. So the
layers nearest the answer learned, the layers nearest the pixels barely moved,
and those are precisely the layers that were supposed to be finding edges.
Occasionally the numbers were larger than one instead and the correction
arrived as an infinity, which at least had the decency to be obvious.

The repairs were dull. A squashing function simple enough not to strangle the
signal on its way through; sensible starting values for the dials instead of
whatever the random number generator produced. Later came wiring that lets the
signal skip past layers altogether, which is what took stacks from tens of
layers to hundreds. Not one of these is a beautiful idea. Together they are the
difference between a stack that trains and a stack that sits there.

## The year everybody changed their mind

There was an annual competition for sorting photographs into categories, and it
had settled into the rhythm of a mature field — a percent or so of improvement
a year, won by teams who had spent months deciding, by hand, what the machine
ought to look for. Roundness. How densely corners cluster. A tally of which
directions the edges run in. Careful human expertise, written out as
instructions.

In 2012 a deep network entered and won by a margin that read like a typo. It
had been told nothing about roundness or corners. It had worked out what to
look for by itself, which is the thing the lower layers of a deep stack do.

Within two years the hand-designed instructions were gone, first from the
competition and then from most of the field. Nothing had been discovered that
week. Something had merely become possible.

## Why not just make the layer wider?

Here is the awkward fact that turns this into a real question. A network with a
single layer of neurons, if you make that layer wide enough, can approximate any
sensible pattern you care to name. That is not a hunch — it was proved in the
late eighties. Depth is not necessary.

Which means the case for depth cannot be that shallow machines are incapable.
It has to be something else, and it is: depth is about how much it costs.

## Parts, not wholes

You have already watched a stack sort itself into a ladder, and met the short
reason it bothers. This is that fact from the other side. Not "why does a ladder
turn up", but "what would it cost you not to have one" — and the only way to
answer that is to put a shallow machine and a deep one on the same job and
compare the bills.

So: look at a photograph and say whether it holds a face or a car.

The wide, shallow machine has one enormous layer between the pixels and the
answer, so every neuron in it stares straight at raw pixels and has to arrive
at a finished verdict in a single move. A neuron that fires for faces must work
out for itself, from nothing, that there are boundaries where light meets dark,
that boundaries make curves, and that certain curves make an eye. A neuron that
fires for cars works out the first two of those separately, from its own
examples, and neither one can tell the other. There is nowhere to put a shared
conclusion, because there is no floor between the pixels and the answer.

The narrow, deep machine works in stages. Its bottom layer finds edges — short
boundaries where light meets dark, at assorted angles — and that is all it
finds. The layer above assembles edges into corners, curves and textures. The
layer above that assembles those into eyes, wheels and window frames. Only at
the top does anything say "face" or "car".

Now the sentence this whole unit exists for. **An edge is an edge.** The
detector that finds a slanted boundary sits under the eye and under the wheel —
the same detector, built once, paid for by every photograph the machine has
ever seen, of anything at all. When the hundredth kind of object turns up, the
deep machine does not have to learn what an edge is. It has one.

So the two machines are billed differently. The wide one pays full price for
every new object, forever; its hundredth costs exactly what its first did. The
deep one pays full price once, for a vocabulary of parts, and after that a new
object costs only the short recipe that arranges parts which already exist.

And the price is paid in examples. Learning fewer things means needing fewer
examples to learn them, which is why the deep machine can be good at a hundred
objects on a pile of photographs that would leave the wide one hopeless at two.
The shallow network really could reach the same answer in principle. It would
need a layer of implausible width and a quantity of examples nobody has.

A joiner who makes a hundred different pieces of furniture has not learned a
hundred unrelated crafts. They have learned to cut a straight edge, cut a
joint, plane a face true — and every piece after that is those few skills,
arranged differently. Somebody who instead memorised the exact sequence of
movements for one particular chair would know precisely nothing about the next
chair and would have to start again from the beginning. Depth is what lets a
machine take the joiner's route.

<Aside title="What 'in principle' is hiding">
  The proof that one wide layer can approximate anything is an existence proof.
  It says a setting of the dials exists. It does not say how wide the layer
  would have to be — the honest answer can grow absurdly fast with the
  difficulty of the pattern — and it says nothing whatsoever about whether
  training could ever find that setting from examples. Those two questions are
  the entire practical problem. Being told the answer is somewhere in the
  building is not the same as being given the room number.
</Aside>

## Who ends up paying for the parts

Keeping somebody else's lower layers and retraining only the top is the
everyday form of all this, and you have already met it. The reuse argument is
what tells you why that habit is not a convenient trick but the shape the whole
industry has settled into.

Parts are expensive and general. Arrangements are cheap and specific. Those two
facts pull the work apart along a seam and keep it apart: a small number of
organisations spend enormous sums building vocabularies of parts out of
quantities of data nobody else can gather, and everyone else buys the vocabulary
and pays only for the arrangement on top. Every claim you will meet later about
a model "tuned on our own documents" is that split being cashed in.

It is also why nobody quotes a price for these things in layers. The bill for a
deep stack is not the depth. It is the examples and the electricity that bought
the bottom of it, and the reason a hospital or a factory can join in at all is
that somebody else has already paid.

None of which makes depth free, and more of it is not automatically better.
"Deep" is not a target to aim at. Past some point the extra layers add nothing
you can measure while costing memory, training time, and every one of the
failure modes those dull fixes were invented to hold back.

Nor is the number derived. Depth is inherited: you start from an arrangement
that already worked on something similar and adjust it, for exactly the reason
nobody can calculate a step size. The honest description is that somebody tried
several and kept whichever behaved.

<Checkpoint question="A colleague proposes skipping the stack entirely: one layer of neurons, made as wide as the budget allows, straight from pixels to answer. They point out — correctly — that a wide enough single layer can approximate any pattern at all. Nothing in that claim is false. So what is wrong with the plan, and what exactly would it cost them?">
  Nothing is wrong with the maths. Everything is wrong with the bill.

A single layer has no floor to rest shared work on. Every neuron runs from raw
pixels to a finished verdict, so whatever one of them works out about
boundaries between light and dark is unavailable to all the others, and each
new object has to be learned whole and from nothing. The cost turns up twice.
The layer has to be implausibly wide, and — the part that actually stops you —
every object needs its own full pile of examples, so the hundredth costs
exactly what the first did.

The deep stack buys a vocabulary of parts once and pays only for the
arrangement afterwards, which is why its hundredth object is cheap. Same
answers available in both machines; wildly different prices.

Worth keeping: "can, in principle" and "can, on the data and hardware you
actually have" are different claims, and almost every argument about how to
build one of these is really about the second.

</Checkpoint>
