Long before any machine could hold a conversation, one could look at a
photograph of an empty beach at dusk and tell you it was a beach at dusk. It
could pick a single face out of a crowd of two hundred. It could find a tumour
that a tired radiologist had walked past at four in the morning.

Show software from those same years a two-line hotel review, and it would
report a happy guest, because the review contained the word _lovely_.

The review said: _Lovely, if you enjoy being woken at four._

That photograph of the beach is several million numbers. The review is eight
words. Whatever beat the machine here, it was not the size of the thing.

## A photograph holds its shape

Nothing you have read so far stops working here. The training loop is the same
loop, the dials are the same dials, and the wrongness score still comes out as
one number. What changes is the material.

Pictures turn out to be unusually kind material, in three ways that are easy to
miss because they never once cause trouble.

Every pixel is in one place, and being in that place is most of what it does. A
pixel does not mean something different depending on which pixels came before
it.

Pixels that sit next to each other almost always belong to the same thing — the
same eyebrow, the same railing, the same patch of sky. So a machine that only
ever looks at small neighbourhoods, and builds up from there, is not missing
much.

And any photograph can be squashed to a standard size before the machine sees
it. A phone snap and a poster both arrive as the same rectangle of numbers, so
the machinery gets built once, for one shape.

Sentences give you none of that. They run from two words to two thousand, and
whatever reads them has to cope with all of it. Words that belong together are
routinely nowhere near each other. And a word's position is not merely where it
happens to be. It is part of what the word is saying.

Three things go wrong. The third is the one that stopped everybody.

## Move the words and the fact moves

_Ravi owes Priya._

_Priya owes Ravi._

Three words each, and the same three words. One of those sentences is going to
annoy somebody.

The simplest way to hand a sentence to a machine is to count which words are in
it and throw the order away. That has a name, **bag of words**, because a bag
is something you take items out of, not something you read a sequence from.

It is not a strawman. Bag-of-words systems scored reviews and sorted spam in
production for years, and some are still running. They cannot tell those two
sentences apart, because the two bags are identical.

That is not a subtle failure either. Word order is not decoration laid over the
meaning; in English it is most of how a sentence says who did what to whom.

And here is the part that makes language different rather than merely harder.
Cut a photograph into a thousand squares and shuffle them, and you get confetti
— something no longer an image of anything, and obviously broken. Shuffle the
words of a sentence and you can get another sentence. A perfectly grammatical
one, about somebody else, which no amount of inspecting the pieces will reveal
as the wrong answer.

## The word that settles it is nowhere near

_Don't, if the parcel arrives while I'm out, leave it by the bins._

The instruction is _don't leave it by the bins_. Those two words are seven words
apart, and the first one reverses the second.

The obvious way to keep a machine's job small is to let it read a fixed number
of words at a time — a **window**, five or six words wide, sliding along the
sentence. It is a reasonable idea. It is how a lot of early language software
worked, and it fails here in a specific way: every window that contains _leave
it by the bins_ contains no _don't_. The machine does not receive a difficult
instruction. It receives the opposite instruction, and it receives it with total
confidence.

You can widen the window, and people did. It costs, a machine looking at
fifteen words at a time has far more to weigh up than one looking at five, and,
worse, no width is ever safe. English puts no limit on how far apart two words
that decide each other can sit. A _not_ can be three sentences back. A name
introduced in the first paragraph of an email can be what the _they_ in the last
paragraph is pointing at.

## The word that settles it has not been read yet

Read this at normal speed:

_She tipped the pan into the bowl until it was empty._

You know what was empty. You did not work it out, and it did not feel like work.

Now change the last word, and nothing else:

_She tipped the pan into the bowl until it was full._

Now it is the bowl. Same eleven words in the same order except one, and the word
that changed is the last word in the sentence — a couple of words _after_ the
word it rewrites.

Notice what a machine reading left to right has in front of it at the moment
_it_ arrives. It has `She tipped the pan into the bowl until it`, and so did the
other version, because up to that point the two sentences are the same string of
characters. Whatever it decides there, it decides on evidence that is identical
in both cases and points at both answers equally.

This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to hear as "the machine
needs to be cleverer". It is not that. The information that settles the question
is not present yet. No amount of skill extracts a fact from evidence that does
not contain the fact.

Grammar cannot rescue it either. Any rule you could write — attach a pronoun to
the nearest earlier noun, or to the subject, or to whichever one is more likely
to be talked about — gives the same answer for both sentences, because
grammatically they _are_ the same sentence. The only thing that separates them
is knowing that tipping empties one container and fills the other. That is a
fact about the world, not a fact about English.

<Aside title="The name for this trick">
  A pair of sentences that differ by one word, where that word flips what a
  pronoun refers to, is called a **Winograd schema**, after the researcher who
  started collecting them in the 1970s. They were designed to be a test that
  grammar alone cannot pass. You can only answer one by knowing something about
  pans and bowls, or tow ropes, or why people win at chess.

For decades they were a quiet embarrassment: easy for any child, close to a coin
toss for the best software available. They are still used as a test today, and
modern systems get most of them right, which is the whole point of the Part you
have just started.

</Aside>

It goes much further than pronouns. _Bank_ means nothing on its own, and the
word that decides whether it is a river or a building can arrive several words
later. So can the word that tells you whether _light_ is a weight or a lamp. A
machine that must fix each word's meaning as it reads, and move on, is wrong
about all of these forever, not occasionally, but by construction.

That is what killed the obvious designs. They read left to right and commit as
they go. What this problem demands instead is one of two awkward things: wait
until the sentence is finished before deciding anything, or be able to go back
and revise a decision already made. Both are demands about the _shape_ of the
machine, not about its accuracy, and neither is what anybody had built.

## Flip the last word yourself

Below are three of these pairs. Change the ending and watch what the pronoun
turns out to mean.

One thing has to be said plainly first, because the panel would be worthless if
it were quietly untrue. **Nothing here is a model's answer.** No machine runs in
that box. Each reading is written down by hand, what the sentence means to a
person who reads it, and you can check every one of them yourself, which is
exactly why it works as a demonstration. This unit is about the problem. Nothing
on this page solves it.

<PronounFlip client:visible />

Try all three pairs first. In each one exactly one word changes, that word is
the last word of the sentence, and it lands after the pronoun it rewrites.

Then turn on **Stop reading at the pronoun**, and flip the ending again. Nothing
on the line moves. Not one word, in any of the three pairs, because everything
before the pronoun is identical in both versions, and the only difference lives
in a word the reader has not reached.

That is the whole problem in one control. The machine is not looking in the
wrong place. There is nothing in the right place to look at yet.

## Why any of this matters to you

Because you use the fixed version daily, and it is invisible when it works.

You ask an assistant about a document, then type _and what about the second
one?_ — no nouns at all, and it answers correctly. A translation system has to
pick a gendered word in French for an English sentence that never marked gender,
and finds the clue several words away. A search box treats _book a table_ and
_table a motion_ as different requests. Every one of those is this unit's
problem, handled well enough that nobody notices.

The rest of this Part is how that happened. Tokenization gets text into the
machine at all, by cutting it into standard pieces. Attention is the idea that
finally addresses the distance problem head-on, by letting any word consult any
other word directly — including ones that come after it. The transformer is what
you get when you build a machine out of nothing else.

    ```java
    String[] words = review.split(" ");
    boolean happy = Arrays.asList(words).contains("lovely");
    ```

    This is the hotel review at the top of the page, and it is not a strawman
    — real ones had a few hundred words in the list and a threshold, and
    nothing else. Its defining property is in the second line: shuffle `words`
    into any order at all and the answer does not change. That is not a bug you
    can find by testing, because it is exactly what the code was asked to do.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="python">
    ```python
    words = review.split()
    happy = "lovely" in words
    ```

    Same two lines. `set(words)` is what "bag of words" means literally, and
    the trap is that this passes review, passes its tests, and reports a
    respectable accuracy on any collection of reviews where people who write
    "lovely" are usually pleased.

<Checkpoint question="A system reads a sentence one word at a time and decides what each word means as it arrives, never going back. Which of this unit's three problems will it handle worst, and why is 'make it more accurate' not a fix for that one?">
  The third one: a word whose meaning is settled by a word that has not been
  read yet. At the moment the machine has to decide, the evidence in front of it
  is identical to the evidence it would have had if the answer were the other
  one. Accuracy is about doing better with what you have; here the thing that
  decides is not in what you have.

So the fix cannot be a better reader. It has to change the shape of the
reading — either wait until the whole sentence has arrived before committing to
anything, or keep the ability to go back and revise. That is a structural
demand, and meeting it is what the rest of this Part is about.

</Checkpoint>
