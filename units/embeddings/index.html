The map nobody drew

The ironmonger’s on the corner has been there since before the war, and nothing in it is where you would put it. Bin 3,180 is brass hinges. Bin 3,181 is fuse wire. Bin 3,182 is bath plugs.

There is a system. Everything went into the next empty bin on the day it arrived, and the numbers have been climbing ever since.

Ask the owner for bin 3,180 and it is in your hand in four seconds. Ask him for “something like this, only longer” and the numbers are no help to him at all. He walks off into the back of the shop and finds it from memory, because nothing about 3,181 tells you what is in it. All it tells you is what turned up next.

A machine handed a pile of words is standing in that shop.

A number can be a name without being a meaning

Everything inside a machine is arithmetic. Dials get multiplied, sums get compared, one number gets nudged a little closer to another. The word happy does not take part in any of that. It is not a number, and there is no obvious number it ought to be.

So the first move is the shop’s move. Take every word anybody uses, sort them into one long list, and give each word its row number. This is not a bad idea and real systems really do it — the number is a name, a way of saying which word you mean in a form a computer can hold on to.

What the number is not is a meaning. Sorted alphabetically, row 1,742 is cat and row 1,743 is catapult, which are neighbours by an accident of spelling. Kitten is three thousand rows away.

That is not merely untidy. It means a machine that has been shown a million sentences about cats has learned nothing at all about kittens, because as far as its arithmetic is concerned those are two unrelated names. Every word has to be learned from scratch, on its own, from however few examples it happened to get. Rare words never stand a chance.

A position instead

Change one thing. Instead of a single number that names the word, give each word a position — a couple of numbers saying where it sits, the way a grid reference says where a village is.

The numbers have not become more meaningful on their own. What has appeared is the space between them. Two words now have a distance, and once there is a distance, an arrangement can be judged: put words that mean similar things close together and words with nothing in common far apart, and near starts carrying information that a row number never could.

A machine that learns something about one word now automatically knows a little about everything sitting nearby, because everything it does with a position it does with the positions around it too.

That position is called the word’s embedding, and the whole arrangement is an embedding space. It is a graceless name for one of the prettiest ideas in the subject.

Nobody chose the positions

Here is the part that makes it work, and it is the fill-in-the-blank game wearing a different hat.

Nobody sits down and arranges the words. The positions are learned — cover a word, make the machine guess it, score how far off the guess was, nudge. The positions are among the dials being nudged.

Think about what winning that game requires. If the machine is guessing what goes in the ——— pulled into the yard, then whatever it does with lorry it had better also do with truck, because either one could be the answer and it has no way of knowing which. The cheapest arrangement that achieves this is to put those two words in the same place.

That is worth saying precisely, because it is easy to get backwards. Words do not end up near each other because they appear together. Lorry and truck almost never appear in the same sentence. They end up near each other because they appear in the same company — surrounded by yards and drivers and motorways, in gaps where each of them would have done.

Run that over billions of sentences and the map builds itself. Nobody defined similar. It fell out of the fact that similar words get used in similar company, which was sitting in the text all along waiting for someone to notice.

The word “word” is doing temporary work here

Everything in this unit says “word”, and two topics from now that turns out to be slightly too neat. What a language model actually reads are pieces — sometimes a whole word, sometimes a fragment of a long one, sometimes a space and a comma travelling together.

That correction changes what gets a position. It changes nothing else here. Whatever the pieces turn out to be, each one gets a position, distance between them still means similarity, and everything below still holds. Read “word” as “whatever unit the machine reads” and you will not have to take any of this back.

Walk around a small one

Below is a map with twenty-five words on it. The marker starts on king.

Leave the arrows alone at first and just move. The two sliders slide the marker across the map and up it, and the sentence underneath names the words nearest to wherever you have put it. Drop it on kitten and the nearest three are puppy, cat and dog. Drop it on doctor and they are teacher, nurse and engineer. Nobody wrote either of those lists.

Notice how much of the map is empty. Most positions are not near anything at all, which will matter in a minute.

The arrows are the surprising bit

Now the second thing, which is stranger than the first.

Set the arrow to man → woman. That is not a setting; it is a measurement. It is the step you would take to walk from the dot marked man to the dot marked woman, and the map shows it faintly, where it was measured.

The marker is still on king. Take that same step, that exact length in that exact direction, from there instead. It ends on queen.

Do it again from uncle and it ends on aunt. From boy it ends on girl. One arrow, measured once, between one pair of words — and it keeps landing on the counterpart of whatever you start it from.

Then try the other arrow, puppy → dog, which if it is about anything is about growing up. Lay it down at kitten and it ends on cat. Lay it down at boy, which is nowhere near the animals, and it ends on man.

You will meet this written as arithmetic, and it is worth being able to read:

king−man+woman≈queen\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen}king−man+woman≈queen

In words: start from where king sits, take away man, add woman, and you end up beside queen. Subtracting one word and adding another is only another way of writing “take the step that leads from man to woman”, and every word in that line is a position, so the plus and minus are the ordinary ones.

Nobody built those directions. Nobody checked that the man-to-woman step would work starting from uncle. It works because of how the words were used by the people who wrote the text, and the arrangement had no cheaper way to keep all of that straight.

What the demonstration is not telling you

Three honest corrections, because this idea is usually oversold.

The map above is drawn by hand. Twenty-five words, placed deliberately, so that there would be something small enough to look at. The shape of it is faithful — that is what real maps do — but no machine learned this one.

Two numbers is a flattening. A real word map gives each word a few hundred numbers, sometimes a few thousand. Not two axes but hundreds of them at once, which nobody can picture and which does not squash down onto a page without losing most of what is in it. Every flat picture of an embedding space you have ever seen, including this one, is a shadow.

The arithmetic is patchier than the demonstration suggests. Put the marker on kitten and follow the man → woman arrow. It lands in open ground, nearest to the word it set off from, because this map holds no feminine of a kitten and was never going to invent one. Most starting points do that, and real maps behave the same way — which is why the usual way of running the famous demonstration quietly forbids the answer from being one of the three words you started with. Without that rule the answer that comes back is very often king itself. The trick is real. It is not the clean machine it looks like on a slide.

The idea is not really about words

The move generalises, and this is where most of its working life is spent. Anything you can teach a machine to place can go on a map like this: photographs, songs, products, faces, molecules, whole documents. Similar things land near each other for the same reason and by the same route.

Once things are positions, “find me more like this one” stops being a question about meaning and becomes a question about distance, which a computer can answer for a billion items without understanding any of them. That is what is behind the search box that copes with you not using the site’s words, and the row of recommendations under whatever you just watched.

It is also where every language model starts. Before the machinery in the rest of this site gets to do anything, the incoming text is turned into positions. Everything after that is arithmetic on the map.

The map learns the prejudice too

Put the marker on doctor in the instrument above and follow the man → woman arrow. It lands on nurse.

That is not a joke somebody dropped into the demonstration. Word maps built from real text put those two words in exactly that relationship, and the early ones were shown doing it — along with putting traditionally male occupations nearer to male words and traditionally female ones nearer to female words. Nobody intended it and nobody wrote it down. The maps were built out of how people actually write, and that is how people actually write.

It matters because these maps are load-bearing. They sit underneath systems that sort job applications and rank search results, where “near” quietly becomes “relevant”, and a machine reproducing the average of what it read is doing exactly what it was built to do.

For developers
float[][] table = new float[vocabularySize][300];  // one row per word

int id = 1742;                 // "cat", from the alphabetical list
float[] cat = table[id];       // the meaning, as 300 numbers

Three lines, and the whole unit is in them. The id is the name — a row number, chosen by whoever built the list. The row it points at is the position, and those 300 numbers are dials like any other, tuned by training. Nobody ever types a value into that array.

Checkpoint

The words 'lorry' and 'truck' almost never turn up in the same sentence as each other. A map built by reading text puts them practically on top of one another anyway. How did that happen?

Show answerHide answer

Because position comes from the company a word keeps, not from the words it appears beside. Both of them show up surrounded by yards, drivers, loads and motorways, in gaps where either would have been a good guess — so anything the machine does with one of them it needs to do with the other, and the cheapest way to arrange that is to put them in the same place.

Worth holding on to, because it also tells you what the map is not. It is not a record of what words mean. It is a record of how they were used by the people who wrote the text it read, which is why it can be right about lorries and trucks and wrong about doctors and nurses in the same breath, without anything having gone wrong.
