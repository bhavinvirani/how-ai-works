The jobs nobody handed out

A photograph lands on your desk as two hundred thousand brightness readings, and your job is to say whether there is a cat in it.

You already know better than to sit down and write the rule. You will collect examples and let a machine tune itself against them. But that leaves a question nobody warns you about at the start: tune itself against what?

Not those two hundred thousand readings. Move the cat two centimetres to the left and every single number changes, while the answer does not. A machine handed the raw grid would have to learn “cat” all over again for every position, every size and every lighting condition a cat might turn up in, and there is no pile of examples large enough for that.

So somebody decides first what is worth measuring. How much of the picture is fur-coloured. Whether there are two dark ovals about a head’s width apart. How sharply the brightness changes at each point, and in which direction. Those measurements go to the machine, and the machine learns from those.

That somebody was a person. For roughly the first two decades of any of this working at all, it was thousands of people, and their measurements had names, papers and whole careers behind them. The machine learned the last step. A human being invented everything it was learning from.

The word for something worth measuring

A feature is one of those measurements — a single number pulled out of an example because somebody believed it helps tell one answer from another. Fur-colouredness is a feature. So is the number of capital letters in an email, and so was floor area when you priced a house.

Hand-made features work. They have a ceiling, though, and the ceiling has a particular shape.

The first problem is that they do not travel. A set of measurements refined over five years for finding faces tells you nothing whatsoever about finding tumours on a scan, or a cracked weld on a production line. Every new job started from an empty page, and the page took years to fill.

The second problem is worse. A feature is a guess about what matters, made by someone who cannot see what their own eyes are doing. You can pick your brother out of a crowd in a tenth of a second and you cannot say how — and the same locked door that stops you writing the rule stops you naming the right measurements. What got written down was whatever happened to be describable, which is not the same list as whatever happens to be useful.

So that was the ceiling, and it is what the rest of this unit exists to get past. Every problem needed a person to invent its vocabulary from scratch, one problem at a time, arriving at results everyone involved agreed were mediocre.

The layers nobody gave a job to

So take the person out.

Hand the machine the raw grid after all — but instead of two dials sitting on the end of somebody’s measurements, give it a stack of layers, each one feeding the next. Then run the loop.

Here is the part worth being precise about, because you have just watched it happen. When an answer comes out wrong, the blame travels backwards. Every layer is told how much of that wrongness was its doing, and every dial inside it moves a little in the direction that would have made the number smaller.

Now notice what is absent from that sentence. Nobody ever tells layer three what to look for. Layer three has no target of its own, no label to match, nothing anywhere describing a corner or a curve or the texture of fur. The only message it ever receives is “of the final wrongness, this much was yours”, and the only thing it can do about it is move.

Do that a few million times, then stop the machine and look inside. The layers have arranged themselves.

What is actually in there

The first layer has become an edge finder. Its neurons respond to a boundary between light and dark, each one at its own particular angle, and to nothing else. On their own they are worthless — an edge fires exactly the same way on a cat, a car and an empty car park.

The layer above takes those edges and responds to arrangements of them. Two meeting at a right angle. Three fanning out from a point. A run of them bending into a curve. Still nothing you would give a name to.

Above that, patterns: stripes, mesh, the specific busyness of fur.

Higher again, and neurons start answering to things you can name out loud. One fires for eye-shaped arrangements wherever they sit in the frame. Another for wheels. Another for the diagonal of an ear against a background.

At the top, a neuron responds to cats — upside down, half behind a chair, or the wrong colour entirely — because it is not looking at pixels at all. It is reading the layer below it, which is reading the layer below that.

Nobody wrote any of that down. There was no design document with “layer 2: corners” in it. There was a stack of dials and a signal saying you were wrong by this much.

The same shape, built out of sentences

None of this is a fact about pictures.

Feed a stack like that a few million customer reviews instead, with nothing to go on but whether each writer ended up happy, and the same thing happens in the same order. Early layers settle on the small mechanical business of language — which endings mark a plural, which words tend to follow “the”.

Middle layers hold phrases and the joins between them: which noun a verb belongs to, and which of the three people already mentioned is the one “she” now means.

Higher layers carry something much closer to what the passage is about. That a complaint is being made. That the writer is being sarcastic. That the second half of the message contradicts the first.

Same stack, same loop, same total silence about what any middle layer is for. Only the raw material changed — which is what makes this a fact about learning rather than a fact about photographs.

Why a ladder, of all things

It is worth asking why this particular arrangement, since nothing ever asked for it.

The loop only does one thing: make the wrongness score smaller. So the real question is not why the machine built a ladder. It is whether a ladder is the cheapest way to be less wrong with a fixed number of dials — and it is, for a plain reason.

Every cat photo, every car photo and every photo of a kitchen contains edges, and there are only so many ways two edges can meet. A layer that works out edges once has done a job that every later layer, on every image it will ever see, gets for free. The alternative is storing each object as its own arrangement of raw pixels, and there are not enough dials in the machine for that, nor enough photographs in the world.

Shared parts are simply smaller to hold than whole answers. The hierarchy is what thrift looks like when the only pressure in the system is to be less wrong.

What people do with this

Here is the consequence that put this idea in ordinary working life rather than in a paper.

A network trained on millions of photographs has, in its lower layers, a general-purpose vocabulary of visual parts — edges, corners, textures — and not one rung of that is specifically about cats. So nobody sensible trains from scratch. You take the finished network, keep everything except the top, bolt on a fresh final layer for your own categories, and train that.

It is how a hospital with four thousand labelled scans gets a working detector, when starting from nothing would have needed several million. The ladder was paid for by somebody else’s data, and it carries over, because the bottom of it is about images in general rather than about the thousand things that network was originally asked to name.

The name for this is transfer learning, and it is by a wide margin the most common thing anyone does with a deep network they did not train themselves.

Tidier in the diagram than in the machine

The clean version — this layer does edges, that one does eyes — is the version the field can show you, and it flatters the truth a little. A single neuron rarely holds one tidy concept. A concept is usually smeared across many neurons, and one neuron will often respond to several unrelated things at once. The famous photographs of “the neuron that fires for faces” are the ones that were easiest to visualise, which is a form of selection. The direction of travel is solid and turns up in every network anyone has opened. The one-neuron-one-idea picture is a simplification, and working out what is really stored where is an open field of research with its own name, interpretability.

For developers
// Before: a person's judgement, hand-written, one problem at a time.
double[] features = { furRatio(img), edgeHistogram(img), symmetry(img) };
Label answer = classifier.predict(features);

// After: the features are layers, and nobody wrote them.
Label answer = network.predict(img.pixels());

The second version deletes the file its author was proudest of. In practice you write neither — you load a stack somebody else trained and replace only the end of it.

Network base = Networks.pretrained("resnet50");   // keep: the ladder
base.replaceHead(new Dense(myLabels.size()));     // train: your problem

Checkpoint

A team has a network trained on millions of ordinary photographs and 800 labelled chest X-rays. They freeze every layer except the last, retrain that one, and have something useful by the afternoon. Why does keeping a stranger's layers work at all, when that network has never seen an X-ray?

Show answerHide answer

Because the lower layers were never about cats and dogs in the first place. Nobody ever told them to be. They only ever received a share of the blame for a wrong answer, and what survived that pressure was a vocabulary of edges, corners and textures — which an X-ray has as much as a holiday snap does. The part that really was about the original categories is the top, and the top is exactly what got thrown away.

Turn it around and the point gets sharper. Freeze the top and retrain the bottom, and they would have kept the one specific rung and discarded the general ones — the same 800 scans, the same afternoon, and nothing to show for it.
