How far to step

The shower in a hotel room is too cold. You know which way to turn the tap. That part is obvious, and it is not the problem.

You turn it a hair’s width and wait. Still cold. Another hair’s width, wait again. Ten minutes later you are washing in something tepid and telling yourself it is fine.

So the next morning you turn it properly — a quarter of the way round. Scalding. Back a quarter. Freezing. Forward a quarter. Scalding again, and now you are angry at a tap.

You never once turned it the wrong way. Every single move was in the correct direction. What you could not do was choose how much to turn it by.

The direction is the easy half

A machine being trained stands somewhere on a landscape of wrongness — high ground means bad answers, low ground means good ones — and it can work out exactly which way that ground falls away beneath it. That calculation is the machine’s own work, and it comes out the same whatever happens next.

What it does not produce is a distance. “Downhill, and to the left” is a direction. It says nothing about whether you should move a centimetre or a mile.

So a person decides instead. Before training begins, somebody types in a number that says how far each nudge goes; from then on every dial moves by that number multiplied by how steep the ground is. The number is called the learning rate, and it is nothing more exotic than the size of the step.

new setting=old setting−(step size×slope)\text{new setting} = \text{old setting} - (\text{step size} \times \text{slope})new setting=old setting−(step size×slope)

In words: take where the dial sits now, and move it against the slope by an amount the step size scales. Everything on the right of that line except the step size is worked out by the machine. That one term is yours.

The multiplication is worth pausing on. Steep ground means a large slope, so the same step size carries the dial further there. Usually that is a kindness — big moves while you are badly wrong, small ones once you are nearly right. It is also the mechanism by which the whole thing tears itself apart.

Two ways to be wrong, and they are opposites

Too small and it never arrives. This is not merely slow. Serious models train on rented machines billed by the hour, so “it would get there eventually” stops being a defence when eventually is nine months. A tiny step has a quieter failure too: it settles into the first shallow dip it meets, because from inside a small dip the ground looks flat in every direction.

Too big and it never settles. Overshoot the bottom and you land part-way up the opposite slope. Look at where that leaves you: further from the bottom means steeper ground, and steeper ground means the next step — same step size, larger slope — is bigger still. Bigger step, worse overshoot, steeper ground again. Three or four rounds of that and the numbers coming out of the machine stop being numbers at all.

That feedback is the part to hold on to. A step that is too large does not make training a bit worse. It makes training destroy itself, and it does so faster the worse it gets.

Between the two there is a band of sizes that work. The band always exists. Where it sits is different for every model, every collection of examples, and often for different stages of the same training run — which is why nobody can hand you a number and promise it.

Wind your own step size up slowly. Around 0.30 your runner drops to the bottom in a few moves and stays there — that is what a healthy training run looks like. Keep going and it starts overshooting, arriving in a wobble rather than a glide, which is untidy but still works.

Then set it to exactly 1.00, where the third runner sits. It bounces between two points forever, landing at precisely the same height every time: working extremely hard, making no progress at all. Push past that and each bounce lands higher than the last, and near the top of the slider the runner is off the hill within a couple of steps.

What the number usually looks like

Real training rarely uses a friendly value like the ones on that slider. Something around 0.001 is common, and moving it to 0.003 can be the whole difference between a model that trains and one that does not. The slider here is stretched out so that both failures fit on one screen; the shape of the problem is identical at any scale.

The setting the machine cannot learn

Everything else inside a model gets tuned by training. So why not this?

Because the machine only improves a number when it can feel which way that number should move, and there is nothing to feel here. The only honest test of a step size is to run the training with it and see what comes out — which costs exactly as much as the training. There is no slope to sample.

That splits the vocabulary in two, and you will meet both words everywhere:

  • Parameters are the dials inside the machine. Training tunes them, and there are billions.
  • Hyperparameters are the settings about the training itself: the step size, how many passes are made over the examples, how many examples are looked at before each nudge. People choose these.

“Hyperparameter tuning” is the grand name for choosing them. The honest name is educated trial and error — run several, keep whatever behaved. There is no formula, and there is no default that is safe.

What people actually do

In practice the step size is not one number but a plan. Start with a long stride to cross the landscape quickly, then shrink it as training goes on so the last moves are small and careful. It is exactly how you would walk down a real hill in fog: striding while the ground is obviously falling away, shuffling once it flattens out.

Many runs do the opposite for their first few hundred steps, starting absurdly small and growing. The first position is a random one, the ground there can be viciously steep, and a full-size step from a random start is the classic way to destroy a run in its first minute.

For developers
double stepSize = 0.10;

for (double[] batch : batches) {
  weight -= stepSize * slope(weight, batch);   // the whole of training
}

stepSize *= 0.90;   // and the stride shortens before the next pass

Two things are worth noticing. The update is one subtraction — everything a framework calls an optimiser is wrapping that line. And stepSize is declared outside the loop and changed by code somebody wrote, not by anything the loop worked out for itself.

Checkpoint

A training run's wrongness score falls steadily for a few hundred steps, then climbs, then turns into a value that is not a number at all. Nothing about the model or the examples changed. What would you suspect first?

Show answerHide answer

The step size, and specifically that it is too large. The giveaway is the order of events: the score fell first, so the machine was finding the right direction perfectly well. Only the distance was wrong. Once a step overshoots the bottom it lands on steeper ground, which makes the next step larger, which overshoots further — and that runs away in a few rounds rather than degrading gently. Halving the step size is the standard first move.

Keep hold of the shape of this failure. A system that improves for a while and then collapses is not usually a system that has learned something wrong. It is usually a system taking steps that are too big to land.
