Nobody could write it, but anyone could choose

Write down what makes a good apology.

Not an example of one. The rule — the thing you could hand to a stranger who has to write an apology by Friday and has never written one before.

Most people get three or four lines in. Say what you did. Do not explain why it was reasonable. Say what you will do instead. Then it starts coming apart, because you can think of a good apology that breaks every line of it, and a dreadful one that obeys them all.

Now read these two.

I am sorry you were upset by what I said. It was a difficult week for everyone. Best to put it behind us.

I said it in front of your team and I should not have. You were right to be angry. I spoke to them this morning and told them I was wrong.

You did not need the rule. You knew which one you would rather have been given, and it took about a second.

That gap is the subject of this unit: the distance between not being able to write a rule down and being perfectly able to judge two attempts at it. Almost everything about how a modern assistant behaves comes out of somebody noticing that gap and building a machine in it.

Why the previous stages stopped working

A machine only ever improves against one number. That was the whole of the loss function: pick what to count, and whatever you counted is what the machine becomes good at.

Every stage of training so far had one, and had it for free. Reading the internet, the number was there in the text itself — the next word either was the word that turned up or it was not. Being shown worked examples of requests and good responses, it was the same again — the response either used the words the example used or it did not.

Then somebody asks for the number that says whether an answer was good.

Helpful, honest, admits what it does not know, does not waffle, does not agree with you to keep the peace, gives you the caveat that matters and leaves out the four that do not. Try to make that countable and you are back writing the rule for an apology. It is not that the job is hard. It is that nobody has ever managed it, in any field, for anything of this shape.

So the model that came out of the previous stage will answer you. Whether it answers you well is not something anyone can measure, which means it is not something anyone can train.

The move: stop describing, start comparing

Here is the whole trick, and it is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is one of the genuinely lovely ideas in the subject.

You do not have to write the rule. You have to be shown two answers and say which one you would rather have got.

That is a judgement almost anybody can make — quickly, without training, and about answers they could not have produced themselves. Most people could not write a decent wedding speech to order. Shown two, they would know which one to give, and they would mostly agree with each other about it. Judging is a far cheaper skill than specifying, and the gap between the two is where this whole stage lives.

What comes back from one comparison is thin: no score, no reason, just a direction. This one, not that one. But collect enough of them and the thinness stops mattering, in the same way that no single person’s opinion of a restaurant tells you much and ten thousand of them tell you a great deal.

Turning a pile of choices back into a number

A pile of comparisons is still not a loss. It says something about the answers people looked at, and training needs a number for answers nobody has ever seen, generated a moment ago, at a rate of thousands a minute.

So you train a second model on the comparisons. Its job is not to answer anything. Given a question and an answer, it predicts which way people would have gone, and to do that it has to produce a number, so that two answers can be put in order. That model is the reward model, and it is easiest to think of as a judge: a stand-in for the taste of everyone who did the picking, fast enough to consult a few million times.

Now the number exists. And the moment it exists, the loop from reinforcement learning has everything it needs.

The model writes an answer. The judge scores it. The dials move in whatever direction produced a higher score, exactly the try-score-adjust cycle you have already met, with human approval standing in as the reward. Do that for long enough and you have shaped behaviour that nobody was able to describe.

The whole procedure is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, usually shortened to RLHF. It is the stage that turns something that can answer into something you would want to ask.

Why the model is kept on a leash

Left alone in that loop, a model does not become helpful. It goes hunting for whatever the judge happens to score highly, and judges have blind spots, so it finds some strange, repetitive form of words that scores wonderfully and reads like nothing a person would write. This is reward hacking, exactly as it was in the boat race, and it is entirely predictable here. The standard fix is a leash: alongside the reward, a penalty for drifting too far from the model it started as. Score high, but do not stop sounding like yourself.

Do the rating yourself

Ten questions, two answers each. Take whichever you would rather have been given. There is no right answer, and nothing is reported back until the tenth pick, which is exactly as much as a paid rater gets while they work.

Pick the better of two

You never write an answer. Pick a winner ten times and watch a personality nobody described come out the other end.

Nothing recorded yet. There is no right answer here — take whichever you would rather have been given.

Whatever you picked, four numbers came out the other end, and you were never asked about a single one of them. They were read off your choices.

Now press reset and go round again as one particular kind of rater: take whichever answer gets to the point. Every pair has one, so there is nothing to decide.

Getting to the point ends at +1.00, which is only a record of what you did. The other three rows are where the interest is.

Being friendly about it comes out at −0.71: in six of the seven pairs where one answer was friendlier, you took the other one. You were not avoiding friendliness. You were taking the short answer, and in this collection the friendlier answer is nearly always the longer one, so your scorer cannot pull the two apart. Nor can the real thing. A well-known and thoroughly measured result about real preference data is that raters mildly prefer longer answers, and the reward model learns length as though it were quality.

Going along with you lands at −0.50. In three of the four pairs where one answer contradicted the asker, you took the contradiction, so your scorer now mildly rewards being told otherwise. Nobody asked you to have a view on that.

Stating it flatly reads exactly 0.00 — three pairs each way. Read that carefully, because it is not the scorer being even-handed. An answer that hedges and an answer that commits now get precisely the same score, and no amount of training against this scorer will ever move a model one way or the other on it. It is not neutral. It is blind.

Then the eleventh pair, which you were never asked to rate. Your scorer gives the plain answer +2.21 and the flattering one −2.21, and it prefers the plain one, which happens to be the true one. Notice how little that has to do with you. Not one of the ten questions asked whether an answer was correct. The true answer came out ahead as a by-product of your taste for short and direct, and it is luck rather than judgement.

Now be somebody else. Reset, and this time take the friendliest answer every time. Three pairs have no friendlier answer; take either, it changes nothing that follows.

Being friendly about it pegs at +1.00 and going along with you at +0.50, which is what you did. Getting to the point goes negative, somewhere between −0.20 and −0.80 depending on those three free choices, although you never once chose an answer for being long-winded.

And the eleventh pair turns over. The flattering answer wins on this scorer, and the flattering answer is the false one. A model trained against it would be pushed towards the ten per cent myth, and every part of the machinery would be working correctly while that happened.

Two readers. Ten picks each. Neither wrote anything down, neither was asked about truth, and both scorers do exactly what they were built to do. They simply disagree about an answer neither of them ever saw.

What is real here, and what is stage scenery

The mechanism is real. Comparisons in, a model fitted to predict them, a number out, and that number then scoring answers nobody looked at. That is RLHF, not an impression of it. The instrument also gets one detail right that is easy to miss: only the gap between two scores means anything. The scale itself is arbitrary, in the toy and in the real thing.

What is staged is the four tidy qualities. A real reward model does not have four named dials; it has billions of the ordinary unreadable kind, and there is no readout anywhere in it saying how much it likes brevity. Teams find that out the way you would find out anything about a black box — by feeding it things and watching what it scores highly.

So the honest version is worse than the panel above, not better. A real collection of preferences carries the same tangles between qualities, the same blind spots on whatever the raters were inconsistent about, and no way to look them up.

Whatever got picked is what you get

A machine becomes good at what you score it on. That was already true when somebody was choosing what to count. It is louder here, because now the thing being counted is whether people approved, and approval is not truth.

Follow that through and a great deal of familiar behaviour stops being mysterious.

Raters, mildly and on average, prefer a confident answer to a hedged one. So models sound certain, including when they should not, and the hedging that would have been useful was trained out of them by people ticking a box.

Raters, mildly and on average, prefer being agreed with. So models agree — including with things you have got wrong, and including after you push back on a correct answer they gave a moment earlier. This has a name, sycophancy. It is measured rather than anecdotal, and it is one of the more stubborn problems in the field.

Raters, mildly and on average, prefer thorough-looking answers. So you get the opening restatement of your question, the bulleted middle, and the summary at the end that says nothing new.

None of these were bolted on. Nobody wrote a rule asking for any of them. They are the average of a great many small preferences, and they are what got picked.

Who actually did the picking

Somebody sat and made those comparisons: workers hired for it, usually through contractors, often paid per task, working from a written guide that runs to tens of pages and says what the company means by helpful, honest and harmless. That guide is the one piece of specification that does exist, and note what it specifies. Not the answer. How to compare two of them.

So a model’s manner is the average taste of a particular group of people, in particular countries, working particular hours, reading a particular document. There is nothing sinister in that, and it is not a detail: it is where the values of the thing come from, and it is a fact about a labour arrangement rather than about the technology. Ask whose preferences these are. It is a reasonable question and it has an answer.

Where you actually meet this

Every mainstream assistant you have used has been through this stage or a close variant of it, and it is the stage that made them usable. It is also where most of the visible differences between them come from — much the same architecture, much the same pretraining, different raters and different guidance, noticeably different manner.

And when one of them behaves in a way that annoys you, the useful question is no longer only what number it was asked to make smaller. It is: what did somebody, at some point, pick?

For developers
record Preference(
  String prompt,
  String chosen,     // the answer the rater took
  String rejected    // the one they did not
) {}

The whole shape of the problem is in the fields that are missing. There is no correct, no score, no rating. A rater never produced one, because producing one is the thing nobody can do. Everything downstream — the reward model, the loop, the finished assistant’s manner — is built out of a pile of these three-string records and nothing else.

Checkpoint

Two companies start from the same pretrained model and run this same stage, with the same method and about the same amount of feedback. The assistants they ship behave noticeably differently — one hedges, one commits; one pushes back, one goes along. Nobody at either company wrote down what a good answer is. Where did the difference come from?

Show answerHide answer

From the people who did the picking and the guidance they were given. The procedure never contained a specification of a good answer, at either company. All it contained was a great many small comparisons, and the model’s manner is the average of those, so two different groups of raters, or the same raters under a different guide, produce two different averages and therefore two different assistants.

Worth keeping, because it puts the question in the right place. Asking what a model believes, or what it values, sounds like a question about the machine. In practice it is very often a question about a set of comparisons somebody collected, and about who was in the room when they were made.
