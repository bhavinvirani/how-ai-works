The table underneath everything

A friend tells you her company has built something that spots which customers are about to cancel. You ask to see it, expecting a diagram, or at least a screen full of code. She turns her laptop round, and there is a spreadsheet.

Fourteen thousand rows, one per customer. About a dozen columns: months with us, what they paid, how many times they rang the help desk. And over on the right, a column of yeses and noes — did they leave in the end.

That spreadsheet is not a summary of what the machine learned from. It is not a starting point for it. It is the whole of it.

Rows across, columns down

Read one row across and you get a single case that already finished: a real customer, some things that were true of them, and what eventually happened.

Read one column down and you get one thing somebody decided to measure, recorded the same way for everybody. Each column is one question asked of every row.

That is the entire structure, and the grid has a name: the training data. So do the two things inside it — a row is an example, a column is a feature — and neither name adds anything you have not just seen.

What is worth sitting with is what is not in the grid. The machine never meets a customer. It never sees a face, hears a complaint, or knows what the product does. It gets the numbers, and everything it later appears to understand came from there.

The column you point at

Now look again at that last column, the yeses and noes. Nothing about it is different in kind. Somebody recorded whether the customer left in exactly the way they recorded what the customer paid.

What makes it the answer is that a person pointed at it and said: this is the one I want guessed. The chosen column has a name — the label — and the name matters far less than the thing underneath it. Nothing in the data told you which column to choose. You chose.

Point at a different column and the same fourteen thousand rows become a different machine. Nominate monthly payment instead and you have built something that estimates what a customer will pay, from how long they stay and how often they ring. Same rows. Same measurements. A different product, useful to different people.

There is one real constraint on the choice, and it is about timing rather than data. The answer column has to be something you will not have at the moment you need the guess. You can train on “did they leave” precisely because these customers’ stories are over. For the person who signed up this morning that cell is empty — and that emptiness is the only reason anybody built the machine.

You have built a price estimator. Show it a bicycle and it hands back a number in pounds. Every other column is now a clue. The column that pulls the answer furthest apart is sold in a week, at 54%.

Sort the bicycles by one column, cut the list in half, and see how far apart the two halves end up on the answer. Further apart means a more useful clue.

The instrument ranks the leftover columns by how far each one pulls the answer apart. Two results are worth stopping on.

Frame size is a real measurement, taken carefully, in centimetres — and it finishes last whichever column you nominate. A column earns its keep by relating to the answer, and no amount of care in the measuring creates that relationship.

Then make asking price the answer and look at what wins: whether the bicycle sold within a week. Which is true, and useless. On the morning you set a price nothing has sold yet. The strongest column in your table is one you will never have in time — and that mistake, which gets its own unit later, almost always arrives looking exactly like this.

Everything has to become numbers

A table of customers is easy to picture because the measurements were numbers already. Most of what people want from machines is not — though two of the three hard cases were never really a problem.

A photograph is already numbers: every dot on the screen is three of them, saying how much red, how much green and how much blue. A sound is already numbers too, a long list of readings of how hard the air pushed, tens of thousands of times a second. You only have to stop thinking of them as pictures and sounds.

Words are the hard one. There is no obvious number for “happy”, and no ordering that puts it sensibly between two other words. Getting language into a shape a machine could do arithmetic on took decades, and it gets its own unit.

Why people say the data is the model

A machine learns the patterns that are in its table. All of them — including the ones nobody meant to put there.

Suppose the rows are twenty years of loan decisions, and for most of those years the decisions quietly went against applicants from one part of the city. The machine has no opinion about that and cannot form one. It finds the pattern, because finding patterns is all it does, and applies it to every application that arrives — faster and more consistently than any person managed.

Which is why “the model is biased” is nearly always a sentence about a spreadsheet.

Where the time actually goes

Most of the work on a system that runs is not modelling. It is finding data, cleaning it, working out why one column is empty for everything before March, and arguing about what a column even means. A plain method on a good table beats a clever method on a poor one, almost every time.

For developers
record Customer(
    int monthsWithUs,
    double monthlyPayment,
    int helpdeskCalls,
    boolean left) {}

Nothing here says which component is the answer. left is a field like any other — same syntax, same accessor, no annotation singling it out. Which one becomes the label is decided elsewhere, in whatever code does the training, and you could reverse it tomorrow without touching this line.

Checkpoint

Your table has a column, filled in by the support team, called 'refund issued'. You are trying to predict which customers will cancel. Should it be one of the clues?

Show answerHide answer

Only if it is reliably filled in before you need the prediction. If refunds are usually issued during the cancellation call, that column is not a clue about cancelling — it is a piece of the cancellation arriving early. A model trained on it scores beautifully in testing and is worthless on anybody who has not cancelled yet.

The question to ask of every column is therefore not “is this related to the answer?” Almost everything is. It is “will I have this in time?”
