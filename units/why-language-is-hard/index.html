The word that has not arrived yet

Long before any machine could hold a conversation, one could look at a photograph of an empty beach at dusk and tell you it was a beach at dusk. It could pick a single face out of a crowd of two hundred. It could find a tumour that a tired radiologist had walked past at four in the morning.

Show software from those same years a two-line hotel review, and it would report a happy guest, because the review contained the word lovely.

The review said: Lovely, if you enjoy being woken at four.

That photograph of the beach is several million numbers. The review is eight words. Whatever beat the machine here, it was not the size of the thing.

A photograph holds its shape

Nothing you have read so far stops working here. The training loop is the same loop, the dials are the same dials, and the wrongness score still comes out as one number. What changes is the material.

Pictures turn out to be unusually kind material, in three ways that are easy to miss because they never once cause trouble.

Every pixel is in one place, and being in that place is most of what it does. A pixel does not mean something different depending on which pixels came before it.

Pixels that sit next to each other almost always belong to the same thing — the same eyebrow, the same railing, the same patch of sky. So a machine that only ever looks at small neighbourhoods, and builds up from there, is not missing much.

And any photograph can be squashed to a standard size before the machine sees it. A phone snap and a poster both arrive as the same rectangle of numbers, so the machinery gets built once, for one shape.

Sentences give you none of that. They run from two words to two thousand, and whatever reads them has to cope with all of it. Words that belong together are routinely nowhere near each other. And a word’s position is not merely where it happens to be — it is part of what the word is saying.

Three things go wrong. The third is the one that stopped everybody.

Move the words and the fact moves

Ravi owes Priya.

Priya owes Ravi.

Three words each, and the same three words. One of those sentences is going to annoy somebody.

The simplest way to hand a sentence to a machine is to count which words are in it and throw the order away. That has a name — bag of words — because a bag is something you take items out of, not something you read a sequence from.

It is not a strawman. Bag-of-words systems scored reviews and sorted spam in production for years, and some are still running. They cannot tell those two sentences apart, because the two bags are identical.

That is not a subtle failure either. Word order is not decoration laid over the meaning; in English it is most of how a sentence says who did what to whom.

And here is the part that makes language different rather than merely harder. Cut a photograph into a thousand squares and shuffle them, and you get confetti — something no longer an image of anything, and obviously broken. Shuffle the words of a sentence and you can get another sentence. A perfectly grammatical one, about somebody else, which no amount of inspecting the pieces will reveal as the wrong answer.

The word that settles it is nowhere near

Don’t, if the parcel arrives while I’m out, leave it by the bins.

The instruction is don’t leave it by the bins. Those two words are seven words apart, and the first one reverses the second.

The obvious way to keep a machine’s job small is to let it read a fixed number of words at a time — a window, five or six words wide, sliding along the sentence. It is a reasonable idea. It is how a lot of early language software worked, and it fails here in a specific way: every window that contains leave it by the bins contains no don’t. The machine does not receive a difficult instruction. It receives the opposite instruction, and it receives it with total confidence.

You can widen the window, and people did. It costs — a machine looking at fifteen words at a time has far more to weigh up than one looking at five — and, worse, no width is ever safe. English puts no limit on how far apart two words that decide each other can sit. A not can be three sentences back. A name introduced in the first paragraph of an email can be what the they in the last paragraph is pointing at.

The word that settles it has not been read yet

Read this at normal speed:

She tipped the pan into the bowl until it was empty.

You know what was empty. You did not work it out, and it did not feel like work.

Now change the last word, and nothing else:

She tipped the pan into the bowl until it was full.

Now it is the bowl. Same eleven words in the same order except one, and the word that changed is the last word in the sentence — a couple of words after the word it rewrites.

Notice what a machine reading left to right has in front of it at the moment it arrives. It has She tipped the pan into the bowl until it, and so did the other version, because up to that point the two sentences are the same string of characters. Whatever it decides there, it decides on evidence that is identical in both cases and points at both answers equally.

This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to hear as “the machine needs to be cleverer”. It is not that. The information that settles the question is not present yet. No amount of skill extracts a fact from evidence that does not contain the fact.

Grammar cannot rescue it either. Any rule you could write — attach a pronoun to the nearest earlier noun, or to the subject, or to whichever one is more likely to be talked about — gives the same answer for both sentences, because grammatically they are the same sentence. The only thing that separates them is knowing that tipping empties one container and fills the other. That is a fact about the world, not a fact about English.

The name for this trick

A pair of sentences that differ by one word, where that word flips what a pronoun refers to, is called a Winograd schema, after the researcher who started collecting them in the 1970s. They were designed to be a test that grammar alone cannot pass — you can only answer one by knowing something about pans and bowls, or tow ropes, or why people win at chess.

For decades they were a quiet embarrassment: easy for any child, close to a coin toss for the best software available. They are still used as a test today, and modern systems get most of them right, which is the whole point of the Part you have just started.

It goes much further than pronouns. Bank means nothing on its own, and the word that decides whether it is a river or a building can arrive several words later. So can the word that tells you whether light is a weight or a lamp. A machine that must fix each word’s meaning as it reads, and move on, is wrong about all of these forever — not occasionally, but by construction.

That is what killed the obvious designs. They read left to right and commit as they go. What this problem demands instead is one of two awkward things: wait until the sentence is finished before deciding anything, or be able to go back and revise a decision already made. Both are demands about the shape of the machine, not about its accuracy, and neither is what anybody had built.

Then turn on Stop reading at the pronoun, and flip the ending again. Nothing on the line moves. Not one word, in any of the three pairs — because everything before the pronoun is identical in both versions, and the only difference lives in a word the reader has not reached.

That is the whole problem in one control. The machine is not looking in the wrong place. There is nothing in the right place to look at yet.

Why any of this matters to you

Because you use the fixed version daily, and it is invisible when it works.

You ask an assistant about a document, then type and what about the second one? — no nouns at all, and it answers correctly. A translation system has to pick a gendered word in French for an English sentence that never marked gender, and finds the clue several words away. A search box treats book a table and table a motion as different requests. Every one of those is this unit’s problem, handled well enough that nobody notices.

The rest of this Part is how that happened. Tokenization gets text into the machine at all, by cutting it into standard pieces. Attention is the idea that finally addresses the distance problem head-on, by letting any word consult any other word directly — including ones that come after it. The transformer is what you get when you build a machine out of nothing else.

For developers
String[] words = review.split(" ");
boolean happy = Arrays.asList(words).contains("lovely");

This is the hotel review at the top of the page, and it is not a strawman — real ones had a few hundred words in the list and a threshold, and nothing else. Its defining property is in the second line: shuffle words into any order at all and the answer does not change. That is not a bug you can find by testing, because it is exactly what the code was asked to do.

The third one: a word whose meaning is settled by a word that has not been read yet. At the moment the machine has to decide, the evidence in front of it is identical to the evidence it would have had if the answer were the other one. Accuracy is about doing better with what you have; here the thing that decides is not in what you have.

So the fix cannot be a better reader. It has to change the shape of the reading — either wait until the whole sentence has arrived before committing to anything, or keep the ability to go back and revise. That is a structural demand, and meeting it is what the rest of this Part is about.
